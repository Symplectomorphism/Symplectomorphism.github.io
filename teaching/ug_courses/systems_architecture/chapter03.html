Thinking about Complex Systems

Systems Architecture · Chapter 3

Aykut C. Satici

From Simple to Complex

  • Chapter 2 deliberately used simple systems — two or three entities — to focus on the ideas: form, function, entities, relationships, emergence
  • Most systems we build and work with professionally are complex
  • This chapter extends system thinking with tools for complexity:
  1. Decomposition and hierarchy
  2. Special logical relationships (class/instance, specialization, recursion)
  3. Techniques for reasoning through complex systems
  4. An introduction to SysML and OPM

Open by naming explicitly why Chapter 2 felt almost too easy — that was deliberate. Using systems with only two or three entities (the amplifier, Team X, the circulatory system, the solar system) let us focus purely on the core ideas — form, function, entities, relationships, emergence — without the added burden of scale. Nobody builds professionally with two-entity systems, though; the amplifier circuit, the design team, and the aircraft we’ll discuss all have dozens of entities in practice, so this chapter’s job is to scale everything from Chapter 2 up to genuinely complex systems.

Preview the four additions this chapter makes: decomposition and hierarchy, formalized as tools specifically for managing complexity (not just occasional structural detail); a small set of special logical relationships that recur constantly once you have many entities (class/instance, specialization/generalization, recursion); techniques for actually reasoning through a complex system without getting lost (top-down/bottom-up/middle-out, zigzagging); and finally, a first introduction to two industry-standard architecture representation tools, SysML and OPM. Flag for students up front, since it’ll come up later in the lecture: this course uses OPM as its primary representation tool going forward, so while we’ll mention SysML for context, we won’t go deep into its diagram types — the emphasis throughout the rest of the semester is on OPM.

Box 3.1 · Definition: Complex System

A complex system has many elements or entities that are highly interrelated, interconnected, or interwoven.

  • The fuzziness of the word “many” is deliberate
  • Complexity is driven into systems by asking more of them: more function, more performance, more robustness, more flexibility
  • It is also driven in by asking systems to work together and interconnect — your car with the traffic control system, your house with the internet

Give the formal definition and immediately flag the deliberate vagueness in it, since students will want to nail down an exact entity count and the book intentionally doesn’t provide one. “Many” is left fuzzy on purpose — there’s no bright line, like “50 entities or more,” that separates complex from non-complex. What matters is the qualitative property: entities that are highly interrelated, interconnected, or interwoven, not just numerous.

Explain the two distinct ways complexity gets driven into systems, since this gives students a diagnostic lens for their own future projects. First, by asking more of a single system — demanding more function, more performance, more robustness, more flexibility than a simpler version would need to provide. Second — and this is a distinct mechanism worth separating out — by asking systems to work together and interconnect with other systems that weren’t necessarily designed together: your car interacting with the surrounding traffic-control infrastructure, your house’s systems interconnecting with the broader internet. This second driver of complexity is increasingly dominant in modern systems and is worth dwelling on, since it means complexity isn’t only a property you can control by simplifying your own design — much of it arrives from the environment your system has to interoperate with.

Complex ≠ Complicated

Complex

An intrinsic property of the system: many interrelated, interconnected entities and relationships.

Complicated

A property of human perception: something overwhelms our ability to understand it. High apparent complexity.

Build systems of the necessary level of complexity that are not complicated.

This is our job as architects: to train our minds to understand complex systems so they do not appear complicated to us — or to anyone else who must work on the system.

This is the single most important distinction in the chapter, and it’s worth being very deliberate about the wording, since these two words are used interchangeably in everyday speech but mean genuinely different things here. Complex is an intrinsic, objective property of the system itself — how many entities it has, and how interrelated or interconnected they are — independent of who’s looking at it. Complicated is entirely different: it’s a property of human perception — of whether something overwhelms a particular person’s, or a particular team’s, ability to understand it. This is why the book calls it “apparent” complexity — apparent specifically to the human trying to comprehend it.

The crucial consequence, worth putting on the board or repeating verbatim, is the mission-statement fragment: build systems of the necessary level of complexity that are not complicated. This is not a contradiction — it says a system genuinely can (and often must) be complex, because the function and performance demanded of it require many interrelated entities, while still not being complicated, if the people who need to understand and work on it have trained themselves — or been given the right abstractions, decompositions, and representations — to comprehend it clearly. That’s explicitly framed as the architect’s job: not to avoid complexity (often impossible, given what’s being demanded of the system), but to manage it so it doesn’t become complicated — for you, and for everyone else who has to work on the system after you. Worth noting for historical grounding: complex systems aren’t new — large water-delivery networks existed by the time of classical Rome — but the ratio of complexity to our tools for managing it has shifted, and that’s exactly the gap this chapter, and really this whole course, exists to close.

Introducing Team XT

  • Team XT is an extended version of Team X from Chapter 2 — same role (producing a design), more entities
  • Walking through Task 1: the system is still the group of people and the process responsible for developing the design
  • Form = the people. Function = developing a design.
  • Determining each member’s function requires observation, experience, or interviews — the book tabulates all 18 people in Table 3.1, along with their formal structure (location, connectivity, reporting) and functional interactions

Introduce the chapter’s running example, and be explicit that this is a deliberate pedagogical device: Team XT is not a brand-new system, it’s Team X from Chapter 2, extended to have realistically many entities instead of just three, while keeping exactly the same role (producing a design). This lets students transfer everything they already know from Chapter 2 directly onto a system that’s now genuinely complex, rather than learning new vocabulary from scratch.

Walk through Task 1 exactly as in Chapter 2, to reinforce that the same four-task procedure applies unchanged: what’s the system? Still the group of people and the process responsible for developing the design. Form is the collective of people; function is developing a design. Determining each person’s individual function in a real organization, worth noting explicitly, isn’t something you can just read off an org chart — it typically requires direct observation, extensive experience with the team, or structured interviews with the individuals themselves, which is exactly the kind of legwork a real systems thinker or architect has to do.

Table 3.1, referenced here (and shown fully on the upcoming Figure 3.1 slide), tabulates all 18 members of Team XT along with three things for each: their function (what Task 1/2 asks for), their formal structure (their location — Cambridge or Moscow — their connectivity via shared design or requirement tools, and their reporting role), and their functional interactions (who they interact with, and how). This table is effectively “Team X’s Table 2.3, scaled up to 18 people” — the exact same kind of information, just at realistic scale. That scale is precisely what’s going to force us to introduce decomposition and hierarchy in the next few slides — nobody can hold 18 individually-tracked entities in mind at once, per the seven-plus-or-minus-two limit from Chapter 2.

The teaching point worth drawing out explicitly: even with this relationship diagram fully drawn out, it doesn’t yet reveal anything about hierarchy — who outranks whom. All 18 people and their interactions are shown here on essentially equal footing. That’s exactly the gap the next few slides (Hierarchy, then Figure 3.2) are about to fill — a genuinely separate kind of relationship that this functional-interaction view simply doesn’t capture on its own.

Decomposition: Divide and Conquer

  • Decomposition: dividing an entity into smaller pieces or constituents — one of the most powerful tools for dealing with complexity
  • “Divide and conquer”: break a problem down until each piece is tractable
  • The difficulty isn’t in the breaking apart — it’s in integration, bringing the decomposed entities back together to build the whole

“All Gaul is divided into three parts.”

— Julius Caesar, The Gallic Wars

In the formal domain, form aggregates — we worry about physical/logical fit. In the functional domain, we decompose functions into more basic functions — and recombining them is where we encounter emergence: the real challenge.

Introduce decomposition as one of the chapter’s two central tools for handling complexity (hierarchy is the other, on the next few slides). Define it precisely: dividing an entity into smaller pieces or constituents. “Divide and conquer” is the ancient strategic idea underlying it — break an intractable problem down until each individual piece becomes tractable — and the Julius Caesar quote is a nice bit of color showing this strategy is at least two thousand years old and well understood by the time of classical Rome, tying back to the historical point from a couple of slides ago about ancient water-delivery networks.

The genuinely important insight on this slide, worth emphasizing because it’s counter-intuitive to students who assume “decomposition is the hard part”: the difficulty is almost never in the breaking apart. Breaking a system down into pieces is usually mechanically straightforward. The real difficulty is in integration — bringing the decomposed entities back together to reconstruct the working whole. This foreshadows an enormous amount of later course content (and of real systems-engineering practice, where integration failures are notoriously common and expensive).

The fragment sharpens this further by splitting integration difficulty across the two domains we’ve used all course: in the formal domain, form genuinely does aggregate in a relatively benign, additive way — putting pieces back together is mostly a question of physical or logical fit (does this bolt hole line up, does this connector match). But in the functional domain, decomposing a function into more basic sub-functions and then recombining them is where we run headlong into emergence — the real challenge, and the reason integration is hard in general, not just an occasional engineering inconvenience.

Hierarchy

  • Hierarchy: a system in which entities belong to layers or grades, ranked one above the other — very common in social systems
  • What makes one grade rank above another?
  1. More scope — a governor outranks a mayor (a state is larger than a city)
  2. More importance or performance — a black belt outranks a brown belt
  3. More responsibility — a president outranks a vice president

Hierarchy is not always obvious from a functional-interaction diagram like Figure 3.1 — it took a separate view (Figure 3.2) to reveal that Sue outranks Amy, John, and Phil, who outrank everyone else.

Introduce hierarchy as the second core tool for managing complexity, and be precise about the definition: a system in which entities belong to layers or grades, one ranked above another — this is extremely common specifically in social/organizational systems, which is why Team XT is such a natural vehicle for teaching it. Give students the three distinct reasons the book identifies for why one grade outranks another, since these often get conflated in casual thinking about hierarchy: more scope (a governor outranks a mayor purely because a state is a larger jurisdiction than a city — nothing about the individual person, just the scope of what they oversee); more importance or performance (a black belt outranks a brown belt in martial arts because they perform to a demonstrably higher standard, with greater skill); and more responsibility (a company president outranks a vice president because more responsibility rests on that role). These are genuinely different justifications, and it’s worth asking students to identify which one applies in various examples they can think of themselves.

The fragment makes an important and slightly surprising methodological point: hierarchy is not always visible just from looking at a functional-interaction diagram, even a complete and carefully-drawn one like Figure 3.1 from a couple of slides ago. That diagram showed all 18 Team XT members and their interactions in what amounts to an egalitarian, flat layout — nothing in it visually signals that Sue, in fact, outranks Amy, John, and Phil, who in turn outrank everyone else on the team. Extracting that hierarchical information required building a genuinely separate view (Figure 3.2, coming up next), pulled specifically from the reporting-structure column of Table 3.1. This is a nice concrete illustration of “views and projections,” a concept the chapter formalizes properly much later in Section 3.6 — different views of the same system surface genuinely different information.

Figure 3.2 · Hierarchy in Team XT

Three flat layers — managers, group leaders, members — with no reporting structure implied within a layer. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 3.2.

Walk through this figure as the direct payoff of the previous slide’s claim. Extracted purely from the reporting-structure information buried in Table 3.1, this diagram sorts all 18 Team XT members into exactly three ranked layers: managers (just Sue) at the top, group leaders (Amy, John, and Phil) in the middle, and all remaining team members at the bottom.

Point out what this diagram deliberately does not claim, since it’s an easy misreading: within a given layer, there is no implied reporting structure at all — the diagram doesn’t say Heather reports to Karen just because they’re drawn in the same tier; it only says they’re at the same rank. Compare this explicitly back to Figure 3.1: that earlier diagram showed rich functional interaction detail but no hierarchy; this one shows clean hierarchy but strips out essentially all the interaction detail. Neither view alone is a complete picture — each one deliberately surfaces one kind of information while suppressing another, which is exactly the “abstraction” idea from Chapter 2 being put into practice at a genuinely complex scale.

Hierarchic Decomposition

  • Often, decomposition and hierarchy combine into a hierarchic decomposition: more than two levels, with reporting relationships between them
  • Cognitively more satisfying than an undifferentiated flat layer — it appeals to our desire to group things in sets of seven ± two
  • The “group” (Requirements / Concepts / Support) is a useful abstraction — but not the only way to decompose the team. We could have clustered by location, connectivity, or functional relationship instead.

Combine the chapter’s two central tools — decomposition and hierarchy — into a single, frequently-used compound structure: hierarchic decomposition, which is more than two levels deep, with explicit reporting relationships connecting adjacent levels. Point out that Figure 3.2 (the previous slide) was hierarchy but essentially flat — three ranked tiers with no further structure — whereas the upcoming Figure 3.3 will show genuine hierarchic decomposition, where the middle tier is organized into distinct groups that Sue’s direct reports each lead.

Explain why this compound structure feels more natural than an undifferentiated flat layer, tying directly back to Miller’s seven-plus-or-minus-two number from Chapter 2: a flat list of, say, 14 people reporting loosely to one manager strains our cognitive limits, but organizing them into three groups of about four or five, each with its own leader, keeps every individual grouping comfortably within that seven-plus-or-minus-two range at each level.

The last bullet is an important caution against over-literal thinking, worth stating clearly: the specific grouping used here — Requirements, Concepts, and Support — is a useful abstraction for this analysis, not the only correct way to decompose Team XT. We could just as validly have clustered these same 18 people by location (Cambridge vs. Moscow), by which shared software tool connects them, or by some other functional-relationship criterion, and gotten an equally legitimate, but different, hierarchic decomposition. This echoes the “abstractions aren’t unique” point from Chapter 2 — there’s no single canonical decomposition, only ones that are more or less useful for the question at hand.

Figure 3.3 · Hierarchic Decomposition of Team XT

Three group leaders reporting to Sue, about four people reporting to each leader. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 3.3.

This is the concrete payoff of the previous slide’s abstract discussion. Trace the structure out loud: Sue sits at the top; three group leaders — Amy (Requirements group), John (Concepts group), and Phil (Support group) — report directly to her; and roughly four team members report to each of those three leaders. Notice this gives us exactly the kind of hierarchic decomposition just described: multiple levels (three, counting Sue, the group leaders, and the members), each level comfortably within the seven-plus-or-minus-two range (Sue has three direct reports; each leader has about four).

This is a good moment to have students explicitly compare all three team diagrams side by side if time allows — Figure 3.1 (rich interaction detail, no hierarchy), Figure 3.2 (clean flat hierarchy, no internal grouping), and this one, Figure 3.3 (hierarchy plus grouping into named clusters). Each represents a legitimate, different projection of the exact same 18 people and their relationships — reinforcing, again, that no single diagram captures everything, and choosing which view to draw is itself a meaningful architectural decision.

Simple, Medium, and Complex Systems

  • Simple system: fully described by a one-level decomposition — generally no more than 7 ± 2 elements, which are essentially atomic (Figure 3.4)
  • Medium-complexity system: a two-level decomposition, up to about (7 ± 2)² ≈ 81 entities (Figure 3.3)
  • Complex system: same two-level diagram as medium-complexity — but at the second level, there are still further abstractions of things below

Figure 3.4: one-level decomposition of Team XT. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016).

Tip

One rarely sees a decomposition diagram more than two levels deep: a third level could hold up to 729 elements — far more than humans can routinely process.

This slide gives students a genuinely quantitative way to classify any system they encounter, which is worth working through numerically on the board. A simple system is one fully captured by a single-level decomposition — generally no more than about seven-plus-or-minus-two elements at that one level, and critically, those elements are essentially atomic (nothing meaningful lurks below them that we care about). Figure 3.4, shown here, is exactly this: Team XT collapsed down to just its top-level groups — Requirements, Concepts, Support, Marketing, Operations — a one-level decomposition with a handful of elements.

A medium-complexity system needs a genuine two-level decomposition, and the arithmetic matters here: if each level can hold up to about seven-plus-or-minus-two elements, then two levels combined can describe up to roughly (seven-plus-or-minus-two) squared, which works out to about 81 entities at the bottom level. Figure 3.3, from the previous slide — Sue, three group leaders, and roughly 12–15 team members below them — is exactly this kind of two-level decomposition.

The subtle and important distinction the slide draws next: a complex system, in this classification, actually has the exact same two-level decomposition diagram as a medium-complexity system — visually, Figure 3.3 could represent either one. What makes it complex rather than merely medium is that the elements shown at that second level are not truly atomic — they’re still abstractions hiding further structure underneath that we haven’t drawn but which is real and potentially important. Give the book’s own example to make this concrete: if Natasha (shown as an individual team member in Figure 3.3) were actually the leader of an entire sub-organization responsible for developing concepts, with her own reports below her that we haven’t shown, then this system is complex, not merely medium — the diagram looks identical, but there’s genuine hidden depth.

The closing fragment explains, with real numbers, why you essentially never see a three-level decomposition diagram drawn out in practice: a third full level could in principle hold up to (seven-plus-or-minus-two) cubed, or up to 729 elements — vastly more than any human or team can routinely process at once. This is exactly why the next slide has to introduce a different, less literal vocabulary (Level 0, 1, 2, and “atomic parts”) for talking about depth beyond what we can draw explicitly.

Level 0, 1, and 2 — and Atomic Parts

  • We refer to Level 0 (“the system”), Level 1 (down), Level 2 (down) — the designation of Level 0 is somewhat arbitrary and depends on the architect’s viewpoint
  • There is little consensus on names for the levels below “the system” (modules, assemblies, components, routines, sections…) — one person’s assembly is another’s component

Atomic part: from Greek átomos, “indivisible.”

  • Mechanical systems: “Call it a part when you can’t take it apart.”
  • Informational systems: “Call it a part if it loses meaning when you take it apart.”

Give students a standardized vocabulary for talking about decomposition depth, since — as the slide itself admits — the informal vocabulary in industry is a genuine mess. We’ll consistently use Level 0 for “the system” itself, Level 1 (down) for its immediate constituent entities, and Level 2 (down) for the entities within those. Be honest about the arbitrariness here, echoing a point from Chapter 2: which layer counts as Level 0 depends entirely on the architect’s chosen viewpoint — you could just as easily have called the circulatory system Level 0 and the whole human body Level 1 (up), or called the heart Level 0 and its chambers Level 1 (down). There’s no objectively correct starting point, only a useful one for the question at hand.

Acknowledge explicitly that industry terminology for the levels below “the system” — modules, assemblies, components, routines, sections, and so on — has essentially no consensus: one person’s “assembly” is routinely another person’s “component,” with no universal standard to appeal to. This is worth saying plainly so students don’t waste energy trying to memorize a “correct” mapping that doesn’t exist across organizations.

The fragment introduces “atomic part,” a term that does have a workable definition, even though it’s admittedly a little fuzzy. Give the etymology (Greek átomos, meaning indivisible) and then the two domain-specific rules of thumb: for mechanical systems, “call it a part when you can’t take it apart” — a bolt, a single screw, a processor chip (even though a chip has enormous internal detail, we usually treat it as atomic for higher-level system reasoning, per the discussion of abstraction from Chapter 2). For informational systems, the physical rule doesn’t apply, so instead: “call it a part if it loses meaning when you take it apart” — a word, an instruction, or a discrete unit of data, where breaking it down further destroys its semantic content. Both rules capture the same underlying idea — a natural stopping point for decomposition — just adapted to physical versus informational domains.

3.4 Special Logical Relationships

The Class/Instance Relationship

  • A class describes the general features of something; an instance is a specific occurrence of that class
  • A model of a car is a class; a specific Vehicle Identification Number (VIN) is an instance
  • A part number refers to a class; a serial number refers to an instance

Tip

Table 3.1 has two “Analyst” entities — we could instead define a class Analyst, with Analyst 1 and Analyst 2 as its instances. The 11 players on a soccer team and the 88 keys of a piano are instances of the classes “Player” and “Key.”

This section-header slide introduces the first of three “special logical relationships” that recur constantly once systems get complex — patterns that are worth naming explicitly because they show up again and again, in software, hardware, and organizations alike. The class/instance relationship: a class describes the general features shared by a category of things, and an instance is one specific occurrence of that class. Point out this term is borrowed more or less directly from software/object-oriented terminology, though the underlying idea long predates programming languages. Give the everyday examples first, since they’re the most intuitive: a car model (say, a particular make and model) is a class, and a specific Vehicle Identification Number identifies one specific instance of that class — an actual physical car sitting in a driveway somewhere. Similarly, a part number designates a class of interchangeable parts, while a serial number designates one specific, individual instance of that part.

The fragment connects this directly back to Team XT: Table 3.1 lists two separate entities simply labeled “Analyst 1” and “Analyst 2” — but we could reformulate this using class/instance language, defining a class called “Analyst” with Analyst 1 and Analyst 2 as its two instances. This is a useful way to manage complexity specifically when an entity type recurs multiple times in a system, and instances can be easily created or added — the 11 players on a soccer team are all instances of the class “Player”; the 88 keys on a piano are all instances of the class “Key.” Ask students to spot other examples from their own experience — most repeated, interchangeable components in any system they can name will fit this pattern.

The Specialization Relationship

  • Specialization/generalization describes the connection between a general object and a set of more specific objects
  • Shopping for a house: the general object is “House,” and specific objects are styles — colonial, Victorian, modern
  • In Team XT, we created three specializations of the generalization “Group”: a requirements group, a design (concepts) group, and a support group

Specialization is akin to inheritance in object-oriented programming: a class is created from a more general class, inheriting some attributes and functionality while adding its own.

Introduce the second special logical relationship, and be careful to distinguish it clearly from class/instance, since students frequently conflate the two — it’s worth asking directly: “what’s the difference between an instance of a class and a specialization of a generalization?” The key distinction: instances of a class are typically interchangeable, near-identical occurrences (any two “Player” instances are structurally the same kind of thing); specializations of a generalization are instead genuinely different variants that share a common general concept but diverge in their specific features. Specialization/generalization describes the connection between one general object and a set of these more specific variant objects.

Walk through the house-shopping example: “House” is the general object, and colonial, Victorian, and modern styles are specific objects — each one is genuinely a kind of house, with real differences in features, not just interchangeable copies of an identical thing. Then reconnect to Team XT directly: recall the “group” abstraction used to build the hierarchic decomposition a few slides back — we didn’t just have three generic, identical groups; we specialized the generalization “Group” into three genuinely different variants: a requirements-focused group, a design (concepts) group, and a support group, each with different internal composition and purpose.

The fragment draws the same object-oriented programming parallel as the previous slide, and it’s worth being precise about which OOP concept maps to which system-thinking concept: specialization corresponds to inheritance — a more specific class is created starting from a more general class, inheriting some of its attributes and functionality automatically, while adding its own additional, more specific attributes and functionality on top. This is a good moment, if students have programming background, to ask them to name a class hierarchy from their own coding experience and map it onto this generalization/specialization language.

Recursion

  • Recursion: when a process or object uses itself within the whole — entities or relationships applied in a self-similar way at multiple levels
  • Team XT: the whole team is a team of people; within it, three groups are each mini-teams — a mild form of recursion
  • Software engineering uses recursion explicitly and often: a class Node with an attribute Neighbors that is itself an array of Nodes

Introduce the third and last of the special logical relationships. Recursion occurs when a process or object uses itself within the whole — the same pattern of entities or relationships repeats, self-similarly, at multiple different levels of the system. Point out immediately that this is a genuinely different kind of relationship than the previous two: class/instance was about interchangeable copies, specialization/generalization was about variants of a shared concept, and recursion is specifically about a self-similar, repeating pattern across levels.

Use Team XT as a gentle, intuitive first example: the whole team is fundamentally a team of people working together — and if you zoom into any one of the three groups (Requirements, Concepts, Support), each of those is also, structurally, a small team of people working together. The same basic “team” pattern repeats one level down. Call this out as a genuinely mild form of recursion — it’s not perfectly self-similar (the sub-teams don’t have their own sub-sub-teams), but the pattern echoes.

The software example is the crisper, more literal illustration, and worth walking through carefully for students with programming background: a class called Node, used to build something like a graph or network data structure, that has an attribute called Neighbors which is itself simply an array of Node objects. The class is defined, quite literally, in terms of itself — a Node’s neighbors are more Nodes. This is explicit, deliberate recursion, used constantly in real software engineering (any graph, tree, or linked-list structure works this way), and it’s a good closing note for this section: recursion isn’t an exotic edge case, it’s one of the most common and useful patterns for managing complexity once you’re comfortable with the idea that structure can legitimately repeat itself at different scales within the same system.

3.5 Reasoning through Complex Systems

Top-Down / Bottom-Up / Middle-Out

Top-down

Start from the goals of a system, proceed to concept and high-level architecture, then increase detail until reaching the smallest entities of interest. Follows the “left-hand side” of the systems-engineering V model.

Bottom-up

Start from the artifacts, capabilities, or services available at the lowest-level entities, and build upward, predicting emergence.

Middle-out

Because truly complex systems have no real top or bottom, we always start somewhere in the hierarchy and reason a level or two up or down from there.

This section-header slide introduces the first of two general-purpose reasoning techniques for navigating complex systems — this one about direction. Top-down reasoning starts from the overall goals of a system, works through concept and high-level architecture, and then progressively increases detail level by level until reaching the smallest entities you actually care about. Point out for students who’ve taken any systems-engineering course that this corresponds directly to the “left-hand side” of the classic systems-engineering V-model — requirements flow down before implementation flows back up. Most of the development in this textbook, worth noting explicitly, follows this top-down approach.

Bottom-up reasoning goes the opposite direction: start from whatever artifacts, capabilities, or services are already available at the lowest level, and build upward from there, trying to predict what will emerge as those low-level pieces are combined. Give a memorable example: if the CEO of Team XT’s company simply gathered the 16 most appropriate employees in a room and let them self-organize into an effective design process, that would be bottom-up thinking in action — starting from the capable individuals available, and letting structure emerge upward, rather than architecting the team structure from the top down first.

Middle-out reasoning resolves an honest tension: because truly complex systems don’t really have a definitive “top” or “bottom” (recall from Chapter 2 that even “Level 0” is somewhat arbitrarily chosen, depending on the architect’s viewpoint), in practice we always start reasoning from some arbitrary point already embedded within the hierarchy, and work outward — a level or two up, a level or two down — from wherever that starting point happens to be. The book’s own honest conclusion, worth stating plainly: because there’s no true top or bottom, we’re always practically doing middle-out reasoning, even when we describe our approach as top-down or bottom-up — and good architects should be comfortable practicing all three modes as the situation demands, switching fluidly between them.

Zigzagging

  • Term coined by Nam Suh in his work on axiomatic design
  • When reasoning about a system, we alternate between the form domain and the function domain — start in one, work as long as practical, then switch
  • Team XT example: we started in the function domain (“design developing”), switched to form (small groups), then switched back to function (what each group does) — and this pattern continues down through the levels

Tip

Good architects practice top-down, bottom-up, and middle-out reasoning, zigzagging between form and function throughout.

Introduce the second general-purpose reasoning technique, this one specifically about domain-switching rather than direction. Give credit precisely: the term “zigzagging” was coined by Nam Suh in his work on axiomatic design (a design methodology worth a brief mention if students haven’t encountered it — it’s a formal framework for mapping customer needs to functional requirements to design parameters). Suh observed something students should recognize immediately from their own experience by this point in the course: when reasoning through a system, we naturally alternate between the form domain and the function domain, working in one for a while — as long as it’s practical — before switching to the other.

Walk through the Team XT example concretely as a worked demonstration, since it retraces exactly the same steps used to build up the earlier hierarchic-decomposition figures: we started in the function domain, identifying the system’s function as “developing a design.” Then we switched to the form domain, identifying the concept of small groups (Requirements, Concepts, Support) as the way to organize that form. Then we switched back to the function domain again, asking what function each of those groups actually performs. And this alternating pattern — function, then form, then function again — continues to repeat as you move down through successive levels of the system, exactly the same zigzag pattern recurring at every level.

The closing fragment ties this slide directly back to the previous one, and is worth stating as the section’s overall takeaway: good architects don’t pick one single reasoning mode and stick to it rigidly. They practice top-down, bottom-up, and middle-out reasoning as the situation demands, while simultaneously zigzagging between the form and function domains throughout. These two techniques — direction and domain-switching — operate independently and combine freely; neither one alone is sufficient for navigating a genuinely complex system.

3.6 Architecture Representation: OPM

Views and Projections

A description of a complex system’s architecture contains more information than any one person can readily comprehend.

Integrated model + projections: build one consistent model, then project it into whatever view is needed (floor plan, façade, section) — like modern 3D CAD. Views are guaranteed consistent because they come from the same model.

This course uses OPM (Object-Process Methodology), which follows the integrated-model approach.

This section-header slide sets up the chapter’s final major topic: how do we actually represent a complex system’s architecture, given that — as stated on the slide — a full description contains more information than any single person can readily comprehend at once? There are, at bottom, two genuinely different strategic approaches, and it’s worth being clear this is a real fork in methodology, not just a stylistic choice.

One option is to maintain multiple separate views, each one showing a different aspect of the system, without necessarily guaranteeing they’re all perfectly consistent with each other (this is the approach SysML and DoDAF take, mentioned briefly here and expanded slightly on the next slide). The other option — what this slide highlights, and what the callout describes — is to build a single integrated model containing everything, and then project whichever specific view is needed for a given purpose out of that one underlying model. The civil-architecture analogy is genuinely illuminating here: before 3D CAD tools existed, an architect drew separate views by hand (a floor plan, a façade, several sections) with no guarantee they’d actually describe a building whose parts all fit together consistently. Since 3D rendering became standard, the architect instead builds one 3D model, and the software projects whatever 2D view is needed — a floor plan, a façade, a section — and those projected views are automatically guaranteed to be mutually consistent, because they all derive from the exact same underlying model.

The fragment states plainly which approach this course commits to: OPM (Object-Process Methodology) follows the integrated-model approach, the modern-CAD-like strategy, rather than the multiple-separate-views strategy. This is worth flagging explicitly as a course-wide policy decision students should remember: we will use OPM as our primary representation tool for the remainder of the semester.

OPM

  • Object-Process Methodology — developed by Professor Dov Dori at Technion, unifying object- and process-oriented paradigms into a single model
  • Objects (boxes) and processes (ovals) appear together in one integrated diagram, connected by typed relationships — an object can be an instrument of a process, or can be changed by a process
  • No separate views: form and function, and every logical relationship, are all represented in a single model

Introduce OPM properly, since this is the tool students will actually be using for the rest of the course. Give the attribution precisely: Object-Process Methodology was developed by Professor Dov Dori at Technion, with the explicit goal of unifying two traditionally separate modeling paradigms — object-oriented and process-oriented — into a single, integrated model, rather than requiring separate diagrams for structure and behavior the way many other frameworks do.

Explain the core visual grammar directly: objects, representing form, are drawn as boxes; processes, representing function, are drawn as ovals — and critically, both appear together in one single integrated diagram, connected via typed relationships that specify exactly how an object relates to a process. Give the two relationship types named on the slide as concrete examples: an object can be an instrument of a process (meaning the process uses that object as a tool or resource to operate, without necessarily changing it), or an object can be changed by a process (meaning the process actually alters, creates, or consumes that object — this is our familiar “operand” concept from Chapter 2, now given a precise OPM relationship type).

The last bullet is the payoff worth emphasizing directly, tying back to the previous slide’s “integrated model” framing: there are no separate views in OPM. Form and function, and every logical relationship we’ve discussed this chapter (decomposition, class/instance, specialization/generalization), are all represented within a single, unified model — exactly the “one 3D model, project as needed” philosophy from the civil-architecture analogy, now applied concretely to system architecture.

Figure 3.6 · An OPM Diagram

Building a house: objects (Crane, Lumber, Foundations…) and processes (Foundation constructing, Wall constructing, Roofing) in one view. System Architect: Dov Dori, inventor of OPM. Source: Dori, D., gollner.ca (2008), reproduced in Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 3.6.

Walk through this diagram carefully, since it’s the first real example of OPM notation students will see, and it’s worth making sure the box/oval distinction and the relationship arrows are all clearly legible before moving on. The system here is building a house. Notice the objects (drawn as rectangular boxes): Crane, Raw material, Plan, Architect, Constructor, Lumber, Nails, Saw, Tile, and the emerging outputs — Foundations, Wall, Roof, and ultimately House. And notice the processes (drawn as ovals, inside the larger circle representing the overall “Building” process): Foundation constructing, Wall constructing, and Roofing.

Point out how the relationships work concretely: Lumber, Nails, and the Saw all feed into Wall constructing as instruments or consumed inputs; the Wall constructing process, in turn, produces the Wall object, which becomes an input to the overall House. Trace the whole sequence — foundations get constructed first (using the crane, raw material, and the architect’s plan and the constructor as instruments), producing the Foundations object; then wall construction uses lumber, nails, and a saw to produce the Wall; then roofing uses tile to produce the Roof; and Foundations, Wall, and Roof together aggregate up into the final House object at top right. This is a genuinely good single-slide illustration of nearly everything covered in this chapter and the last one operating together in one diagram: form (all the boxes), function (all the ovals), decomposition/aggregation (Foundations, Wall, and Roof combining into House), and functional relationships (instrument and consumption arrows) — all represented in exactly one integrated model, with no separate views required.

OPM Representations

OPM has its own visual conventions for the logical relationships from Section 3.4:

Relationship OPM Notation
Decomposition / aggregation Triangle-headed tree
Specialization / generalization Open-triangle tree
Class / instance Instance nested inside the class box

Tip

This course uses OPM as its architecture representation tool throughout.

Give students the specific visual grammar OPM uses for each of the three special logical relationships covered earlier in Section 3.4 — this is the practical reference table they’ll want to come back to whenever they’re actually drawing OPM diagrams for the rest of the course. Decomposition and aggregation are drawn using a solid, filled triangle-headed tree connecting the whole to its parts. Specialization and generalization use a visually similar tree structure, but with an open (unfilled) triangle head instead — a small but important visual distinction that keeps these two logically different relationships from being confused on a diagram. Class and instance relationships are shown differently altogether — not as a tree at all, but by literally nesting the instance’s box inside, or directly beneath, the class’s box, visually representing that the instance is a specific occurrence of that class.

The fragment restates the course policy plainly, since it’s worth repeating at every opportunity this early in the semester: OPM is the architecture representation tool used throughout this course, going forward. Every architecture diagram assigned in coursework or examined in later case studies will use these conventions, so it’s worth having students practice, even briefly, converting a small example (Team XT’s hierarchic decomposition would be a good choice) into proper OPM notation right now, while the grammar is fresh.
