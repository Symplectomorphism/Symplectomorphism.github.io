12.2 Applying Creativity to Concept

What Is Creativity?

  • Creativity must result in a novel output — on this, most agree. Less agreement: must it be intentional? Must it have influence or impact?
  • We take the position that creativity must be intentional — accidentally spilling paint on a canvas and discarding it isn’t creative — but creativity should not be defined by impact, since a focus on impact too early can restrict ideation

In an ideal creative process, the number of concepts under consideration should balloon — an idea first articulated by Alex Osborn, originator of “brainstorming”: “quantity breeds quality.”

  • The book stakes out a precise position on a contested term rather than leaving it vague. Everyone agrees creativity must be novel. Disagreement: must it be intentional? judged by impact?
  • Intentionality — book’s position: creativity must be intentional. Memorable counter-example: spilling paint accidentally, then discarding it — not creative, technically novel but no deliberate effort. Intent doesn’t require a known/linear process, just deliberately applied effort
  • Impact — more counter-intuitive, explain why: creativity should not be defined by eventual influence/impact — judging by impact too early is premature filtering that kills ideation. Suppresses raw, half-formed ideas that might mature later. Impact assessment is the job of metrics applied afterward (Pugh matrices, later this chapter), not an ideation-stage gate
  • Alex Osborn (coined “brainstorming”): “quantity breeds quality” — number of concepts under consideration should balloon before narrowing
  • → This ballooning-then-narrowing shape is the next slide’s funnel diagram, and recurs as the organizing image for the whole chapter (quantified with real numbers in Figure 12.10)

Unstructured vs. Structured Creativity

Unstructured Creativity

The more prevalent approach: brainstorming, blue-sky ideas, free association. Focuses on ideating without prejudices from previous experience — forming new pathways through the concept-space (de Bono).

Structured Creativity

Holds that problem analysis can help solution synthesis. Creative thinking is not fundamentally different from ordinary problem solving — creativity can be stimulated through analysis.

Tip

Both are necessary. Our view of system architecture more closely reflects structured creativity — we’d rather succeed with an architecture that’s chosen, not one arrived at by luck.

  • Two broad schools of thought on ideation — rest of the chapter’s tools sort cleanly into one or the other
  • Unstructured creativity: brainstorming, blue-sky, free association — rooted in unconscious processing, an “Aha!” that seems to arise from nowhere. Edward de Bono: mind is physically constrained by existing thought-channels; unstructured techniques force new pathways (e.g., open a book to a random page, point blindly, force a connection to the problem)
    • Be honest with students: some hold creativity is by definition unconscious, must occur without warning — if plannable, not really creative. Book calls this feasible but “somewhat counterproductive”
  • Structured creativity: analyzing the problem helps synthesize the solution — creative thinking isn’t fundamentally different from ordinary problem-solving, can be stimulated by structured analysis
    • Famous “creative thinkers” (Einstein, Picasso, da Vinci, Maya Angelou) show strong divergent thinking — counterpoint: this capacity can be built through structure, not just innate/unconscious
  • Book’s considered position — repeat to students, explains the shape of the rest of the chapter: both necessary, but leans structured — would rather succeed with an architecture chosen through traceable reasoning than arrived at by luck
  • → Same sentiment as Ch. 1’s “structured creativity is better than unstructured,” now fully grounded methodologically

Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 12.3.

  • First concrete structured-creativity technique: component recombination
  • Diagram’s example: transporting → three internal functions (lifting, propelling, guiding), each with more than one form-choice (wheels, propeller, etc.) per row
  • Synthesize a concept by selecting one choice per row; different row-selections → genuinely different concepts

Completeness Frameworks and TRIZ

  • Completeness frameworks use lists to stimulate ideation — e.g., cataloging forms of energy (linear kinetic, rotational kinetic, potential, chemical…) and asking what a concept using each would look like
  • De Bono’s Six Hats is a team-based completeness framework: six roles (Managing, Information, Emotions, Discernment, Optimistic Response, Creativity) that force a more holistic evaluation of a problem
  • TRIZ (Theory of Inventive Problem Solving), developed by Genrich Altshuller from 40,000 patent abstracts, resolves apparent contradictions (“a faster train needs a more powerful engine, but a more powerful engine is heavier”) using 40 inventive principles — e.g., Mechanics Substitution: replace a mechanical means with an optical, acoustic, magnetic, or electromagnetic one
  • Second family: completeness frameworks — pre-built lists systematically stimulate ideation, rather than recombining decomposed pieces
  • Energy-forms example: checklist of energy forms (linear kinetic, rotational kinetic, potential, chemical…) — for each, ask “what would a concept using this look like?” Forces coverage a free-associative brainstorm might miss
    • Concrete: hybrids mostly store energy chemically (batteries), but rotational kinetic exists too — flywheels in Porsche’s GT3RS hybrid and the 2009 Williams F1 car
  • De Bono’s Six Hats: team-based version — six roles (Managing, Information, Emotions, Discernment, Optimistic Response, Creativity), cycling the team through different reasoning modes for a more holistic evaluation
    • Be honest: not proven orthogonal cognitively, but still a useful, practical decomposition of team roles
  • TRIZ (Theory of Inventive Problem Solving) — most time of the three, real engineering pedigree
    • Genrich Altshuller, Soviet Navy, reviewed ~40,000 patent abstracts → 40 general principles of invention
    • Starts by naming apparent contradictions: “a faster train needs a more powerful engine, but a more powerful engine is heavier”
    • Example principle — Mechanics Substitution: replace a mechanical means with a sensory one (optical, acoustic, magnetic, electromagnetic) — e.g., diesel locomotive → electric locomotive or linear induction motor
    • → Caveat: principles don’t guarantee the same weight or any specific property — they give a direction/concept to explore, not a guaranteed optimized outcome

Identifying Concept

  • Recall from Chapter 7: concept is a vision, idea, notion, or mental image that maps function to form — it embodies a principle of function and operation, and includes an abstraction of form
  • Creating the concept is a moment of peak creativity — the selection of concept has a deep, far-reaching impact, and establishes the solution-specific vocabulary
  • “The concept rationalizes the structure of the architecture.” — Steve Imrich. Concept is not a product attribute; it is a mapping from one attribute (function) to another (form) — separate from, and prior to, the architecture itself
  • Callback-and-consolidate before the four-step framework
  • Restate Ch. 7’s definition precisely — everything downstream depends on it: concept = vision/idea/notion/mental image mapping function to form, embodies a principle of function and operation, includes an abstraction of form (not full detail)
  • Why “peak creativity”: once selected, a concept’s impact is deep and far-reaching — establishes the entire solution-specific vocabulary every downstream team uses. Same “decisions cascade” idea as Ch. 1’s engine-mounting example, now at concept selection
  • Steve Imrich (civil architect — note the domain-crossing citation): “the concept rationalizes the structure of the architecture.” Rationalizes = gives the architecture its underlying logic, the reason it’s organized this way and not another
  • → Precise, easy to get backwards: concept is not a product attribute — it’s a mapping from one attribute (function) to another (form). Separate from, and logically prior to, the architecture itself

A Four-Step Concept Framework

1. Develop the Concepts — start with the SPS and goals; identify solution-neutral operands/processes; apply creativity to specialize a specific operand/process/instrument; check against goals.

2. Expand and Develop Concept Fragments — decompose rich, multifunctional concepts to reveal internal functions; repeat Step 1 for each fragment.

3. Evolve and Refine Integrated Concepts — search systematically through fragments; combine combinatorially, with constraints.

4. Select a Few Integrated Concepts — apply backward (fit to goals) and forward (potential for good architecture) considerations.

  • Chapter’s central organizing structure — rest of the chapter (through 12.6) is a detailed worked example of these four steps on the Hybrid Car. Introduce all four as a roadmap first
  • Step 1 — Develop the Concepts: start from SPS + Ch. 11 goals; identify solution-neutral operands/processes; apply creativity to specialize a specific operand/process/instrument; check against goals, reformulate if it fails
  • Step 2 — Expand and Develop Concept Fragments: for rich/multifunctional concepts, decompose to reveal internal functions; repeat Step 1 for each fragment
  • Step 3 — Evolve and Refine Integrated Concepts: search systematically for coverage, combine fragments combinatorially (subject to constraints) into integrated concepts
  • Step 4 — Select a Few Integrated Concepts: backward consideration (fit to goals) + forward consideration (potential for good architecture); re-check against SPS/goals
  • → Steps 1-2 = “ballooning” half of Figure 12.2’s diamond; Steps 3-4 = “narrowing” half — this framework operationalizes the diamond into four repeatable steps

Box 12.1 · Principle of Creativity

“Imagination is more important than knowledge.” — Albert Einstein

“Creativity is bred by creating a gap between current reality and the vision for the system.” — Peter Senge

  • For any interesting, real problem, there will be essential tensions among the goals — creativity in architecture is the process of resolving these tensions
  • To maximize the possibility of creativity: treat all goals as tradable, remove organizational/cultural barriers, and consider the entire space of possibility
  • “Creativity is like driving a car at night. You never see further than your headlights, but you can make the whole trip that way.” — E.L. Doctorow
  • Reframes what “creativity in architecture” actually means
  • Central claim: any genuinely interesting problem has essential tensions among goals (fuel efficiency vs. power, cost vs. capability — familiar from Hybrid Car goal-setting in Ch. 11). Creativity = the process of resolving these tensions, not generating ideas in a vacuum
  • Three actionable practices to maximize creativity: treat all goals as tradable; remove organizational/cultural barriers suppressing ideas; deliberately consider the entire space of possibility, not an early comfortable subset
  • Einstein’s quote: imagination exceeding known facts
  • → Doctorow’s headlights quote — a genuinely useful mental model: you never see further than your headlights, but you can make the whole trip that way. Comforting reframe: you don’t need to see the whole solution before generating concepts — just as far as your current headlights

Section 12.2 Summary

  • Creativity must be intentional, but should not be judged by impact during ideation — that comes later, from metrics
  • Unstructured creativity (brainstorming) and structured creativity (component recombination, completeness frameworks, TRIZ) are complementary, not competing, approaches
  • Concept is a mapping from function to form, developed through a four-step framework: develop, expand into fragments, evolve/refine integrated concepts, and select a few for further development
  • Checkpoint before shifting from theory (12.2) to the fully worked Hybrid Car example (12.3-12.6)
  • Students should be able to state: creativity must be intentional but not judged by impact during ideation (deferred to metrics afterward); unstructured and structured creativity are complementary, not rivals, though this course leans structured; concept = mapping from function to form via the four-step framework (develop, expand into fragments, evolve/refine integrated concepts, select a few)

12.3–12.5 Developing the Hybrid Car Concept

Step 1: Develop the Concept

Recall the Hybrid Car’s system problem statement from Chapter 11:

Provide our customers a product to transport them and their possessions inexpensively and in an environmentally sound manner, by allowing them to drive themselves, their passengers, and light cargo fuel-efficiently and with good handling characteristics, using a hybrid gas/electric car.

  • The word “hybrid” is itself a short form — it suppresses which two energy poles are combined. Historically, there was even a time when “hybrid” commonly meant hybrid steam/gas, not gas/electric
  • Begin the fully worked example by re-grounding: this SPS is the same To-By-Using statement from Ch. 11’s stakeholder/goal analysis, carried forward unchanged
  • Read aloud slowly: To (transport inexpensively, environmentally sound) = value delivered; By (drive themselves/passengers/light cargo, fuel-efficiently, good handling) = process; Using (hybrid gas/electric car) = instrument/form, already partially specialized
  • → Historical seed for the closing case study: “hybrid” suppresses which two energy poles combine. Today assumes gas+electric, but historically “hybrid” often meant steam/gas — pays off in the case study on electric/steam/ICE architectural competition

Step 2: Expand the Concept — Propulsion

  • “Driving” is a concept rich in meaning — we decompose it into fragments, beginning with propulsion
  • Three overarching vehicle classifications, based on how many external energy sources the propulsion system depends on:

Monovalent

One external energy source (most cars today, including simple hybrids with one fuel source)

Bivalent

Two external energy sources (e.g., a plug-in hybrid: electricity and fuel)

Multivalent

Three or more (e.g., the Fiat Siena Tetrafuel: gasoline, ethanol blends, or CNG)

  • Step 2 applied for the first time: “driving” = Step 1’s specific process, flagged “rich in meaning” (bundles several internal functions) — the trigger condition for Step 2. First fragment: propulsion
  • Three-way propulsion classification, based on number of external energy sources:
    • Monovalent: one external source — most cars today, including simple hybrids (still only gasoline externally, battery is an internal secondary store)
    • Bivalent: two external sources — plug-in hybrid (electricity from grid + gasoline)
    • Multivalent: three or more — Fiat Siena Tetrafuel (gasoline, E20-E25 ethanol blends, pure ethanol E100, or CNG)
  • → This classification matters directly later, when asking which category the Hybrid Car concept should specialize into

  • ICE: dominant today
  • Battery-based: a spectrum — HEV (small assist) → PHEV (plug-in) → BEV (no combustion engine at all)
  • Fuel-cell: converts fuel chemically into electricity for electric motors, rather than burning directly
  • → This is the “energy carrying/storage” fragment of “driving,” expanded per Step 2 — a separate fragment from propulsion-architecture (monovalent/bivalent/multivalent) on the previous slide, both expanded independently

Seven Additional Concept Fragments

Beyond propulsion, seven internal functions capture the value added by hybrid systems:

  1. Motor start-stop — shut off the engine at rest, restart on demand
  2. Regenerative braking — capture braking energy that would otherwise be lost to friction/heat
  3. Power boost — electric motor adds torque beyond what the engine alone delivers
  4. Load level increase — engine drives a generator to recharge the battery
  1. Electric driving — propel using only stored electric energy, engine decoupled
  2. External battery charging — plug into the grid (differentiates PHEVs from other HEVs)
  3. Gliding — decouple both engine and electric system, coast on gravity alone
  • Catalogs the remaining seven fragments of “driving” capturing hybrid-added value beyond conventional ICE — these get directly reused later to build integrated vehicle concepts
  • Motor start-stop: engine shuts off at a full stop (traffic light), restarted via motor/starter-generator when power needed — in micro-hybrids without electric driving, restart in well under a second, triggered by brake release/clutch depression
  • Regenerative braking: braking energy normally lost to friction/heat captured by running the traction motor generatively, stored in the high-voltage battery
  • Power boost: electric motor adds torque beyond what the engine alone delivers (inclines, towing) — especially effective from rest, since electric motors peak at zero RPM, exactly where combustion engines are weakest (Figure 12.7)
  • Load level increase: engine runs a generator to recharge the battery, shifting to a more efficient operating point when excess power capacity is available
  • Electric driving: stored electric energy alone powers the traction motor, engine fully decoupled (off, or generating instead of propelling) — limited by battery storage capacity
  • External battery charging: differentiates plug-in hybrids from every other type — charging unit replenishes from the grid, not just onboard sources; opens possibilities for nighttime charging and vehicle-to-grid power flow
  • Gliding: decouple both engine and electric system, coast on gravity alone, avoid powertrain friction losses — conventional cars do this via neutral downhill; hybrids need rapid reconnection when conditions change

Figure 12.6 · Fuel Savings from Start-Stop and Braking

Idling reduction via motor start-stop saves ~5–7%; an optimized hybrid control strategy (combining load level increase, boosting, electric driving, gliding) saves a further ~5–9%; regenerative braking saves another ~5–9% — totaling ~15–25%. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 12.6, after Ehsani, Emadi & Gao (2009).

  • Numbers quantify what the fragments are worth — makes the abstract discussion concrete
  • Baseline: conventional ICE. Motor start-stop alone (eliminate idling): saves ~5-7% city driving
    • optimized control strategy (load level increase, boosting, electric driving, gliding combined): further ~5-9%
    • regenerative braking: further ~5-9%
  • → Combined: ~15-25% savings vs. conventional baseline — real, quantified backward-considerations for comparing candidates against Ch. 11’s fuel-efficiency goal
  • Subtlety: external battery charging is absent from this figure — it adds energy from outside the vehicle, not a cleverer use of onboard energy. These percentages represent full hybrids with limited (non-plug-in) electric driving, not plug-in hybrids (which replace a much larger fuel share by design)

Figure 12.7 · Why Boosting Helps at Low Speed

The electric motor delivers its highest torque starting from rest and low RPM — exactly where a combustion engine is weakest. Boosting lets the electric motor supply torque during the acceleration phase the engine handles worst. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 12.7.

  • The physical reason power-boost works, not just an assertion
  • Electric motor’s torque curve: flat and high from zero RPM, tapers at higher speeds
  • Otto-cycle combustion engine’s curve: opposite — low at rest/low RPM, peaks at higher RPM (~2000-2500 RPM)
  • → Complementary shapes are the whole point: exactly where combustion is weakest (from rest, low RPM) is exactly where electric is strongest — boosting especially improves 0-100 km/h acceleration by using electric torque from rest, then letting combustion take over its favorable range
  • Nice concrete example of “emergence depends on structure” (Ch. 2) applied to powertrains — neither curve alone is as good as the combination, because weaknesses/strengths are complementary, not overlapping

Step 3: Evolve and Refine Integrated Concepts

  • The third step searches the space of concept fragments systematically, organized by the possible mappings between functions and forms
  • Key question: is the energy storage function an input to the vehicle-moving function (a shared form — parallel hybrid), or do they use separate forms (series hybrid)?

Tip

Vehicles that mix both modes are combined hybrid systems — they may split fuel-converter energy across series and parallel paths simultaneously (power-split hybrids), or switch between the two.

  • Step 3: evolve and refine integrated concepts, searching systematically through Step 2’s fragments
  • Book candidly excerpts a full combinatorial search for brevity — exhaustive computational methods deferred to Part 4, later in the course. Here: organize analytically around Figure 12.5’s two primary internal functions — energy storage and vehicle moving
  • Key architectural question: is energy-storage an input to vehicle-moving (shared form, additive — parallel hybrid), or do they use genuinely separate forms (series hybrid — fuel system works sequentially, only recharges the electric system, which alone drives the load)?
  • → Third category: combined hybrid — mixes both, either splitting fuel-converter energy across series+parallel simultaneously (power-split hybrids) or switching between only-parallel/only-series modes. This taxonomy (parallel/series/combined) is exactly what the next figure visualizes

Figure 12.8 · Parallel vs. Series Hybrid Drive

Parallel hybrid: both fuel and electric converters feed the summing energy load additively. Series hybrid: the fuel-based system runs sequentially — it only recharges the electric system, which alone drives the load. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 12.8.

  • Makes the parallel-vs-series distinction fully concrete and visual
  • Parallel (top): fuel source → fuel converter; electric source → electric converter; both feed directly into the same summing junction (Σ) → energy load. Additive and simultaneous
  • Series (bottom, dashed lines = recharging flow): fuel converter output does not go directly to the load — recharges the electric converter/battery instead (power system 2, bidirectional); only the electric converter drives the load. Fuel system is entirely sequential/indirect
  • → Have students trace both paths visually — solid arrows = propulsion power flow, dashed = recharging flow. This additive/simultaneous vs. sequential/indirect distinction is the single most important engineering fact underlying the whole hybrid taxonomy that follows

Figure 12.9 · The HEV Conceptual Solution Space

Two dimensions organize the space: electric range and degree of electrification (ratio of peak electric motor power to maximum combined power). Seven integrated concepts populate this space — from conventional ICE (0,0) to battery EV (degree = 1). Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 12.9, after Gorbea (2011).

  • Payoff of Step 3 fully realized: two-dimensional solution space combining energy-storage/vehicle-moving fragments (parallel/series/combined) with the seven additional fragments (start-stop, regenerative braking, etc.)
  • Horizontal axis — degree of electrification: ratio of cumulative peak electric-motor power to max combined power. 0 = purely conventional ICE, 1 = purely battery-electric
  • Vertical axis — electric range: how far on electrical propulsion alone
  • → Regions on the map (detailed next slide): conventional ICE/micro hybrid at the origin; mild hybrid further along electrification, still minimal range; full hybrid moderate electrification, short range; PHEV higher electrification + materially larger range, external charging; series-PHEV (range extender) very high electrification + substantial range, small backup engine; BEV and FCEV both at degree = 1, differing in how electricity is produced (stored battery vs. fuel cell converting on the fly)

  • Seven integrated concepts populating the solution space, precise numbers attached — these feed into the Pugh matrix a few slides ahead
  • Conventional ICE: current dominant architecture (diesel/Otto-cycle), electrification and electric range both exactly zero
  • Micro Hybrid: propulsion exclusively via ICE + motor start-stop, needs a more robust 12-16V battery and starter-generator — “advanced ICE cars,” minimal electrification, no electric-driving capability
  • Mild Hybrid: some limited electric-driving functionality (though some still don’t offer electric-only driving) — high-voltage battery, electric starter-motor for assist, coordinating control system; solely parallel, motor-assist + expanded regenerative braking
  • Full Hybrid: 10-30% electrification, short electric-only range (~500m-3km) — ICE remains primary, electric system materially assists; parallel or combined configs, full regenerative braking
  • Plug-In Hybrid (PHEV): differentiated by external charging — spans parallel/series/combined, >35% electrification, 5-160 km electric range; series-PHEV range-extender engine can be substantial or a small “limp home” engine
  • Battery EV (BEV): outside the HEV solution space, electrification = 1, no ICE at all, plug-in by definition, range depends entirely on battery capacity
  • Fuel Cell EV (FCEV): electrochemical cell converts fuel to current directly; primarily series architecture — fuel-cell system feeds the electrical system, which alone propels
  • → These seven were generated by combining the two primary internal functions’ form-to-function mappings with the seven additional fragments, combinatorially — real coverage of the concept space for Step 4 next

Step 4: Select Concepts with a Pugh Matrix

A Pugh matrix rates each candidate concept against a set of criteria — here, the goals from Chapter 11 — on a five-level scale from very advantageous (++) to many disadvantages (−−), relative to a chosen reference concept.

  • Two screening criteria: a backward-looking comparison against the prioritized goals, and a forward-looking judgment of the concept’s potential for a good, elegant architecture
  • Not every goal differentiates — a goal met equally by every concept (like “must accommodate a driver”) doesn’t help choose among them; it belongs to detailed design more than to the concept decision
  • Step 4: select a few integrated concepts from the seven generated in Step 3
  • Why down-selection is necessary: a decision with only one option isn’t a decision. But the architect’s role is to preserve options as long as reasonably possible, resisting pressure to down-select too early before concepts are detailed enough to fully judge fit against goals
  • Human-judgment subtlety (citing Bazerman): systematic biases toward concepts easier to recall, more information/fidelity, confirming existing beliefs, or already familiar/anchored — none about which concept is actually best. Exactly why a structured tool like Pugh matters: forces criterion-by-criterion comparison, not gut call
  • Pugh matrix defined: rate each candidate against criteria on a five-level scale (++ to −−), always relative to a chosen reference concept (full HEV serves as reference here, rated “o” against itself by construction)
  • Two screening considerations: backward (fit against Ch. 11’s prioritized goals — critically/importantly/desirably tiers) and forward (potential for good, elegant architecture)
  • → Important filter: not every goal differentiates. “Must accommodate a driver” is met equally by all seven — constrains detailed design later, doesn’t help choose among these concepts now, so set it aside from this comparison

  • Transport range tells the opposite story: ICE/micro/mild score well (not battery-limited), FCEV and especially BEV score poorly (genuine range limitations)
  • Investment/volume, handling, cost: same pattern — ICE/micro/mild score well, PHEV/FCEV/BEV score poorly, reflecting added weight/complexity/manufacturing risk of heavier electrification

Case Study: Architectural Competition in the Automotive Industry

Early Architectural Competition

Architectural competition refers to differentiating a product from others in the market based on product architecture, rather than incremental improvement within a single dominant one.

  • In the automotive industry’s early years, three fundamentally different concepts — electric, steam, and internal combustion — competed to dominate the market
  • Electric cars were marketed to female drivers for ease of use and minimal maintenance; ICE cars targeted male drivers seeking power and speed; steam cars promised long range on a single “filling of the tanks”
  • Introduce the closing case study — credit Dr. Carlos Gorbea’s contribution (book acknowledges his dissertation on vehicle architecture and lifecycle cost analysis) — model good academic citation practice
  • Architectural competition, defined: firms differentiating products by fundamentally different architecture, not incremental improvement within one dominant architecture — contrasted with the later “architectural dominance” period (subsystem-level competition)
  • Historical stage: automotive industry’s early years — electric, steam, and internal combustion genuinely competed for dominance, each linking powertrain elements differently to achieve the same core function
  • → Marketing segmentation (memorable, concrete): electric marketed to female drivers (ease of use, minimal maintenance); ICE targeted male drivers (power, speed); steam promised long range on one “filling of the tanks” (note: plural — both fuel and water tank, relevant again shortly)

The Baker Electric Runabout marketed as “The Aristocrats of Motordom” — evidence of genuine architectural competition in the early automotive market, each architecture appealing to a different customer segment. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 12.11. Source: Baker Electrics.

  • Tagline: “The Aristocrats of Motordom” — pitch: constant speed/mileage increases, reliability no longer limited by charging-station availability, “the ideal car for town and suburban service” — refined, low-maintenance, genteel customer base
  • → Contrast: a 1904 steam-car ad (referenced in the same passage) pitched in technical language — 100 miles of range on one filling of “the tanks” (plural: fuel + water). Same aggregate function (driving), genuinely different architectures, visibly different customer sensibilities — clean illustration of real architectural competition in an actual marketplace

What Triggered ICE Dominance?

  • Early steam cars had rapid acceleration but needed frequent water refills and 20 minutes to build boiler pressure; early electrics were simple but limited to ~64 km range and 32 km/h; ICE cars matched electrics in performance but were harder and riskier to start (hand crank)
  • Two breakthroughs resolved steam’s core weakness (water dependency): the internal combustion engine and the electric motor — both first proven in rail and power generation before entering automobiles
  • Ford’s assembly line (lower price) and the electric starter (removed the crank-injury risk) made ICE cars affordable and safe for the masses by 1920 — steam disappeared from the market entirely by 1930
  • Three-way performance comparison — explains why the outcome wasn’t obvious in advance
  • Steam: best raw acceleration (0-65 km/h in under 10s, peak torque from a standing start), but frequent water refills (~2 miles between fills on early models) and up to 20 minutes to build boiler pressure
  • Electric: comparable price to steam, needed a power source mostly available in major cities; range <64 km, top speed ~32 km/h (low energy density: ~15-20 Wh/kg lead-acid vs. gasoline’s ~12,200 Wh/kg) — but simple to operate, no complicated shifting, no maintenance/emissions
  • ICE: performance on par with electric, inferior to steam — steam proponents called it the “internal explosion engine” to imply danger, with real basis: startable in under a couple minutes, but many motorists were genuinely injured hand-cranking it
  • Two breakthroughs resolved steam’s core weakness (water dependency): internal combustion engine and electric motor — both first proven in rail and stationary power generation before entering automobiles
  • → What actually tipped it to ICE: Ford’s assembly line (lower price) + electric starter (removed hand-crank injury risk) → affordable and safe by ~1920. Steam, despite a lineage back to the 1780s, disappeared from the market entirely by 1930

  • Dominant-architecture era: ICE design principles relatively unchanged since the mid-1930s.     • Triggering events annotated: Ford’s assembly line (price); steam’s failed 1920s-1930 last stand (water-recycling condenser, faster-pressure spark-ignition starter — made it heavier/more expensive, gone by 1930); rising regulatory requirements (safety, emissions); GM’s EV-1 as an early, ultimately unsuccessful disruption attempt
  • → Renewed architectural competition (~1998-2008): reintroduction of EVs + Toyota Prius (first mass-produced HEV in the U.S.) — book frames this as a return to genuine architectural, not merely incremental, competition, for the first time in nearly a century

The Lesson of Dominant Architecture

  • Once the market adopted ICE as the single dominant architecture, the risk of not knowing which architecture would prevail was eliminated — manufacturers could focus innovation at the subsystem level, not the architecture level
  • Decades of incremental innovation followed — most automakers built core competencies deeply specific to ICE design, leaving them poorly positioned to pivot when architecture became contested again

A shift back toward architectural competition can place established firms in real jeopardy — this was exactly the case for most steam manufacturers in the 1920s, who failed to adapt. The electric car was a loser in 1910. Could it become a winner again in a new period of architectural competition?

  • The case study’s payoff slide — strategic business lesson
  • Once the market settled on ICE, the risk of not knowing the winning architecture was eliminated for everyone simultaneously — industry redirected innovation to the subsystem level rather than the riskier question of overall architecture
  • The real strategic warning: decades of incremental innovation meant automakers built core competencies — expertise, supplier relationships, manufacturing investment — deeply around ICE design, leaving them structurally poorly positioned to pivot when architectural competition returned
  • → Read close to verbatim: a shift back toward architectural competition can place even established firms in real jeopardy — exactly what happened to steam manufacturers in the 1920s.
