The Three Roles of the Architect

Reduce ambiguity

Define the boundaries, goals, and functions of the system.

Employ creativity

Create the concept.

Manage complexity

Choose a decomposition of the system.

These three roles all center on information: reducing ambiguity by identifying necessary, consistent, important information; adding new information through creativity; and managing the explosion of information in the final architecture.

  • Unpack the three roles concretely — previous slide only named them
  • Reduce ambiguity = defining boundaries/goals/functions (Ch. 2’s Task 1/Task 2, now for a system that doesn’t exist yet)
  • Employ creativity = creating the concept (Ch. 7’s function→form mapping, not yet determined)
  • Manage complexity = choosing a decomposition (central topic of Ch. 13, echoes Ch. 3’s decomposition/hierarchy tools)
  • → Unifying lens: all three are modes of working with information — reduce ambiguity = identify needed info; creativity = add new info; manage complexity = handle the explosion of info once concept is chosen (own slide shortly)

9.2 Ambiguity and the Role of the Architect

Before the Architect Is Engaged

  • There has been an “upstream process” — full of ambiguity, issues, opportunities, and needs for the new system
  • What are the high-priority needs vs. the “nice to haves”? What regulations apply? How is manufacturing competence aligned with the envisioned product?

The architect drives ambiguity out of the upstream process. The architect is responsible for creating boundaries and concretizing goals.

  • Set the scene: before the architect is ever engaged, an “upstream process” is already running — loosely-defined issues, opportunities, needs
  • Nobody designed or controls this upstream process (returns strongly in Box 9.2) — it’s inherently messy
  • Flavor of the actual questions the architect walks into: high-priority needs vs. “nice to haves”? Which regulations apply and how? How does manufacturing competence align with the envisioned product? — none come with clean answers
  • → Architect’s core job at this interface (have students write it down verbatim): drives ambiguity out of the upstream process by creating boundaries and concretizing goals — turns fuzzy into workable/bounded. Thesis for the rest of 9.2

Driving Ambiguity Out: The Architect’s Inputs

  • Interpreting corporate and functional strategies, competitive marketing analyses
  • Listening to users, beneficiaries, customers, or their representatives
  • Considering the competence of the enterprise and its supply chain
  • Considering operations and the operational environment of the system
  • Infusing technology where appropriate
  • Interpreting regulatory and pre-regulatory influences
  • Recommending standards, frameworks, and best practice
  • Developing goals for the system based on the upstream influences
  • The concrete, actionable version of “driving ambiguity out” — this is literally what the architect spends time doing
  • Interpreting corporate/functional strategies + competitive marketing analyses → translating business intent into something concrete enough to design against
  • Listening to users/beneficiaries/customers → direct human-input side (full treatment in Ch. 11)
  • Considering enterprise/supply-chain competence → honesty about what can actually be built, not just what’s ideal
  • Considering operations/operational environment → how the system will actually be used/maintained once fielded
  • Infusing technology where appropriate → new capability only where it serves goals, not for its own sake
  • Interpreting regulatory influences + recommending standards/frameworks → navigating external rules and conventions
  • → Capstone: developing goals from all these upstream influences — synthesizing everything into a concrete set the design team can work from. Compressed preview of Ch. 10

From Goals to Concept to Complexity

Employing creativity: once goals are defined, there’s a creative task — defining a concept. A good concept doesn’t ensure success, but a bad concept almost certainly dooms a system to failure. Tasks include proposing concept options, identifying key metrics and drivers, conducting highest-level trades, selecting a concept (and perhaps a backup), thinking holistically about the full lifecycle, and anticipating failure modes.

Managing complexity: once a concept is chosen, information explodes rapidly — external interfaces, first-level design, decomposition, downstream considerations. The architect manages this by decomposing form and function, allocating functionality to form, defining interfaces, configuring subsystems, balancing flexibility vs. optimality, and controlling product evolution.

  • Advances past “reducing ambiguity” into the other two roles, which follow naturally once goals exist
  • Once goals are defined: creative task = defining a concept
  • Blunt, memorable formulation: a good concept doesn’t ensure success, but a bad one almost certainly dooms the system — concept selection is asymmetric risk
  • Concrete tasks: propose multiple concept options (don’t settle for the first idea), identify key metrics/drivers, run highest-level trades, select a concept (+ maybe a backup), think holistically about the full lifecycle, anticipate failure modes
  • Pivot — counter-intuitive dynamic: at the moment a concept is chosen, information available is small — but it explodes rapidly right after (external interfaces, first-level design/decomposition, downstream considerations)
  • → Managing that explosion = the architect’s third role, managing complexity: decompose form/function, allocate functionality to form, define interfaces, configure subsystems, balance flexibility vs. optimality, control product evolution — the toolkit Ch. 13 develops in full

Box 9.1 · Principle of the Role of the Architect

“Some single mind must master, else there will be no agreement in anything.” — Abraham Lincoln

“Timing has a whole bunch to do with the outcome of a rain dance.” — Cowboy saying

  • The role of the architect is to resolve ambiguity, focus creativity, and simplify complexity — creating elegant systems that create value and competitive advantage
  • Given the ambiguity and complexity in most systems, it’s often desirable to have architecture created by a single individual or a small group
  • The architect maintains a holistic view but always focuses on the small number of issues critical to the design, and adopts different frameworks and paradigms as appropriate — not a single fixed method
  • Chapter’s first formally named principle — unpack both quotes, they hit different aspects of the role
  • Lincoln quote (“some single mind must master…”) → second bullet: given typical ambiguity/complexity, architecture is often best created by a single individual or small group, not committee — someone must hold the whole picture and make final calls
  • Cowboy saying (rain dance timing) → more playful, but real point: judgment and sequencing — when to commit matters as much as the decision itself
  • Restate role as a formal principle: resolve ambiguity, focus creativity, simplify complexity → elegant systems that create value/competitive advantage (callback to Boeing/BlackBerry, Ch. 1)
  • → Methodological close: architect keeps a holistic view but focuses on the few critical issues at any moment (echo of Ch. 2’s Principle of Focus) — and doesn’t lock into one fixed method, adopts whatever framework fits

Two Kinds of Ambiguity

Ambiguity is composed of two ideas: fuzziness and uncertainty. In common usage it also connotes incorrect, missing, or conflicting information.

Fuzziness

Occurs when an event or state is subject to multiple interpretations. “Smooth finish” or “good gas mileage” mean different things to different customers — fuzziness is influenced by context.

Uncertainty

Occurs when an event’s outcome is unclear or in doubt. We can articulate the possible states, but not which one will occur — like a coin flip.

  • Chapter’s most rigorous contribution: pin down exactly what “ambiguity” means, not a vague catch-all
  • Strictly: ambiguity = fuzziness + uncertainty; everyday usage also stretches it to mean incorrect/missing/conflicting info (next slide’s precise notation)
  • Fuzziness (more subtle): event/state subject to multiple valid interpretations — category boundaries unclear, not the answer unknown
    • Book’s example: is a color “blue” or “purple” — one fixed wavelength, but the label is genuinely fuzzy, observer-dependent
    • Customer requirements: “smooth finish,” “good gas mileage” mean different things to different people; North America vs. Europe have different baselines for “good” gas mileage
  • Uncertainty (contrast): event’s outcome unclear/in doubt, but possible states are well-defined — we just don’t know which occurs (coin flip: heads/tails both well-defined, outcome unknown)
  • → Clean distinction: fuzziness = ambiguity of categories/interpretation; uncertainty = ambiguity of which known outcome occurs

The (X, Y, Z) Shorthand for Compounding Ambiguity

Where X, Y, Z are the true inputs to the system:

  • Unknown information (X, __, Z): information is under-determined or unavailable. A known unknown is knowing you lack it (a competitor’s plans); an unknown unknown is not even knowing you lack it (a brand-new competitor appears)
  • Conflicting information (X, D, Z and X, B, Z): two or more sources offer opposing indications — the problem is over-determined
  • False information (F, Y, Z): you believe you have complete information, but some of it is simply wrong
  • Book’s notational device for three additional, compounding forms of ambiguity — genuinely useful for diagnosing what kind of informational problem you’re facing
  • Setup: true inputs to a system = triple (X, Y, Z), which if known correctly/completely would fully specify the situation
  • Unknown information (X, __, Z) — one input under-determined/unavailable, a genuine gap, extremely common early in PDP
    • Known unknown: aware of the gap (don’t know if a competitor will launch a rival product) — can budget effort to investigate
    • Unknown unknown: don’t even know you’re missing it (a brand-new, unconsidered competitor appears) — more dangerous
  • Conflicting information — two competing triples (X, D, Z) and (X, B, Z): sources disagree on the middle input, problem is over-determined. Example: one source says regulation will affect the product, another says it won’t
  • False information (F, Y, Z) — you believe your info is complete, but one piece is silently wrong. Example: told a supplier will be ready by a date, but they have no plan to be
  • → Why false info is worse than unknown: with an unknown, you know to be cautious; with false info, you proceed with unwarranted confidence

Classifying Ambiguity — A Quick Drill

  • Will it be a boy or a girl? (Uncertain, Fuzzy)
  • Produce a low-cost, high-quality product. (Conflicting, Fuzzy, Unknown)
  • Every fourth year is a leap year. (False)
  • Please make me a smooth cover for this phone. (Fuzzy)
  • Make sure we meet your quarterly goals. (Unknown, Conflicting)

Tip

Ambiguity is almost always present in the upstream influences — segmented roughly by corporate function: strategy, marketing, customers, manufacturing, operations, R&D, regulations, standards.

  • Active practice, not a read-off — cover the classifications, have students attempt each before revealing
  • “Boy or girl?” → uncertain (two well-defined outcomes, don’t know which); also fuzzy in the book’s fuller discussion
  • “Low-cost, high-quality product” → three kinds at once: conflicting (cost vs. quality pull against each other), fuzzy (“low”/“high” are interpretive), unknown (may not know achievable levels)
  • “Every fourth year is a leap year” → false info dressed as fact — actual rule excludes century years not divisible by 400 (1900 wasn’t a leap year, 2000 was)
  • “Smooth cover for this phone” → clean, pure fuzziness (echoes “smooth finish” earlier)
  • “Meet your quarterly goals” → unknown (may not know exactly what’s required) + conflicting (stakeholders may disagree on what “meeting the goals” means)
  • → Generalizes: ambiguity is almost always present in upstream influences, segmentable by corporate function — strategy, marketing, customers, manufacturing, operations, R&D, regulations, standards. Previews the next slide and sets up Ch. 10

Box 9.2 · Principle of Ambiguity

“The best-laid schemes o’ mice an’ men / Gang aft agley [often go awry]” — Robert Burns

  • The early phase of a system design is characterized by great ambiguity — the architect must resolve it to produce (and continuously update) goals for the team
  • Development is possible only with the acceptance of uncertainty
  • No one designs or rigorously controls the upstream process — there should be no expectation of unambiguity
  • Uncertainty can create opportunities; it is not always bad
  • Uncertainties should be identified and prioritized so they can be managed
  • Robert Burns quote (“best-laid schemes… gang aft agley”) — sets the tenor: plans routinely go sideways, architect must operate productively despite that
  • Five bullets as a connected argument, not independent facts:
    • Early design phase = great ambiguity; architect’s job to resolve enough to produce and continuously update usable goals — ongoing, not one-time
    • Development is only possible with acceptance of uncertainty — insisting on perfect certainty first is a decision to never start
    • No one designs/controls the upstream process (corrects a misconception) — no expectation of unambiguity; waiting for a clean start is a losing strategy
    • Uncertainty can create opportunities, not just risk — an unresolved situation sometimes has room for a better solution than a rigid brief
    • → Prescription: don’t try to eliminate uncertainties (impossible) or ignore them (dangerous) — identify and prioritize them so the most consequential get managed deliberately (echo of Ch. 2’s Principle of Focus)

Deliverables of the Architect

Deliverables are different from tasks — they are the end result, not the procedure by which it’s achieved.

  • A clear, complete, consistent, attainable (80–90% confidence) set of goals, emphasizing functional goals
  • A description of the broader context, including the whole product context
  • A concept for the system, and a concept of operations including contingency/emergency operations
  • A functional description with at least two layers of decomposition
  • The decomposition of form to two levels of detail, and the structure at this level
  • Details of all external interfaces and a process for interface control
  • A notion of developmental cost, schedule, risk, and design/implementation plans
  • Crisp distinction first: deliverable = the end result; task = the procedure to get there. This slide lists deliverables, not a to-do list
  • Walk through each, tied to prior material:
    • Clear/complete/consistent/attainable goals — book gives 80-90% confidence target, emphasizes functional goals (Ch. 2)
    • Description of broader/whole-product context — Ch. 2’s Task 2, now at product-wide scale
    • A concept (Ch. 7) and a concept of operations, including contingency/emergency ops
    • Functional description, ≥2 layers of decomposition — recalls “2 Down, 1 Up” (Ch. 13 formalizes)
    • Decomposition of form to 2 levels + structure — formal-domain counterpart
    • External interface details + an actual control process, not one-time documentation
    • Cost/schedule/risk notion + design/implementation plans
  • Table 8.1 (previous chapter) leads to all of these except the last (a project summary instead)
  • → Ongoing challenge: representing deliverables in a form the org can absorb is part of the job — OPM/SysML don’t always fit an org’s culture; effective architecting means finding what actually lands with your audience

Section 9.2 Summary

  • The role of the architect includes reducing ambiguity, employing creativity, and managing complexity
  • Architecture sits astride upstream activities (before) and downstream activities (after) — ambiguity arises from both
  • In practice, ambiguity is a combination of fuzzy, uncertain, missing, conflicting, and incorrect information
  • The principal job of the architect at the interface to the upstream (the “fuzzy front end”) is to drive as much of this ambiguity from the system as possible
  • Checkpoint before Section 9.3 — students should recite the three roles from memory
  • Architecture sits “astride” upstream and downstream — literally straddling the boundary, ambiguity flowing in from both (upstream: strategy, fuzzy needs; downstream: manufacturing/operational questions)
  • Should be comfortable with the ambiguity taxonomy: fuzziness + uncertainty (core ideas), missing/conflicting/incorrect info (practical manifestations, the (X,Y,Z) shorthand)
  • → Restate the section’s thesis with memorable vocabulary: the upstream interface = the “fuzzy front end” of development (Zhang and Doll) — architect’s job there is to drive out as much ambiguity as possible, never “all of it” (Box 9.2)

9.3 The Product Development Process

What Is a PDP?

Nearly every large firm has a product development process (PDP) — capturing methodology, terminology, phases, milestones, and tasks/outputs.

  • A principal advantage: it’s a tool for reducing ambiguity by defining tasks and responsibilities
  • But it’s easy to be lulled into thinking the PDP resolves upstream ambiguity automatically — this is only partially true. A PDP can do the architect a disservice if it presumes more certainty than is actually present
  • We seek a PDP that is “birth to death” and “lust to dust” — capturing everything from first envisioning a product to retiring the last instance
  • Define the object of study: PDP = internal methodology nearly every large firm has — terminology, phases, milestones, tasks/outputs
  • Why it matters for an architect: principal advantage is reducing ambiguity by defining tasks/responsibilities — the Section 9.2 role, partially automated by process
  • Caution — easy trap: tempting to think a well-defined PDP resolves upstream ambiguity automatically — only partially true. A rigid-looking process with clean gates can create false confidence that strategy/market/needs/tech are well-understood when they’re still deeply ambiguous underneath
  • → Ambitious target scope: “birth to death” and “lust to dust” — first envisioning through retirement of the last instance. Most real PDPs fall short; the four case studies (NASA, Helicopter Inc., Camera Co., Agile) each cover a different partial slice

Comparing the Four PDPs

Are differences between PDPs superficial, or substantial? Some observed differences:

  • Existence/number of phases and phase exit criteria
  • Existence/number of design reviews, timing for committing capital
  • Requirements “enforcement,” degree of customer input/feedback/aftermarket integration
  • Degree of explicit/implicit iteration, amount of prototyping
  • Internal testing/validation effort, importance of traceability, timing of supplier involvement

Tip

Many factors could drive real differences: hardware vs. software, existing vs. new product, standalone vs. platform, production volume, capital intensity, technology push vs. market pull.

  • Step back and synthesize across all four case studies — the analytical move the book wants practiced, not just four admired diagrams
  • Central question: are the differences among these four PDPs superficial or substantial?
  • Observed differences: number/existence of phases + how rigorously exit criteria enforced; number of formal reviews + when capital commits; how strictly requirements are enforced + customer input/feedback/aftermarket integration; explicit vs. implicit iteration + prototyping amount; internal testing/validation effort, traceability importance, supplier-involvement timing
  • Deeper diagnostic question: what factors could drive real differences — hardware vs. software, existing vs. new product, standalone vs. platform, production volume, capital intensity, technology-push vs. market-pull
  • → Book’s conclusion (next slide builds toward it): despite surface differences, there’s enormous common underlying structure — motivates building a single Generic PDP

Figure 9.5 · The Generic PDP

Four common activity groups: conceiving, designing, implementing, operating — a checklist, not a stage-gate process. The architect’s primary domain sits in “conceive,” but must move relevant downstream information back into the architecting phase. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.5.

  • Payoff of the comparative exercise — anchors the rest of the chapter
  • Despite real differences among the four case studies, there’s a large body of common effective practice — codified as the Generic PDP: four activity groups — conceiving, designing, implementing, operating
  • Crucial framing (stated at the top, worth repeating): this represents activities, not a sequence of stage-gates — NOT “first conceive, then design, then implement, then operate” in strict linear order (that’s the flawed linear representation the next slide criticizes)
  • What’s in each group:
    • Conceiving: strategy, functional strategies, customer needs, segments, competitors, technology, regulation, scope/plan, mission, conceptual design — driven by market needs and tech availability
    • Designing: preliminary/detailed design, requirements definition/elaboration, model development, flow-down, decomposition, interface control — converts concept into the info object defining what’s to be implemented
    • Implementing: sourcing, element creation, integration, testing, positioning, certification — converts design into reality; deliberately bundles software coding + hardware manufacturing + systems integration under one term
    • Operating: sales, distribution, logistics, support, maintenance/repair/overhaul, upgrades, product improvement, platform expansion, retirement
  • → Visually important: “Primary Domain of the Architect” band sits under conceiving, but a “Downstream influences” arrow feeds back into it — architect must move relevant downstream info back into the conceiving phase, not architect in isolation (developed further in Ch. 10)

Linear Representations Are Flawed

Studies have shown that linear representations of the design process are deeply flawed — they fail to represent iterations and feedback, presume a linear flow of time and effort across stages, and can mask a design’s immaturity when gate criteria are compromised.

  • The Generic PDP is a checklist of activities that appear in complete PDPs, not a mandated sequence
  • “Implementing” deliberately includes coding software, manufacturing hardware, and integrating into systems
  • “Operating” includes support and release of future product instances — ending in retirement
  • Why the Generic PDP was drawn this way: studies have shown linear design-process representations are deeply flawed (genuine research finding, not editorial opinion)
  • Three failure modes of linear diagrams:
    • Fail to represent iterations/feedback (the loops explicit in Helicopter Inc.’s PDP)
    • Presume a linear flow of time/effort across stages, when real projects rarely proceed cleanly
    • Most operationally dangerous: can mask a design’s immaturity when gate criteria are compromised — a project can nominally “pass” a gate on paper while the underlying work isn’t ready, with no visual signal
  • → Restate intended use: the Generic PDP is a checklist of activities in complete PDPs, not a mandated order
  • Deliberate word choices: “implementing” bundles software coding + hardware manufacturing + systems integration under one term; “operating” includes ongoing support of existing instances and release of future ones, ending in retirement (“lust to dust”)

  • Nearly every large firm has an internal PDP — a tool for reducing ambiguity, but one that can mislead if it presumes more certainty than is actually present
  • Comparing PDPs across NASA, Helicopter Inc., Camera Co., and Agile reveals many superficial differences, but real underlying similarities
  • The Generic PDP codifies this common practice as four activity groups: conceive, design, implement, operate — deliberately not a stage-gate process
  • Checkpoint before the Global PDP — the chapter’s final major move
  • A PDP is a genuine but double-edged ambiguity-reduction tool: useful for defining tasks/responsibilities, dangerous for creating false confidence about how settled the upstream situation is
  • Students should name all four case studies and their distinguishing priorities: NASA (traceability/review-heavy, low-volume high-cost high-perceived-risk systems), Helicopter Inc. (iteration loops, certification-as-instrument-of-sales), Camera Co. (manufacturing precedes product development), Agile (iterative/incremental, low cost-of-change)

9.3.2 The Global PDP

Building the Global PDP

  • To further support comparison across PDPs, we construct an extended baseline: the Global PDP, in three nested views
  • Level 1 centers on the architecture of the product — function and form — as a reflection of product goals, themselves set to respond to market/stakeholder needs
  • The architect answers the canonical W Questions: why, what, how, where, when, who, how much

Tip

Quo is the shared Indo-European root of these words across languages — French qui/quoi/quand, Hindi kab/kya/kyon, and the seven circumstances of the ancient Greek rhetorician Hermagoras.

  • Chapter’s most ambitious construct: the Global PDP, three progressively broader nested views (this slide = Level 1; next two = Levels 2 and 3)
  • Level 1 centers on the product’s architecture — function and form (Ch. 2/4/5 vocabulary) — as a reflection of product goals, set in response to market/stakeholder needs
  • → Architecture (form+function) sits at the literal center of the framework, since it’s what everything else exists to produce
  • “W Questions”: why, what, how, where, when, who, how much
  • Etymological color: all trace to Indo-European root quo — French (qui/quoi/quand/combien/ou/comment/pourquoi), Hindi (kab/kya/kyon/kitne/kisne/kahan)
  • Greek rhetorician Hermagoras: seven circumstances (quis, quid, quando, ubi, cur, quem ad modum, quibus adminiculis) = same who/what/when/where/why/how/by-what-means structure, ~2000 years ago
  • → Implication: this cross-linguistic, cross-era recurrence signals the question set captures something fundamental about how humans interrogate any situation — an architect needs to understand all of these attributes well

  • → Architecture (form=where + function=how) sits at the literal center, both geometrically and conceptually — everything else (need, goals, behavior, users, cost) surrounds and relates back to it

Figure 9.7 · Level 2 — Design and Implementation in Parallel

Moving up a layer: the design process and implementation process each get their own instantiation of the W Questions. The design process has its own form too — notably design tools, which shape and constrain the system form that’s achievable. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.7.

  • Level 2: adds two more full applications of the same seven W Questions — one for the design process, one for the implementation process — running in parallel alongside Level 1’s “conceiving” row (three horizontal rows: Design process / Conceiving the product and operations / Implement process)
  • Subtle key point: the design process is itself a system with its own form — notably design tools. A straightedge vs. a French curve produces different renderings, hence different achievable system forms — the tools used to design meaningfully constrain what’s achievable
  • Automotive example: many automakers split non-recurring engineering (NRE) design costs evenly across platform variants launched the same year/volume — not part of any technical evaluation, wouldn’t show up in a stage-gate review, but clearly part of the Generic/Global PDP because it materially affects whether a variant can economically deliver value
  • Implementation process similarly has its own capabilities/limitations the architect must factor in — existing manufacturing/coding capability constrains what can be built regardless of the paper design
  • → Practical use: critically evaluate any PDP or new design method against the 21 implicit questions (3 rows × 7 W Questions) — few methods answer all 21; the value is seeing which areas a framework is strongest in. Example: Lean began from Toyota Production System studies (manufacturing cost/quality), moved upstream to design-for-lean-manufacturing, and (more controversially) has also been applied to the design process itself — far removed from Lean’s original intent

Figure 9.8 · Level 3 — The Enterprise Context

The broadest nested view: the firm’s PDP inside the enterprise boundary (R&D and corporate functions mostly upstream; PR, sales, distribution mostly downstream), plus external actors and attributes — capital, competition, regulation — that affect the enterprise. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.8.

  • Level 3, the broadest view: whole PDP (conceive/design/implement/operate, overlapping ellipses, architecture at the center intersection) placed inside a larger enterprise boundary
  • Firm functions positioned by upstream/downstream role: R&D and corporate functions primarily upstream (feed conceiving); PR/sales/distribution primarily downstream (connected to operating); some (human skills, information systems, engineering tools) permeate the whole diagram; others (corporate strategy) play multiple roles at once

    • Out: economic impact, profits, political impact, social impact, corporate image, IP, goods, services, technology, pollution
  • → Purpose of this broadest view (easy to misread as detail-for-its-own-sake): reminds the architect that understanding the PDP’s contents alone isn’t sufficient — must also track issues influencing the firm and its context (Box 9.3 next)

Box 9.3 · Principle of the Stress of Modern Practice

“Things which matter most must never be at the mercy of things which matter least.” — Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

Modern product development — with concurrency, distributed teams, and early supplier engagement — places even more emphasis on having a good architecture:

  • Accelerating development via concurrency increases the importance of initial conceptual decisions
  • Empowering distributed/non-collocated teams increases the importance of well-coordinated, visible high-level design guidance
  • Involving suppliers early increases the importance of an architectural concept and baseline — suppliers can define or defy decomposition
  • Close the Global PDP discussion — Goethe quote (“things which matter most must never be at the mercy of things which matter least”) captures the worry: modern pressures can let minor operational details crowd out consequential early architectural decisions
  • Each modern trend raises the stakes on getting architecture right, not lowers them (contrary to a naive “efficiency improvement” reading):
    • Concurrency: parallel activities lock in initial conceptual decisions faster, less room to course-correct — getting them right upfront matters more
    • Distributed/non-collocated teams: well-coordinated, visible high-level design guidance matters more — without it, autonomous teams drift out of alignment
    • Early supplier engagement: architectural concept/baseline matters more — suppliers can either define or defy the intended decomposition depending on how clearly it’s communicated
  • → Overarching message: none of these three practices are inherently bad — but each raises the cost of not having architected well, which is why this principle closes the chapter

Section 9.3.2 Summary

  • The Global PDP is a nested framework used to compare different PDPs on a common baseline, built around the canonical W Questions
  • It reveals the architect’s system boundary — largely, but not exclusively, the “conceive” activities
  • The purpose of the broadest view is to remind the architect: understanding the contents of the PDP is not sufficient — the firm and its context matter too
  • Closing checkpoint for the whole PDP discussion — students should describe all three nested levels in order: product architecture (center), design/implementation processes in parallel (own W Questions, own form — design tools), whole enterprise context (upstream/downstream functions + external actors)
  • Built specifically to give a common baseline for comparing genuinely different PDPs (NASA, Helicopter Inc., Camera Co., Agile) fairly, not superficially
  • Concrete takeaway: architect’s primary system boundary is largely (not exclusively) the “conceive” activities — with the caveat (Fig. 9.5) that downstream info must still be actively moved back in
  • → Broadest lesson: understanding PDP contents alone is necessary but not sufficient — firm and context matter too. Transitions into the civil-architecture case study, where “context” is nearly sacred

Box 9.4 — Case Study: Civil Architecture and System Architecture

Where “Architect” Comes From

By Steve Imrich, Architect and Principal at Cambridge Seven Associates

  • Civil architecture has an interpretive-interactive-performance aspect that differs from most industrial/product design — buildings inherit complex cultural and symbolic qualities affecting space, materials, and user experience
  • Ideally, the “special problems” of a building inform its design — as with helicopters, sailboats, or computers, concept/design/engineering can transcend mere style
  • Because buildings are only seen/experienced as part of their site, their special problems always relate to a wider context: time, culture, infrastructure, human behavior, environment, re-interpreted use
  • Buildings are rarely mocked up beforehand — a large element of risk, usually associated with the arts
  • Closing case study, unusual format: guest contribution by practicing civil architect Steve Imrich (Cambridge Seven Associates) — included because the word “architect” is borrowed directly from civil/building architecture, worth hearing from the original field
  • Central question to pose: what do civil architecture and system architecture actually share?
  • Distinguishing feature #1: buildings carry an interpretive-interactive-performance aspect more pronounced than most product design — inherit complex cultural/symbolic qualities affecting space, materials, user experience beyond pure function
  • “Special problems” (recurring phrase): a building’s special problems — particular, non-generic challenges of that site/client/moment — should inform its design
    • Parallel to other fields: helicopters, sailboats, computers successfully resolve their own special problems, where concept+design+engineering transcend mere style — callback to the Principle of Elegance (Ch. 13)
  • Two more distinguishing features: buildings are only seen/experienced as part of their site, so special problems always relate to a wide context (time, culture, infrastructure, human behavior, environment, re-interpreted use)
  • → Buildings are rarely mocked up beforehand — a large element of risk, one the book says is generally associated more with the arts than conventional engineering

Left: Trulli houses, Alberobello, Italy (a). Right: industrial ductwork, Centre Pompidou, Paris (b). The concept is the departure point for integrating performance and form. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.9. Photos: (a) Funkyfood London-Paul Williams/Alamy, (b) Francisco Javier Gil/Fotolia.

  • Trulli houses (Alberobello, Italy) — conical stone roofs — vs. Centre Pompidou (Paris) — exposes structural/mechanical systems on the exterior. Stylistically opposite, same underlying idea: the concept is the departure point for integrating performance and form

  • → Precise definition: concept = departure point for organization/experience, the unifying idea by which all design decisions get evaluated. Conical roofs responding to climate, or a museum making its mechanical guts a visual statement — either way, one strong organizing idea lets performance and form cohere (same pattern as Ch. 2’s process/operand, instrument/process/operand)

Six Ideas in “Design Process with Attitude”

  • Context — the built environment, cultural norms, and history of the organizations and people the building serves
  • Content — the building’s programmatic requirements and functional performance
  • Concept — the unifying idea by which all design decisions are evaluated; concept generation succeeds when it integrates and balances the nature of content and context
  • Circuitry — the connective tissue between functional components; more than a path from A to B, it creates interest and anticipation
  • Character — a building’s material, site, and sense of life beyond the aggregation of parts
  • Magic — the largely intangible quality defined by the user’s experience; magic fosters delight and joyful appreciation of the unexpected
  • Guest author’s vocabulary — “design process with attitude”, deliberately contrasted against a conventional PDP. Define each of the six:
    • Context: the wider setting — adjacent buildings, cultural norms, environmental conditions, history/motivations of the people the building serves
    • Content: what the building is for — programmatic requirements + functional performance (Ch. 5’s function vocabulary, at building scale)
    • Concept: the unifying idea all decisions are evaluated by — succeeds specifically when it integrates and balances content and context together
    • Circuitry: connective tissue between functional components — more than A-to-B; done well, creates interest/anticipation as boundaries and spaces get progressively revealed
    • Character: material choices, site, sense of life beyond the aggregation of parts — related to elegance/emergence themes, applied to how a building feels over time
    • Magic: largely intangible, defined by the user’s experience (not designer/owner intent) — fosters delight and joyful appreciation of the unexpected
  • → Present this six-part framework as genuinely different vocabulary from the process/operand, function/form language elsewhere in the course — captures dimensions (character, magic) that purely functional analysis leaves out

  • Both are, at bottom, functional infrastructure (performance venues; a vehicular/pedestrian crossing) — yet both transcended pure function to become iconic symbols of their cities/countries — exactly the “magic” quality from the previous slide
  • → Ask students for their own examples (buildings, bridges, everyday products) and push them to articulate why, using the six-part vocabulary, not a vague “it just looks cool.” Sets up the next slide’s deeper development of magic

On Magic

“Surprise, as all art and architecture, helps us contemplate. Life wears out our ability for surprise. Surprise is the beginning of a true vision of the world.” — Eladio Dieste

  • Magic is the emergence of elegance and character at the building level — the sleight of hand
  • It is difficult to define, but when something is magical, it’s immediately recognizable
  • Magic is the unusual circumstance that becomes normalized in good architecture — that moment of surprise that leaves a person in wonder and awe
  • Real magic makes architecture symbolic and iconic in a sustained manner — it reaches beyond the original intent, communicating viscerally with users
  • Eladio Dieste quote: surprise helps us contemplate — valuable because ordinary life, through repetition, wears out our capacity for surprise; a genuine architectural surprise is rare and valuable, “the beginning of a true vision of the world”
  • Progressively deeper definition of magic:
    • Magic = emergence of elegance and character at the building level (technical “emergence” sense, Ch. 2) — “the sleight of hand”
    • Difficult to define, but immediately recognizable when present — parallel to “you know it when you see it” properties, including elegance itself
    • The unusual circumstance that becomes normalized in good architecture — a moment of surprise leaving wonder/awe, not confusion
    • → Real, lasting magic (not passing novelty) makes architecture symbolic/iconic in a sustained way — reaches beyond original designer intent, communicates viscerally with users regardless of whether they know that intent

Chapter 9 Summary

  • The architect is not a generalist, but a specialist in resolving ambiguity, focusing creativity, and simplifying complexity
  • Ambiguity comes in many forms — fuzziness, uncertainty, and missing, conflicting, or incorrect information — and the architect must recognize and deal with each
  • The architect is responsible for producing specific deliverables, central to reducing ambiguity and bridging corporate functions to the design environment
  • Product development processes differ superficially across firms and sectors, but share deep common structure — codified here as the Generic and Global PDPs
  • Architects must discern which segments of a PDP are centrally linked to their role, and stay skilled at interpreting new product development initiatives

  • → Closing practical charge: architects must discern which PDP segments are centrally linked to their role, and stay skilled at interpreting new development initiatives (Lean, Agile, Six Sigma, whatever’s next) on their actual merits, not fashion
