Starting Part 3: Creating System Architecture

  • Part 2 was about holism — the architect must manage a huge number of considerations
  • This can be misread as “the architect is a generalist.” Not so: to paraphrase Eberhardt Rechtin, the architect is a specialist in simplifying complexity, resolving ambiguity, and focusing creativity
  • The architect is not the design team, the financial controller, the marketing executive, or the plant manager

Tip

Three principal roles: reduce ambiguity, employ creativity, manage complexity.

Open Part 3 by naming the risk this chapter is specifically designed to head off. Throughout Part 2, we emphasized holism repeatedly — the architect has to manage a huge number of considerations spanning form, function, interfaces, and context. Students (and practitioners) can easily misread that emphasis as “the architect must therefore be a generalist who understands a little about everything.” The book pushes back hard on this, paraphrasing systems engineer Eberhardt Rechtin: the architect is not a generalist at all, but a genuine specialist — specifically a specialist in simplifying complexity, resolving ambiguity, and focusing creativity. Those are the specific, nameable skills of the role.

Make the negative definition explicit too, since it helps students who are used to thinking of “architect” as some kind of super-manager: the architect is specifically not the design team (who does the detailed engineering), not the financial controller (who manages budget), not the marketing executive (who defines customer positioning), and not the plant manager (who runs manufacturing operations). All of those roles matter, and the architect must interact with all of them — but none of them is the architect’s job.

The closing fragment previews the chapter’s entire structure, and it’s worth having students hold onto this three-way split for the rest of the lecture: reduce ambiguity, employ creativity, manage complexity. Section 9.2 develops the first role in depth (creativity gets its full chapter-length treatment later, in Chapters 11 and 12; complexity gets the same in Chapter 13) — this chapter is really laying the conceptual foundation that the rest of Part 3 will build out one role at a time.

The Three Roles of the Architect

Reduce ambiguity

Define the boundaries, goals, and functions of the system.

Employ creativity

Create the concept.

Manage complexity

Choose a decomposition of the system.

These three roles all center on information: reducing ambiguity by identifying necessary, consistent, important information; adding new information through creativity; and managing the explosion of information in the final architecture.

Unpack each of the three roles concretely, since the previous slide only named them. Reducing ambiguity means defining the boundaries, goals, and functions of the system — precisely the Task 1/Task 2 vocabulary from Chapter 2, now applied to a system that doesn’t exist yet, where none of that is given. Employing creativity means creating the concept — recall from Chapter 7’s vocabulary, the mapping from function to form that isn’t yet determined. Managing complexity means choosing a decomposition of the system — the central topic of Chapter 13, echoing the decomposition and hierarchy tools from Chapter 3.

The fragment gives students a genuinely unifying lens for all three roles, worth dwelling on: they’re all fundamentally about information. Reducing ambiguity is about identifying the necessary, consistent, and important information buried in a noisy, ambiguous upstream process. Employing creativity is about adding new information — a concept doesn’t exist until the architect invents it. And managing complexity is about handling the explosion of information that occurs once the concept is chosen and detailed design begins in earnest (this “information explosion” idea gets its own slide shortly). Seeing all three architectural roles as different modes of working with information is a genuinely useful mental model for students to carry through the rest of Part 3.

9.2 Ambiguity and the Role of the Architect

Before the Architect Is Engaged

  • There has been an “upstream process” — full of ambiguity, issues, opportunities, and needs for the new system
  • What are the high-priority needs vs. the “nice to haves”? What regulations apply? How is manufacturing competence aligned with the envisioned product?

The architect drives ambiguity out of the upstream process. The architect is responsible for creating boundaries and concretizing goals.

Set the scene for where the architect actually enters the picture. Before the architect is ever engaged, there has already been an “upstream process” running — full of loosely-defined issues, opportunities, and needs for whatever new system is being contemplated. This upstream process is not something anyone designed or controls (a point the chapter returns to and emphasizes strongly later, in Box 9.2) — it’s inherently messy.

Give students the flavor of the actual questions the architect walks into mid-stream: which needs are truly high-priority versus merely “nice to have”? What regulations must be considered, and how will they be dealt with? How does the enterprise’s actual manufacturing competence align with whatever product is being envisioned? None of these questions come with clean answers already attached — that’s exactly what “ambiguity” in the upstream process means in concrete terms.

The fragment states the architect’s core job at this interface, worth having students write down verbatim: the architect drives ambiguity out of the upstream process, and specifically does so by creating boundaries and concretizing goals — turning a fuzzy situation into a workable, bounded problem. This is the thesis the rest of Section 9.2 unpacks in detail.

Driving Ambiguity Out: The Architect’s Inputs

  • Interpreting corporate and functional strategies, competitive marketing analyses
  • Listening to users, beneficiaries, customers, or their representatives
  • Considering the competence of the enterprise and its supply chain
  • Considering operations and the operational environment of the system
  • Infusing technology where appropriate
  • Interpreting regulatory and pre-regulatory influences
  • Recommending standards, frameworks, and best practice
  • Developing goals for the system based on the upstream influences

Walk through this list as the concrete, actionable version of “driving ambiguity out of the upstream process” — this is literally what the architect spends time doing at this stage. Interpreting corporate and functional strategies, and competitive marketing analyses, means translating high-level business intent into something concrete enough to design against. Listening to users, beneficiaries, customers, or their representatives is the direct human-input side (this gets a full, rigorous treatment later in Chapter 11 on stakeholders and needs). Considering the competence of the enterprise and its supply chain means being honest about what the organization and its partners can actually build, not just what would be ideal in the abstract.

Considering operations and the operational environment means thinking ahead to how the system will actually be used and maintained once fielded — not just how it’s built. Infusing technology where appropriate means bringing in new technical capability, but only where it genuinely serves the goals, not for its own sake. Interpreting regulatory and pre-regulatory influences and recommending standards, frameworks, and best practice both mean navigating the external rules and conventions the system must respect. And finally, developing goals for the system based on all of these upstream influences is the capstone activity — synthesizing everything above into an actual, concrete set of goals the design team can work from. This list is worth returning to explicitly when Chapter 10 covers upstream and downstream influences in much greater depth — this slide is the compressed preview of that entire chapter.

From Goals to Concept to Complexity

Employing creativity: once goals are defined, there’s a creative task — defining a concept. A good concept doesn’t ensure success, but a bad concept almost certainly dooms a system to failure. Tasks include proposing concept options, identifying key metrics and drivers, conducting highest-level trades, selecting a concept (and perhaps a backup), thinking holistically about the full lifecycle, and anticipating failure modes.

Managing complexity: once a concept is chosen, information explodes rapidly — external interfaces, first-level design, decomposition, downstream considerations. The architect manages this by decomposing form and function, allocating functionality to form, defining interfaces, configuring subsystems, balancing flexibility vs. optimality, and controlling product evolution.

This slide advances the narrative past “reducing ambiguity” into the other two roles, showing how they follow naturally in sequence once goals exist. Once goals for the system have been defined (the payoff of the previous two slides), there’s now a genuinely creative task: defining a concept. State the book’s blunt, memorable formulation precisely: a good concept does not ensure system success, but a bad choice of concept almost certainly dooms a system to failure — concept selection is asymmetric risk, not a guarantee of reward. Walk through the concrete tasks this involves: proposing and developing multiple concept options (not settling for the first idea), identifying the key metrics and drivers that matter, conducting the highest-level trades and optimization across those options, selecting a concept to carry forward (and, prudently, perhaps a backup in case the primary choice runs into trouble), thinking holistically about the entire product lifecycle rather than just the initial build, and anticipating failure modes along with plans for mitigation and recovery.

Then pivot to what happens right after a concept is chosen, since this is a genuinely important and somewhat counter-intuitive dynamic worth dwelling on: at the exact moment the concept is chosen, there’s actually a relatively small amount of information available to define the system — but that information explodes very rapidly almost immediately afterward, as team members begin defining external interfaces, the first-level design and decomposition take shape, and downstream considerations get worked out. Managing that explosion — ensuring the system stays comprehensible to everyone despite this rapid growth in information — is exactly the architect’s third role, managing complexity, and the specific mechanisms listed here (decomposing form and function, allocating functionality to form, defining interfaces, configuring subsystems, balancing flexibility against optimality, articulating vertical vs. horizontal strategies, balancing in-house vs. outsourced design and manufacturing, controlling product evolution) are precisely the toolkit Chapter 13 develops in full.

Box 9.1 · Principle of the Role of the Architect

“Some single mind must master, else there will be no agreement in anything.” — Abraham Lincoln

“Timing has a whole bunch to do with the outcome of a rain dance.” — Cowboy saying

  • The role of the architect is to resolve ambiguity, focus creativity, and simplify complexity — creating elegant systems that create value and competitive advantage
  • Given the ambiguity and complexity in most systems, it’s often desirable to have architecture created by a single individual or a small group
  • The architect maintains a holistic view but always focuses on the small number of issues critical to the design, and adopts different frameworks and paradigms as appropriate — not a single fixed method

This is the chapter’s first formally named principle, and it’s worth unpacking both quotes since they point at genuinely different aspects of the role. The Lincoln quote — “some single mind must master, else there will be no agreement in anything” — speaks to the second bullet on this slide: given how much ambiguity and complexity a typical system carries, it’s often genuinely desirable for the architecture to be created by a single individual or a small group, rather than by broad committee, precisely because someone has to hold the whole picture together and make the final calls where reasonable people could disagree. The cowboy saying about rain dances and timing is a bit more playful, but it’s making a real point about the architect’s role in judgment and sequencing — knowing when to commit to a decision matters as much as the decision’s content.

Restate the role’s definition once more, now formalized as a principle rather than just descriptive prose: the role of the architect is to resolve ambiguity, focus creativity, and simplify complexity, with the ultimate goal of creating elegant systems that generate value and competitive advantage (a callback to the Boeing/BlackBerry discussion from Chapter 1). And close with a methodological point that matters for how students should think about applying everything in this course: the architect maintains a holistic view of the whole system, but always focuses attention on the small number of issues that are genuinely critical to the design at any given moment (an echo of Chapter 2’s Principle of Focus) — and, importantly, doesn’t lock into one single fixed method for doing this. The architect adopts whatever frameworks, views, and paradigms fit the situation, drawing on the full toolkit this course provides rather than mechanically applying one procedure to every problem.

Two Kinds of Ambiguity

Ambiguity is composed of two ideas: fuzziness and uncertainty. In common usage it also connotes incorrect, missing, or conflicting information.

Fuzziness

Occurs when an event or state is subject to multiple interpretations. “Smooth finish” or “good gas mileage” mean different things to different customers — fuzziness is influenced by context.

Uncertainty

Occurs when an event’s outcome is unclear or in doubt. We can articulate the possible states, but not which one will occur — like a coin flip.

This slide begins the chapter’s most rigorous, technically precise contribution: pinning down exactly what “ambiguity” means, rather than leaving it as a vague catch-all word. State the decomposition precisely: strictly speaking, ambiguity is composed of exactly two distinct ideas — fuzziness and uncertainty — though in everyday usage the word “ambiguity” also gets stretched to connote incorrect, missing, or conflicting information (which the next slide formalizes with precise notation).

Define fuzziness carefully, since it’s the more subtle of the two: it occurs when an event or state is subject to multiple valid interpretations — not that the answer is unknown, but that the very category boundaries are unclear. The book’s own example is a nice one to use verbatim: whether a particular color counts as “blue” or “purple” — the color itself has one fixed, well-defined wavelength, but whether that wavelength falls under the label “blue” or “purple” is genuinely fuzzy, dependent on the observer. Apply this directly to customer requirements, which is where fuzziness matters most for an architect: when a customer says they want a “smooth” finish, or “good” gas mileage, those phrases mean genuinely different things to different people, and are further shaped by context — the book notes that customers in North America and customers in Europe have different baseline expectations for what counts as “good” gas mileage, given different market norms.

Define uncertainty by contrast: it occurs when an event’s outcome is unclear or in doubt — but critically, unlike fuzziness, the possible states themselves are well-defined; we just don’t know which one will occur. A coin flip is uncertain in exactly this sense: heads and tails are both perfectly well-defined outcomes, we simply don’t know which one is coming. The clean distinction to leave students with: fuzziness is about the ambiguity of categories or interpretation; uncertainty is about the ambiguity of which known outcome will occur.

The (X, Y, Z) Shorthand for Compounding Ambiguity

Where X, Y, Z are the true inputs to the system:

  • Unknown information (X, __, Z): information is under-determined or unavailable. A known unknown is knowing you lack it (a competitor’s plans); an unknown unknown is not even knowing you lack it (a brand-new competitor appears)
  • Conflicting information (X, D, Z and X, B, Z): two or more sources offer opposing indications — the problem is over-determined
  • False information (F, Y, Z): you believe you have complete information, but some of it is simply wrong

Introduce the book’s precise notational device for talking about three additional, compounding forms of ambiguity beyond fuzziness and uncertainty — this shorthand is genuinely useful for precisely diagnosing what kind of informational problem the architect is actually facing at any given moment. Set up the notation carefully: imagine the true inputs to a system are some specific triple (X, Y, Z) — three pieces of information that, if known correctly and completely, would fully specify the situation.

Unknown information is written (X, blank, Z) — one of the three true inputs is simply under-determined or unavailable, a genuine gap. This is extremely common early in a product development cycle. Distinguish the two flavors precisely, since this maps directly back to Chapter 2’s known-unknown/unknown-unknown vocabulary: a known unknown here means you’re aware the gap exists — you know you don’t know whether an existing competitor plans to launch a rival product, so at least you can budget effort to investigate. An unknown unknown is more dangerous — you don’t even know you’re missing this information; the book’s example is a genuinely new competitor entering the market that nobody had even considered as a possibility.

Conflicting information is written as two competing triples, (X, D, Z) and (X, B, Z) — where two or more distinct sources give opposing indications about the true value of the middle input. The problem here is technically over-determined: you have too much information, and it disagrees with itself. The book’s example: one source tells you government regulation will affect your product; another source suggests it won’t — both can’t be simultaneously acted on as given.

False information is written (F, Y, Z) — here, you mistakenly believe your information set is complete (you don’t perceive any gap at all), but one piece of what you think you know is simply, silently wrong. This is arguably the most dangerous of the three, precisely because it doesn’t announce itself as a problem the way a known gap or an open conflict does — the book’s example is being told a supplier will be ready by a certain date, when in fact the supplier has no plan whatsoever to be ready then. Give students a moment to sit with why false information is worse than unknown information: with an unknown, you at least know to be cautious; with false information, you proceed with unwarranted confidence.

Classifying Ambiguity — A Quick Drill

  • Will it be a boy or a girl? (Uncertain, Fuzzy)
  • Produce a low-cost, high-quality product. (Conflicting, Fuzzy, Unknown)
  • Every fourth year is a leap year. (False)
  • Please make me a smooth cover for this phone. (Fuzzy)
  • Make sure we meet your quarterly goals. (Unknown, Conflicting)

Tip

Ambiguity is almost always present in the upstream influences — segmented roughly by corporate function: strategy, marketing, customers, manufacturing, operations, R&D, regulations, standards.

Use this slide as active practice, not just something to read off — cover the parenthetical classifications and have students attempt each one themselves before revealing the answer, since correctly classifying ambiguity is a skill, not trivia. “Will it be a boy or a girl?” is uncertain (the two outcomes are well-defined, we just don’t know which will occur) and also fuzzy in the book’s fuller discussion (interestingly, gender itself can carry fuzziness beyond a strict binary, though the classic textbook framing treats this mainly as an uncertainty example). “Produce a low-cost, high-quality product” packs in three kinds at once — it’s conflicting (low cost and high quality often pull against each other), fuzzy (“low” and “high” are both subject to interpretation), and can carry unknown information too (you may not yet know what cost or quality level is actually achievable). “Every fourth year is a leap year” is false information dressed up as a simple fact — students should recognize the actual Gregorian calendar rule excludes century years not divisible by 400 (1900 was not a leap year; 2000 was), so this statement, stated as flatly as it is, is simply incorrect. “Please make me a smooth cover for this phone” is a clean, pure fuzziness example, echoing the “smooth finish” example a couple of slides back. “Make sure we meet your quarterly goals” combines unknown information (you may not know precisely what’s required to hit the goal) with conflicting information (different stakeholders may have different, incompatible views on what “meeting the goals” actually requires).

The fragment generalizes the drill into a genuinely important, practical point: ambiguity of these various kinds is almost always present specifically in the upstream influences the architect has to engage with, and it’s useful to segment where it shows up roughly by corporate function — strategy, marketing, customers, manufacturing, operations, R&D, regulations, standards. This list previews the fuller “upstream influences” discussion two slides ahead, and directly sets up Chapter 10, which develops each of these upstream and downstream influence categories in much greater depth.

Box 9.2 · Principle of Ambiguity

“The best-laid schemes o’ mice an’ men / Gang aft agley [often go awry]” — Robert Burns

  • The early phase of a system design is characterized by great ambiguity — the architect must resolve it to produce (and continuously update) goals for the team
  • Development is possible only with the acceptance of uncertainty
  • No one designs or rigorously controls the upstream process — there should be no expectation of unambiguity
  • Uncertainty can create opportunities; it is not always bad
  • Uncertainties should be identified and prioritized so they can be managed

The Robert Burns quote — “the best-laid schemes o’ mice an’ men gang aft agley” (often go awry) — captures the emotional tenor of this whole principle: even the most carefully laid plans routinely go sideways, and the architect has to operate productively despite that, not pretend it away. Walk through the five bullets as a connected argument, not five independent facts.

The early phase of any system design is genuinely characterized by great ambiguity, and it’s the architect’s job to resolve enough of it to produce — and, importantly, continuously update — usable goals for the team; this isn’t a one-time act at project kickoff, it’s ongoing. Development is only possible at all with some acceptance of uncertainty — insisting on perfect certainty before starting is itself a decision to never start. The third bullet is worth emphasizing directly, since it corrects a common misconception: no one actually designs or rigorously controls the upstream process (recall the earlier point that nobody explicitly “designs” corporate strategy, marketing, or customer opinion the way an engineer designs a component) — so the architect should have no expectation of ever encountering a clean, unambiguous starting point. Waiting for one is a losing strategy.

The fourth bullet reframes uncertainty in a genuinely useful, more positive light: uncertainty can create opportunities, not just risk — it is not automatically a bad thing, since an unresolved situation sometimes contains room for a better solution than would have existed under a rigid, fully-specified brief. And the closing bullet gives the practical prescription: rather than trying to eliminate all uncertainties (impossible) or ignore them (dangerous), the architect should identify and prioritize them, so that the most consequential ones get managed deliberately — an echo of the Principle of Focus from Chapter 2, now applied specifically to managing ambiguity.

Deliverables of the Architect

Deliverables are different from tasks — they are the end result, not the procedure by which it’s achieved.

  • A clear, complete, consistent, attainable (80–90% confidence) set of goals, emphasizing functional goals
  • A description of the broader context, including the whole product context
  • A concept for the system, and a concept of operations including contingency/emergency operations
  • A functional description with at least two layers of decomposition
  • The decomposition of form to two levels of detail, and the structure at this level
  • Details of all external interfaces and a process for interface control
  • A notion of developmental cost, schedule, risk, and design/implementation plans

Make the deliverables/tasks distinction crisp before walking through the list, since it’s easy to conflate the two and the book is deliberate about separating them: a deliverable is the end result — the concrete artifact or outcome the architect must produce — while a task is the procedure by which that result gets achieved. This slide lists deliverables specifically, not a to-do list of activities.

Walk through each deliverable, connecting it back to material students have already seen: a clear, complete, consistent, and attainable set of goals — the book even gives a specific confidence target, 80–90%, and specifically emphasizes functional goals, echoing the form/function distinction from Chapter 2. A description of the broader context, including the whole product context — recall “context” precisely from Chapter 2’s Task 2, now applied at the level of an entire product rather than a single entity. A concept for the system (Chapter 7’s vocabulary) and a concept of operations, explicitly including contingency and emergency operations — not just the nominal, everything-goes-right case. A functional description with at least two layers of decomposition — directly recalling the “2 Down, 1 Up” and decomposition-depth guidance that Chapter 13 will formalize. The decomposition of form to two levels of detail, plus the structure at that level — the formal-domain counterpart to the previous bullet. Details of all external interfaces, plus an actual process for controlling those interfaces going forward — not just documenting them once. And finally, a notion of developmental cost, schedule, and risk, plus design and implementation plans.

It’s worth noting for students, since the book makes this connection explicit, that Table 8.1 (from the previous chapter, on developing Level 1 and Level 2 architectures) leads to the development of all of these deliverables except the last one, which is instead a summary of the project to be undertaken. And flag one genuine, ongoing challenge the book raises here: representing these deliverables in a form the rest of the organization can easily absorb and use is itself part of the architect’s job — semantically precise diagramming languages like OPM (or SysML) don’t always fit naturally with an existing organization’s process or culture, so part of being an effective architect is finding ways to communicate this information that actually land with the audience in front of you.

Section 9.2 Summary

  • The role of the architect includes reducing ambiguity, employing creativity, and managing complexity
  • Architecture sits astride upstream activities (before) and downstream activities (after) — ambiguity arises from both
  • In practice, ambiguity is a combination of fuzzy, uncertain, missing, conflicting, and incorrect information
  • The principal job of the architect at the interface to the upstream (the “fuzzy front end”) is to drive as much of this ambiguity from the system as possible

Use this as a checkpoint before moving into Section 9.3. By this point students should be able to recite the three roles from memory, and should understand why architecture is described as sitting “astride” both upstream and downstream activities — literally straddling the boundary, with genuine ambiguity flowing in from both directions (upstream: unclear strategy, fuzzy customer needs; downstream: unresolved manufacturing and operational questions). They should also be comfortable with the precise taxonomy of ambiguity built up over the last several slides: fuzziness and uncertainty as the two core ideas, plus missing, conflicting, and incorrect (false) information as the common practical manifestations, captured by the (X, Y, Z) shorthand.

The closing bullet restates the section’s thesis one more time, introducing a memorable piece of vocabulary worth having students retain: the interface to the upstream is sometimes called the “fuzzy front end” of development (a term the book attributes to Zhang and Doll), and the architect’s principal job right at that interface is to drive as much ambiguity out of the system as possible — recognizing, from Box 9.2, that “as much as possible” will never mean “all of it.”

9.3 The Product Development Process

What Is a PDP?

Nearly every large firm has a product development process (PDP) — capturing methodology, terminology, phases, milestones, and tasks/outputs.

  • A principal advantage: it’s a tool for reducing ambiguity by defining tasks and responsibilities
  • But it’s easy to be lulled into thinking the PDP resolves upstream ambiguity automatically — this is only partially true. A PDP can do the architect a disservice if it presumes more certainty than is actually present
  • We seek a PDP that is “birth to death” and “lust to dust” — capturing everything from first envisioning a product to retiring the last instance

Introduce the chapter’s second major section by defining the object of study precisely: a product development process, or PDP, is the internal methodology nearly every large firm maintains, capturing terminology, phases, milestones, and expected tasks and outputs for how products get developed. Frame why this matters for an architect specifically: a PDP’s principal advantage is that it’s a genuine tool for reducing ambiguity, by defining clear tasks and responsibilities — exactly the architect’s role from Section 9.2, now partially automated by organizational process.

But issue the caution the book is explicit about, since it’s an easy trap: it’s tempting to be lulled into thinking a well-defined PDP resolves upstream ambiguity automatically, just by virtue of existing — this is only partially true. A PDP can actually do the architect a disservice if it presumes more certainty than genuinely exists at that stage — a rigid-looking process with clean phase gates can create a false sense that strategy, market, customer needs, and technology are all well-understood, when in fact they’re still deeply ambiguous underneath the process’s veneer of structure.

Close with the ambitious scope the book wants a PDP to actually cover, using its own memorable phrasing: “birth to death” and “lust to dust” — capturing the entire arc from the very first envisioning of a product (before it’s even approved) all the way through to the retirement of its very last instance in the field. Most real PDPs, worth noting, fall well short of this full scope — a point the upcoming case studies (NASA, Helicopter Inc., Camera Co., Agile) will illustrate concretely, each covering a different, partial slice of this full birth-to-death arc.

Figure 9.1 · NASA’s PDP

Includes some of the fuzzy front end (feasibility, approval, requirements) and includes operations — since NASA is usually the operator of its own products. Very traceability- and review-heavy, reflecting low-volume, high-cost, high-perceived-risk systems. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.1.

Walk through this as the first of four concrete, real-world PDP case studies, and set up the comparative frame the book uses across all four: for each one, ask what upstream activities it includes, what downstream activities it includes, and what that reveals about the organization’s actual priorities. NASA’s PDP, shown here across eight phases from feasibility assessment through operations, includes some of the fuzzy front end — feasibility, approval, and requirements definition are explicitly represented as early phases — and, notably, includes operations as an explicit final phase, since NASA is usually the actual long-term operator of its own products (unlike many commercial firms that hand off operations to a customer).

Point out what’s conspicuously not well captured: the upstream influences on NASA’s PDP are barely represented — where you might expect a rich market-analysis phase, NASA’s process notes only a single feasibility question. The book’s explanation is that NASA’s actual “market analysis” is really a blend of budget decisions, public value considerations, science goals, and industrial-base questions, rather than anything resembling commercial market research — and NASA’s process proceeds notionally in a straight line from feasibility to operations, without an explicit, separate technology-development process shown.

The heavy emphasis on stage-gates (SRR, PDR, CDR, and so on — the diagram’s control-gate labels across the top) and formal review reflects the realities of NASA’s stakeholders and products: predominantly low-volume, extremely expensive space systems (think science satellites, the International Space Station, the Space Shuttle) that cannot be tested in their actual operating environment before real operations begin, and that carry an unusually high perceived cost of public failure — especially for human spaceflight. This traceability- and review-heavy character has been both praised (it enabled the successful, celebrated repair of the flawed Hubble Space Telescope mirror) and criticized (the Space Shuttle Columbia accident investigation board raised serious questions about whether this same process adequately surfaced known risks in time).

Figure 9.2 · Helicopter Inc.’s PDP

Compared to NASA, iteration loops stand out — even in aerospace, iteration is inevitable and not at odds with a stage-gated process. The process centers on FAA certification, itself an instrument of the more important process: sales. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.2.

Move to the second case study, whose firm name the book deliberately masks as “Helicopter Inc.” — a company that builds helicopters for both military and civilian applications and derives most of its revenue from government contracts, competing in a heavily regulated market. Compared to NASA’s relatively clean, linear-looking process, point out what visually stands out first in this diagram: explicit iteration loops (visible as the dashed feedback arrows looping back from later stages to earlier ones, around detail design, fabrication/code, and system testing). This is a genuinely important observation worth stating directly to students: even in a NASA-like aerospace development environment, iteration is inevitable in practice, and it is not fundamentally at odds with having an overall stage-gated process — the two can, and do, coexist.

The process’s real center of gravity, worth pointing out explicitly, is FAA certification (marked in the diagram) — but push students to look one level deeper, the way the book does: certification itself isn’t the ultimate goal, it’s an instrument of the more important underlying process, which is sales. A helicopter cannot legally be sold or operated without certification, so the entire development process is structured around achieving it, even though certification per se creates no direct customer value on its own.

One more subtlety worth raising, since the book poses it as an open, genuinely interesting question: this diagram includes product feedback (implied to come from customers) but does not explicitly represent customers “up front,” at the start of the process. Ask students directly — does Helicopter Inc. conceive products first and then go test them in the market, or does it gather customer needs and development grants first and design in response? Based on this diagram alone, all we can really determine is that customer input is present somewhere, implicitly folded into the mission statement — its exact role and timing simply aren’t made visible in the process as drawn.

Figure 9.3 · Camera Co.’s PDP

  • Camera Co.’s PDP explicitly shows upstream processes: strategy, voice of the customer, and R&D — all part of “conceiving”
  • Manufacturing process development precedes product development — Camera Co. is a process-driven industry
  • Raises a question: does the architect’s role in reducing manufacturing ambiguity matter more than innovation, given a stable customer base?

Move to the third case study, another masked company name — “Camera Co.,” which sells camera film and cameras for both amateur and professional applications. Contrast this PDP directly against the previous two: Camera Co.’s process explicitly shows upstream activities — business strategy, voice of the customer, and R&D — all folded into “conceiving,” rather than leaving them implicit or under-represented the way NASA’s and Helicopter Inc.’s diagrams largely did.

The genuinely distinctive feature of this PDP, worth spending real time on: manufacturing process development actually precedes product development in the diagram’s flow — the opposite ordering from what students might assume by default. The explanation is that Camera Co. operates in a fundamentally process-driven industry: how something can be manufactured shapes what gets designed, rather than the more familiar pattern of designing first and then figuring out manufacturing afterward.

Use the discussion questions the book poses here to get students actively reasoning, rather than just absorbing facts: although Camera Co. is often thought of as an R&D-driven company (film and camera technology obviously involves significant R&D), notice that in this diagram, voice of the customer notionally precedes R&D and the technology stream. Is this an accurate representation of how the process actually works, or is it a somewhat aspirational diagram, intended to influence and reorient a company that’s traditionally been more technology-focused, toward being more customer-driven? And connect this to the level of market competition Camera Co. faces — distributed and diverse, unlike NASA’s essentially unique-customer government relationship, or Helicopter Inc.’s concentrated, certification-gated market. The closing, genuinely open question worth posing to the room: given Camera Co.’s stable, loyal customer base, is the architect’s role in reducing manufacturing-quality ambiguity actually more valuable here than driving product innovation? There’s no single correct answer — the point is to get students reasoning about how a PDP’s actual structure reveals what an organization has decided matters most.

Figure 9.4 · An Agile PDP

Agile emphasizes iterative, incremental development — collaborative teams evolve requirements and solutions evolutionarily. Originated in rapid prototyping of software for small/medium applications; since extended (with mixed success) to capital-intensive, longer-lifecycle industries. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.4.

This is the fourth and final case study, and it’s worth noting explicitly why it’s included alongside the other three, more traditional-looking PDPs: Agile represents a genuinely different philosophical approach, not just a different industry’s flavor of the same basic stage-gate idea. Define Agile precisely: it emphasizes iterative and incremental development, where collaborative teams evolve both requirements and solutions together in a somewhat evolutionary manner, rather than fully specifying requirements up front and then executing against them linearly.

Give the honest history: Agile’s origins are in the rapid prototyping of software code, specifically for small- and medium-sized applications, where the cost of iterating quickly is genuinely low. It emphasizes close interactions among individuals actually building working code, over heavier upfront documentation, negotiation, and planning (visible in the diagram’s overlapping “developer track” and “interaction designer track” cycles, iterating through design, implementation, and testing repeatedly).

Give students the honest caveat the book raises, worth taking seriously rather than treating Agile as a universal solvent: beyond pure software, Agile has been used — with genuinely mixed success — as something of a by-word for “shaking up” development processes even in far more capital-intensive, longer-lifecycle industries, where the underlying assumptions that made Agile work well for small-scale software prototyping (cheap iteration, low cost of change) may simply not hold. To be an educated consumer of PDPs, as the book puts it, students should think critically about in what contexts Agile is actually appropriate and useful, rather than assuming its popularity in software automatically transfers to, say, aerospace hardware development.

Comparing the Four PDPs

Are differences between PDPs superficial, or substantial? Some observed differences:

  • Existence/number of phases and phase exit criteria
  • Existence/number of design reviews, timing for committing capital
  • Requirements “enforcement,” degree of customer input/feedback/aftermarket integration
  • Degree of explicit/implicit iteration, amount of prototyping
  • Internal testing/validation effort, importance of traceability, timing of supplier involvement

Tip

Many factors could drive real differences: hardware vs. software, existing vs. new product, standalone vs. platform, production volume, capital intensity, technology push vs. market pull.

Having walked through NASA, Helicopter Inc., Camera Co., and Agile individually, now step back and synthesize across all four — this is exactly the analytical move the book wants students to practice, not just admire four separate diagrams. Pose the central question directly, since it’s the whole point of this comparative exercise: are the differences we’ve just seen among these four PDPs superficial, or are they substantial, reflecting genuinely different underlying needs? List the observed differences concretely: existence and number of phases, and how rigorously phase-exit criteria are enforced; existence and number of formal design reviews, and when capital gets committed; how strictly requirements are “enforced” once set, and how much customer input, feedback, and aftermarket integration factor in; how much iteration is explicit versus merely implicit, and how much prototyping occurs; and how much internal testing and validation effort goes in, how important formal traceability is, and when suppliers get involved.

The fragment then raises the deeper diagnostic question, worth spending real class time on: many factors could plausibly drive genuine, substantive differences between PDPs — hardware versus software, an existing product versus an entirely new one, a standalone product versus a shared platform, production volume, capital intensity, and technology-push versus market-pull dynamics. The book’s own conclusion, which the very next slide builds toward, is that despite all these surface-level differences, there’s actually an enormous amount of common underlying structure once you look past the superficial variation — which is exactly what motivates constructing a single Generic PDP that all four of these examples are really instances of.

Figure 9.5 · The Generic PDP

Four common activity groups: conceiving, designing, implementing, operating — a checklist, not a stage-gate process. The architect’s primary domain sits in “conceive,” but must move relevant downstream information back into the architecting phase. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.5.

This is the payoff of the comparative exercise from the previous slide, and it’s worth spending real time here since this diagram anchors the rest of the chapter. Despite the real differences among NASA, Helicopter Inc., Camera Co., and Agile, engineers and architects moving past those superficial distinctions find a large body of genuinely common effective practice — codified here as the Generic PDP, organized into four activity groups: conceiving, designing, implementing, and operating.

Make sure students grasp the crucial framing choice, stated explicitly at the top of the slide and worth repeating: this diagram represents activities, not a sequence of stage-gates. It should specifically not be read as “first you conceive, then you design, then you implement, then you operate, in strict linear order” — that would be exactly the flawed linear representation the next slide criticizes directly.

Walk through what falls into each of the four groups: conceiving covers business strategy, functional strategies, customer needs, segments, competitors, technology, regulation, scope and plan, mission, and conceptual design — largely determined by market needs and technology availability. Designing covers preliminary and detailed design, requirements definition and elaboration, model development, requirements flow-down, detail decomposition, interface control — converting the concept into the information object that fully defines what is to be implemented. Implementing covers sourcing, element creation, integration, testing, positioning, product/system implementation, and certification — converting the design into reality, and deliberately including software coding, hardware manufacturing, and systems integration all together under one umbrella term. Operating covers sales, distribution, logistics, customer support, maintenance, repair, overhaul, upgrades, product improvement, platform expansion, and ultimately retirement — the actual operation of the real system to deliver value over its whole life.

Point out something visually important on the diagram: the shaded band labeled “Primary Domain of the Architect” sits specifically under conceiving — but there’s also an explicit arrow running right-to-left labeled “Downstream influences,” feeding back into that primary domain. This captures a genuinely important role the architect must play: moving relevant downstream information (constraints from design, implementation, and operations) back into the architecting phase, rather than architecting in isolation and only discovering downstream problems later. This reverse-information-flow idea gets developed much more richly in Chapter 10.

Linear Representations Are Flawed

Studies have shown that linear representations of the design process are deeply flawed — they fail to represent iterations and feedback, presume a linear flow of time and effort across stages, and can mask a design’s immaturity when gate criteria are compromised.

  • The Generic PDP is a checklist of activities that appear in complete PDPs, not a mandated sequence
  • “Implementing” deliberately includes coding software, manufacturing hardware, and integrating into systems
  • “Operating” includes support and release of future product instances — ending in retirement

Reinforce, with citation-backed weight, exactly why the Generic PDP was deliberately drawn the way it was — the callout states this plainly and it’s worth treating as a genuine research finding, not just editorial opinion: studies have shown that linear representations of the design process are deeply flawed. Unpack the three specific failure modes: linear diagrams fail to represent iterations and feedback (exactly the loops we saw explicitly called out in Helicopter Inc.’s PDP); they presume a linear flow of time and effort across stages, when real projects rarely proceed that cleanly; and — this is the most operationally dangerous failure mode, worth emphasizing — they can actually mask a design’s immaturity when gate criteria get compromised, because a project can nominally “pass” a phase gate on paper even when the underlying work isn’t genuinely ready, and a purely linear diagram gives no visual signal that this has happened.

Restate the Generic PDP’s actual intended use, since it’s easy to slip back into reading it as a sequence despite the earlier framing: it’s meant to function as a checklist of the activities that appear in complete PDPs, loosely bundled into the four groups — not a mandated order in which those activities must occur. Reinforce the deliberate word choices in two of the four groups, since they carry real intent: “implementing” is chosen specifically to include the coding of software, the manufacturing of hardware goods, and the integration of components into systems, all under one unified term, rather than treating software and hardware development as fundamentally different activities. “Operating” is chosen to include the ongoing support of existing product instances and the release of future ones, with the full arc ending, eventually, in retirement — reinforcing the “lust to dust” ambition from a few slides back.

Section 9.3.1 Summary

  • Nearly every large firm has an internal PDP — a tool for reducing ambiguity, but one that can mislead if it presumes more certainty than is actually present
  • Comparing PDPs across NASA, Helicopter Inc., Camera Co., and Agile reveals many superficial differences, but real underlying similarities
  • The Generic PDP codifies this common practice as four activity groups: conceive, design, implement, operate — deliberately not a stage-gate process

Use this as a checkpoint before the chapter’s final major analytical move, the Global PDP. By now students should be comfortable with the idea that a PDP is a genuine tool for reducing ambiguity, but a double-edged one — useful precisely because it defines tasks and responsibilities, dangerous precisely because it can create false confidence about how settled the upstream situation actually is. They should be able to name all four real-world case studies and, more importantly, articulate what distinguished each one’s actual priorities (NASA’s traceability and review-heaviness given low-volume, high-cost, high-perceived-risk systems; Helicopter Inc.’s iteration loops and FAA-certification-as-instrument-of-sales; Camera Co.’s manufacturing-before-product-development ordering; Agile’s iterative, incremental, low-cost-of-change philosophy). And they should hold onto the Generic PDP itself — conceive, design, implement, operate — as the unifying, non-sequential checklist underneath all four. This sets up the chapter’s final section, which builds an even more elaborate comparative framework: the Global PDP.

9.3.2 The Global PDP

Building the Global PDP

  • To further support comparison across PDPs, we construct an extended baseline: the Global PDP, in three nested views
  • Level 1 centers on the architecture of the product — function and form — as a reflection of product goals, themselves set to respond to market/stakeholder needs
  • The architect answers the canonical W Questions: why, what, how, where, when, who, how much

Tip

Quo is the shared Indo-European root of these words across languages — French qui/quoi/quand, Hindi kab/kya/kyon, and the seven circumstances of the ancient Greek rhetorician Hermagoras.

Introduce the chapter’s most ambitious analytical construct: the Global PDP, built specifically to support deeper comparison across PDPs than the Generic PDP alone allows, organized as three progressively broader nested views (this slide covers Level 1; the following two slides cover Levels 2 and 3). Level 1 centers directly on the architecture of the product itself — its function and form, exactly the vocabulary from Chapters 2, 4, and 5 — framed as a reflection of the product’s goals, which are themselves set in response to market and stakeholder needs. This is a nice moment to point out how tightly this integrates everything from Part 2 of the course: architecture (form + function) sits at the very center of this framework, precisely because it’s the thing all the surrounding activities ultimately exist to produce.

Introduce the “W Questions” as the organizing structure the architect uses to interrogate any product/system under development: why, what, how, where, when, who, and how much. These aren’t arbitrary — the fragment gives genuinely fascinating etymological grounding, worth sharing as color that makes the idea memorable: all of these English question words share a common linguistic ancestor, the Indo-European root quo — visible directly in French (qui, quoi, quand, combien, ou, comment, pourquoi), and even in more distantly related languages like Hindi (kab, kya, kyon, kitne, kisne, kahan, all sharing the “k” sound). The book also connects this to the ancient Greek rhetorician Hermagoras, who identified seven circumstances at the heart of any issue: quis, quid, quando, ubi, cur, quem ad modum, and quibus adminiculis — essentially who, what, when, where, why, how, and by what means, the same seven-question structure, independently arrived at in classical rhetoric roughly two thousand years ago. The implication worth stating plainly: an architect who wants a genuinely holistic view of product development needs to understand all of these attributes well — this ancient, cross-linguistic recurrence of the same basic question set is a strong signal that it captures something fundamental about how humans naturally interrogate any situation.

Figure 9.6 · Level 1 — The Holistic Framework

Architecture — form and function — sits at the center of the framework. Interactions are two-directional; flow from left to right is not implied. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.6.

Walk through this diagram as the direct visual realization of the previous slide’s W Questions. Trace across the seven columns: global why corresponds to need/opportunity — the reason the system is built at all; global what corresponds to goals/performance — what the system accomplishes; global how corresponds to function/interaction — how the system acts; global where corresponds to form/structure — what the elements of the system are (form answers “where” specifically because form exists and therefore has location, a nice, precise justification worth pointing out); global when corresponds to behavior/dynamics — when things occur; global who corresponds to operator/user — the people involved; and global how much corresponds to cost/expense.

Emphasize the two callouts in the caption, since they head off a natural but incorrect reading of the diagram: interactions here are explicitly two-directional (shown by the double-headed arrows in the actual figure), and the apparent left-to-right ordering of the columns does not imply that information or work flows strictly in that direction — this is the same non-sequential, checklist-like framing principle from the Generic PDP, now applied to this richer seven-question structure. And highlight the diagram’s visual emphasis directly: architecture — consisting primarily of form (global where) and function (global how) — is placed at the literal center of the framework, both geometrically and conceptually, reinforcing that everything else in this holistic view (need, goals, behavior, users, cost) surrounds and relates back to the architecture itself.

Figure 9.7 · Level 2 — Design and Implementation in Parallel

Moving up a layer: the design process and implementation process each get their own instantiation of the W Questions. The design process has its own form too — notably design tools, which shape and constrain the system form that’s achievable. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.7.

Move to Level 2 of the Global PDP, and make the key structural insight explicit: rather than replacing Level 1, this view adds two more full applications of the same seven W Questions — one for the design process itself, and one for the implementation process itself — each running in parallel alongside the “conceiving the product and operations” row from Level 1 (visible in the figure as three horizontal rows, bracketed on the right as “Design process,” “Conceiving the product and operations,” and “Implement process”).

Draw out the genuinely subtle and important point the book makes here: the design process is itself a system, with its own form — most notably, design tools. A drafts-person equipped with a straightedge will produce fundamentally different renderings, and therefore different achievable system forms, than one equipped with a French curve; the tools used to design the system meaningfully shape and constrain what system form is even achievable. This is a genuinely non-obvious insight worth pausing on: the architecture of the design process itself has downstream consequences for the architecture of the product being designed.

Use the book’s concrete automotive example to make the “design costs influencing architecture” point land: many automakers maintain a non-recurring engineering (NRE) design-cost policy for platform vehicles, whereby any variants launched in the same year and produced at the same volume must split their NRE design costs evenly. This policy is not explicitly part of any technical evaluation of a concept, and it wouldn’t show up in a stage-gate review — but it’s clearly part of the Generic (and Global) PDP, because it has a significant, real impact on whether a given vehicle variant can economically deliver value. Similarly, the implementation process has its own capabilities and limitations that the architect must factor directly into the product’s architecture — existing manufacturing or coding capability constrains what can actually be built, regardless of what’s designed on paper.

Close by mentioning the practical use the book puts this figure to: any PDP, or any new design method being considered, can be critically evaluated by checking it against the 21 questions implicit in this diagram (three rows times seven W Questions). Few methods will actually answer all 21 — the real value of the exercise is understanding which specific areas a given framework is strongest in. The book’s own example: the Lean movement began from studies of the Toyota Production System, evolving into principles for improving manufacturing cost and quality — Lean later moved upstream to ask how design decisions could enable leaner manufacturing, but has also, more controversially, been applied directly to the design process itself, an application the book notes is now far removed from Lean’s original intent.

Figure 9.8 · Level 3 — The Enterprise Context

The broadest nested view: the firm’s PDP inside the enterprise boundary (R&D and corporate functions mostly upstream; PR, sales, distribution mostly downstream), plus external actors and attributes — capital, competition, regulation — that affect the enterprise. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.8.

Move to the third and broadest nested view, which places the entire PDP (conceive, design, implement, operate, shown here as overlapping ellipses, with architecture at the center where they intersect) inside a larger enterprise boundary. Walk through what sits inside that enterprise boundary: several firm functions are shown explicitly, positioned according to whether they’re predominantly upstream or downstream of the PDP itself. R&D and corporate functions sit primarily upstream, feeding into conceiving. Public relations, sales, and distribution sit primarily downstream, connected to operating. And notice some functions — human skills, information systems, engineering tools — are shown permeating the whole diagram, since they support every phase roughly equally, while others, like corporate strategy, may genuinely play a role in multiple places at once, not cleanly upstream or downstream alone.

Then point out what’s drawn entirely outside the enterprise boundary: an explicitly incomplete list of external actors and attributes that affect the whole enterprise regardless of its internal process — things like the availability of capital in the broader market, the intensity of competition, and the social impact of the firm’s activities. On the diagram itself, these appear as inputs and outputs crossing the enterprise boundary on both sides: global economy, shareholder goals, political inputs, human resources, regulations, customer needs, competition, technology, services, and raw material all flow in on the left; economic impact, profits, political impact, social impact, corporate image, intellectual property, goods, services, technology, and even pollution flow out on the right.

State the purpose of this broadest view plainly, since it’s easy to read this diagram as just “more detail for its own sake”: the point is to remind the architect that understanding the contents of the PDP alone is not necessarily sufficient — the architect must also be aware of other issues that influence the firm and its broader context, which is exactly the theme the next slide (Box 9.3) develops directly.

Box 9.3 · Principle of the Stress of Modern Practice

“Things which matter most must never be at the mercy of things which matter least.” — Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

Modern product development — with concurrency, distributed teams, and early supplier engagement — places even more emphasis on having a good architecture:

  • Accelerating development via concurrency increases the importance of initial conceptual decisions
  • Empowering distributed/non-collocated teams increases the importance of well-coordinated, visible high-level design guidance
  • Involving suppliers early increases the importance of an architectural concept and baseline — suppliers can define or defy decomposition

Close out the Global PDP discussion with this forward-looking principle, whose Goethe quote — “things which matter most must never be at the mercy of things which matter least” — is a nice, quotable way of expressing the underlying worry: modern development pressures (speed, distribution, supplier involvement) can easily let less-important operational details crowd out the truly consequential early architectural decisions, unless the organization actively guards against it.

Walk through why each of the three modern trends listed specifically raises the stakes on getting the architecture right, rather than lowering them (as a naive reading might assume, given that these trends are usually framed as efficiency improvements). Accelerating development through concurrency — running multiple activities in parallel rather than sequentially — means initial conceptual decisions get “locked in” faster and with less opportunity for course-correction downstream, so getting them right upfront matters even more than in a slower, more sequential process. Empowering distributed or non-collocated teams (driving decision-making down to the lowest appropriate level, and having teams that aren’t physically together) means that well-coordinated, clearly visible high-level design guidance becomes more important, not less — without it, physically separated teams working somewhat autonomously can easily drift out of alignment with each other. And involving suppliers early in the process means having a genuine architectural concept and baseline established becomes more important still, because — as stated directly on the slide — suppliers themselves can either define or defy the intended decomposition of the system, depending on how clearly that decomposition has been communicated to them before they start contributing their own design work.

The overarching message worth leaving students with: none of these three modern practices (concurrency, distributed teams, early supplier engagement) are inherently bad — they’re often genuinely valuable for speed and flexibility — but each one specifically increases the cost of not having done the architecting work well, which is exactly why this principle belongs at the close of a chapter about the architect’s role.

  • The Global PDP is a nested framework used to compare different PDPs on a common baseline, built around the canonical W Questions
  • It reveals the architect’s system boundary — largely, but not exclusively, the “conceive” activities
  • The purpose of the broadest view is to remind the architect: understanding the contents of the PDP is not sufficient — the firm and its context matter too

Use this as the closing checkpoint for the whole product-development-process discussion. Students should now be able to describe all three nested levels of the Global PDP in order — the product’s own architecture (form and function, at the center), the design and implementation processes running in parallel alongside it (each with its own W Questions and its own form, notably design tools), and the whole enterprise context surrounding all of it (upstream and downstream firm functions, plus external actors like capital markets, competition, and regulation). They should be comfortable that this whole apparatus was built specifically to give a common baseline for comparing genuinely different PDPs like NASA’s, Helicopter Inc.’s, Camera Co.’s, and Agile’s fairly, rather than superficially.

And the closing bullet restates the chapter’s broadest lesson: understanding the contents of a PDP alone is necessary but not sufficient — the firm and its surrounding context genuinely matter too, which is exactly the transition into the chapter’s closing case study on civil architecture, a domain where “context” (as we’re about to see) is treated as an almost sacred design consideration.

Box 9.4 — Case Study: Civil Architecture and System Architecture

Where “Architect” Comes From

By Steve Imrich, Architect and Principal at Cambridge Seven Associates

  • Civil architecture has an interpretive-interactive-performance aspect that differs from most industrial/product design — buildings inherit complex cultural and symbolic qualities affecting space, materials, and user experience
  • Ideally, the “special problems” of a building inform its design — as with helicopters, sailboats, or computers, concept/design/engineering can transcend mere style
  • Because buildings are only seen/experienced as part of their site, their special problems always relate to a wider context: time, culture, infrastructure, human behavior, environment, re-interpreted use
  • Buildings are rarely mocked up beforehand — a large element of risk, usually associated with the arts

Introduce this closing case study by noting its genuinely unusual format: it’s a guest contribution, written by a practicing civil architect (Steve Imrich, of Cambridge Seven Associates), included specifically because the word “architect” itself, as used throughout this entire course, is borrowed directly from civil/building architecture — so it’s worth hearing directly from that original field about what the word actually means there, and what genuinely transfers (and doesn’t) to systems.

Frame the central comparative question this whole case study is built around, and pose it to students directly: when you compare civil architecture with system architecture, what do the two fields actually share? The first distinguishing feature Imrich identifies: civil architecture carries an interpretive-interactive-performance aspect that’s more pronounced than in most industrial or product design — buildings, more than most other kinds of products, inherit complex cultural and symbolic qualities that genuinely affect space planning, material choices, and the user’s experience, independent of pure function.

Introduce the phrase “special problems,” which recurs through this whole case study: ideally, a building’s special problems — the particular, non-generic challenges specific to that site, that client, that cultural moment — should genuinely inform its design. Give the parallel Imrich draws to other fields, worth pausing on since it connects directly back to earlier course material: certain devices, like helicopters, sailboats, and computers, are good examples of successfully resolving their own special problems, where concept, design, and engineering together introduce an elegance, simplicity, and beauty that transcends mere surface style — this is a nice callback to the Principle of Elegance students will see formalized later in Chapter 13.

Two further distinguishing features worth drawing out: because buildings can only ever be seen and experienced as part of their specific site, their special problems always relate to a genuinely wide context — time, culture, existing infrastructure, human behavior, the local environment, and how the building’s use gets re-interpreted over time by people who weren’t part of its original design. And, strikingly different from most engineered systems: buildings are not normally mocked up or prototyped beforehand the way a physical product might be — which introduces a large element of genuine risk in determining whether a design will actually succeed, a kind of risk the book explicitly says is generally associated more with the arts than with conventional engineering.

Figure 9.9 · Concept in Civil Architecture

This is a good moment to introduce, in preview, the six “design process with attitude” ideas the next slide formalizes — context, content, concept, circuitry, character, and magic — since concept is explicitly called out here as one of the two the guest author chooses to focus on most deeply (the other being magic, covered a few slides later). Read the book’s own definition carefully, since it’s precisely worded: the concept for a building project is the departure point for organization and experience — the unifying idea by which all design decisions get evaluated. Whether it’s a cluster of conical stone roofs responding to local materials and climate, or a Paris museum that makes its own mechanical guts into a defining visual statement, in both cases a single strong organizing idea is what lets performance (what the building needs to do) and form (what it physically is) come together coherently — exactly the same underlying pattern as the process/operand and instrument/process/operand ideas from Chapter 2, now expressed in the specific vocabulary of building design.

Six Ideas in “Design Process with Attitude”

  • Context — the built environment, cultural norms, and history of the organizations and people the building serves
  • Content — the building’s programmatic requirements and functional performance
  • Concept — the unifying idea by which all design decisions are evaluated; concept generation succeeds when it integrates and balances the nature of content and context
  • Circuitry — the connective tissue between functional components; more than a path from A to B, it creates interest and anticipation
  • Character — a building’s material, site, and sense of life beyond the aggregation of parts
  • Magic — the largely intangible quality defined by the user’s experience; magic fosters delight and joyful appreciation of the unexpected

Walk through the guest author’s own vocabulary for a genuinely distinctive way of approaching design, contrasted explicitly against a conventional product-development process — hence the deliberately provocative label “design process with attitude.” Define each of the six ideas in turn, using the book’s own descriptions closely, since the language itself is doing real conceptual work here. Context is the wider setting a building exists within — drawing on adjacent existing buildings, cultural norms and associations about the built environment, environmental conditions, and the history and motivations of the organizations and people the building is meant to serve. Content is what the building is actually for — both its programmatic requirements (the concrete functions it needs to support) and its functional performance, echoing the function vocabulary from Chapter 5 but at a building’s scale.

Concept, already introduced on the previous slide, gets its fullest statement here: it’s the unifying idea by which all design decisions are evaluated, and — this is the key criterion for a successful concept, worth emphasizing — concept generation succeeds specifically when it integrates and balances the nature of content and context together, not by satisfying one at the expense of the other. Circuitry is a wonderfully specific idea worth dwelling on: it’s the connective tissue between a building’s functional components — but explicitly framed as more than merely a path for getting from point A to point B; done well, circuitry itself creates interest and anticipation as someone moves through a space, actively shaping how boundaries, thresholds, and spaces get dynamically and progressively revealed to a person walking through them.

Character is a building’s material choices, its site, and a sense of life that goes beyond the mere aggregation of its individual parts — closely related to the “elegance” and “emergence” themes from earlier in the course, now applied specifically to how a building feels over time as people form associations with its use and its visual presence. And magic — introduced here, developed fully over the next several slides — is a largely intangible quality, defined primarily by the user’s actual experience of a building, rather than by the designer’s or owner’s original intent; magic fosters an inherent sense of delight and joyful appreciation of the unexpected. This whole six-part framework is worth presenting to students explicitly as a genuinely different design vocabulary from the process/operand, function/form language used throughout the rest of the course — useful precisely because it captures dimensions (character, magic) that a purely functional analysis tends to leave out entirely.

Figure 9.10 · Magic in Civil Architecture

Left: Sydney Opera House (a). Right: Golden Gate Bridge (b). Qualities of “Magic” allow structures to become memorable and iconic to a culture. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.10. Photos: (a) Wim Wiskerke/Alamy, (b) EvanTravels/Fotolia.

Use these two globally iconic structures — the Sydney Opera House and the Golden Gate Bridge — as maximally recognizable examples of “magic” in the sense just defined. Yet both have transcended pure function to become instantly recognizable, deeply memorable symbols of their entire cities and, arguably, their countries — precisely the “magic” quality described on the previous slide: an intangible quality defined by how people experience the structure, fostering delight and a joyful appreciation of something unexpected, that becomes iconic to an entire culture rather than staying merely functional.

This is a good moment to ask students directly for their own examples — buildings, bridges, or even everyday products they personally consider to have this quality — and to push them to articulate why, using the vocabulary just introduced (context, content, concept, circuitry, character, magic), rather than settling for a vague “it just looks cool.” The next slide develops the concept of magic even further, including a striking quote about surprise that’s worth setting up here as coming next.

On Magic

“Surprise, as all art and architecture, helps us contemplate. Life wears out our ability for surprise. Surprise is the beginning of a true vision of the world.” — Eladio Dieste

  • Magic is the emergence of elegance and character at the building level — the sleight of hand
  • It is difficult to define, but when something is magical, it’s immediately recognizable
  • Magic is the unusual circumstance that becomes normalized in good architecture — that moment of surprise that leaves a person in wonder and awe
  • Real magic makes architecture symbolic and iconic in a sustained manner — it reaches beyond the original intent, communicating viscerally with users

Unpack the Eladio Dieste quote carefully, since it makes a genuinely subtle claim about why surprise matters, beyond simply being a pleasant sensation: surprise helps us contemplate — it’s positioned as valuable precisely because ordinary life, through sheer repetition, wears out our capacity for surprise over time, so an architectural experience that genuinely surprises someone is doing something rare and valuable, described as “the beginning of a true vision of the world.”

Walk through the bullet points as a progressively deeper definition of magic. First: magic is the emergence of elegance and character specifically at the building level — deliberately using “emergence” in exactly the technical sense from Chapter 2 (recall: greater than the sum of the parts) — described evocatively here as “the sleight of hand.” Second, an epistemically interesting point worth dwelling on: magic is difficult to define in the abstract, yet when something genuinely is magical, people recognize it immediately and without hesitation — a nice parallel to how “you know it when you see it” properties work in many domains, including, arguably, elegance itself. Third, magic is specifically the unusual circumstance that somehow becomes normalized within good architecture — that particular moment of surprise that leaves a person in genuine wonder and awe, rather than confusion or discomfort. And fourth, the distinguishing mark of real, lasting magic (as opposed to a passing novelty) is that it makes a piece of architecture symbolic and iconic in a sustained way over time — it reaches beyond whatever the original designer’s intent may have been, and communicates viscerally, emotionally, with the people who actually use and experience it, independent of whether they know or care about that original design intent at all.

Figure 9.11 · Overlaying Performance and Poetry

A ballerina and a gymnast share athleticism, dynamic patterning, and nuance of expression — yet one is considered “art,” the other “athletic expertise.” An architect’s opportunity and responsibility: to overlay performance and poetry, and understand how those ingredients interact. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 9.11. Photos: (a) Cheese78/Fotolia, (b) Gerard Rancinan, Jean Guichard/Sygma/Corbis)

Close the civil-architecture case study with this evocative closing comparison, and take the time to unpack why it’s a genuinely apt final image for the whole discussion. A ballerina and a gymnast, side by side, actually share a great deal in common — both display genuine athleticism, dynamic patterning of movement through space, and real nuance of expression in how they move. And yet, culturally, one activity is squarely categorized as “art” while the other is categorized as “athletic expertise” — a distinction that has more to do with tradition and framing than with any objective difference in the underlying skill or beauty on display.

Draw the explicit parallel to architecture, since that’s the whole point of including this image at the end of a chapter that’s ostensibly about product development processes: an architect’s genuine opportunity — and, the book adds, responsibility — is to overlay performance (the ballerina/gymnast’s raw athletic capability, standing in for engineering function and structural performance) and poetry (the artistic, expressive quality, standing in for magic and character), and to genuinely understand how those two ingredients interact with and shape each other, rather than treating them as separable concerns that get addressed independently.

Use this as the bridge back to the rest of the course: this entire civil-architecture case study, and this closing image specifically, exists to remind students that even though most of this course focuses on precise, analyzable concepts — form, function, decomposition, interfaces, goals — the actual practice of architecting, in any domain, still has genuine room for something closer to art: elegance, character, and even magic, alongside rigorous engineering performance. The chapter summary immediately following brings the discussion back to the more analytical vocabulary the rest of the course will continue building on.

Chapter 9 Summary

  • The architect is not a generalist, but a specialist in resolving ambiguity, focusing creativity, and simplifying complexity
  • Ambiguity comes in many forms — fuzziness, uncertainty, and missing, conflicting, or incorrect information — and the architect must recognize and deal with each
  • The architect is responsible for producing specific deliverables, central to reducing ambiguity and bridging corporate functions to the design environment
  • Product development processes differ superficially across firms and sectors, but share deep common structure — codified here as the Generic and Global PDPs
  • Architects must discern which segments of a PDP are centrally linked to their role, and stay skilled at interpreting new product development initiatives

Tip

Reference: Crawley, E., Cameron, B., & Selva, D. (2016). System Architecture: Strategy and Product Development for Complex Systems. Pearson. Chapter 9.

Use this final summary to walk the chapter’s full arc one more time in order, tying each bullet back to a specific section. We opened by correcting a common misconception: the architect is not a generalist, but a specialist, with three specifically nameable roles — resolving ambiguity, focusing creativity, and simplifying complexity (the opening two slides). Section 9.2 then developed the first of these roles in genuine technical depth: ambiguity comes in precisely definable forms — fuzziness and uncertainty as the two core ideas, plus missing, conflicting, and incorrect information as the practical manifestations captured by the (X, Y, Z) shorthand — and recognizing which kind of ambiguity you’re facing is itself a real skill. That section also established the architect’s concrete deliverables — the tangible end results (goals, context, concept, functional and formal decomposition, interfaces, cost/schedule/risk plans) that reducing ambiguity is meant to produce, and that bridge corporate functions to the actual design environment.

Section 9.3 then examined product development processes as an organizational tool for managing this same ambiguity — comparing four genuinely different real-world PDPs (NASA, Helicopter Inc., Camera Co., Agile) and finding that their differences, while real, sit on top of a deep common structure, codified first as the four-activity-group Generic PDP, and then elaborated into the three-level, W-Questions-based Global PDP for richer comparison and for situating the architect’s role within the broader enterprise. And the closing civil-architecture case study reminded us that for all this analytical rigor, the practice of architecting still has genuine room for concept, character, and even magic.

The closing practical charge, worth stating directly to students as this chapter’s overall takeaway: architects must be able to discern which segments of any given PDP are centrally linked to their own role (versus segments that belong to other functions), and must stay skilled and current at interpreting whatever new product-development initiatives come along in their careers (Lean, Agile, Six Sigma, and whatever comes next) — evaluating each on its actual merits and appropriate context, rather than adopting or rejecting it wholesale based on fashion.
