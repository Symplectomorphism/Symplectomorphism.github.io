13.2 Understanding Complexity

What Is Complexity?

Box 13.1 · Definition: Complexity. Several simple measures: N1 = number of things, N2 = number of types of things, N3 = number of interfaces, N4 = number of types of interfaces.

\[C = N_1 + N_2 + N_3 + N_4\]

  • The most-quoted measure of complexity is simply N1: Microsoft cites servers in its data centers; Boeing cites parts (6 million) in the 747
  • Analogously, N3/N4 capture interfaces and interface types — a building’s electrical system is more complex if its outlets are of many types (US, UK, EU, AU…)
  • A variation: Boothroyd & Dewhurst’s formal complexity \(C = (N_1 \times N_2 \times N_3)^{1/3}\). Complexity is an absolute, quantifiable property once “measure” and “atomic level” are defined — regard it as a quantity to be managed, not an intractable outcome to be ignored
  • Box 13.1’s four counting measures — precise, recur all lecture: N1 = # things, N2 = # types of things, N3 = # interfaces, N4 = # types of interfaces
  • Most-quoted: N1 alone — Microsoft cites data-center servers, Boeing cites 6 million 747 parts — intuitive, easy to communicate externally, though crude alone
  • Why N3/N4 matter separately: two buildings, same # outlets (N1), but one more complex if outlets come in many incompatible types (US/UK/EU/AU plugs) — more interface types to design/stock/adapt for
  • Boothroyd & Dewhurst variation: geometric-mean-style formula, not a simple sum — worth knowing
  • → Key philosophical point the lecture depends on: fix what’s measured + atomic granularity → complexity becomes absolute, quantifiable, not a vague impression — a real design variable to track/manage, not something to throw up your hands at

Figure 13.1 · A Real (but Unwieldy) Complexity Measure

A vehicle manufacturer’s attempt to define complexity via part-sharing across a 13-level product hierarchy. The measure was not incorrect — but it was so cumbersome (the decomposition itself was never agreed upon) that it went unused. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 13.1.

  • Cautionary tale about measurement usability, not correctness
  • Vehicle manufacturer built a 13-level product hierarchy to define/track complexity via part-sharing across models/platforms — technically not wrong, captured something real
  • The problem (subtle, common failure mode): the decomposition underlying the measure was never agreed upon org-wide — different teams, different ideas of what belongs at which level → hierarchy contested → measure too cumbersome to maintain/interpret → went unused
  • → Lesson: a complexity measure is only as useful as the decomposition it’s built on — no org agreement, even a correct measure dies from disuse
  • Sets up the chapter’s tension: complexity can be measured in principle, but perceiving it is separate — next slide’s topic

Complex versus Complicated

  • Complexity is a negative-sounding property, yet complex systems (transportation infrastructure, networked communication) deliver far more value than simple counterparts (bicycles, letter mail)
  • Chapter 3 introduced complicated as a measure of our ability to perceive and comprehend complexity. Complicated things have high apparent complexity (complicatedness) — our human processor is more easily confused
  • The pyramids were structurally less complicated than the Taj Mahal — but we leave comparing their emergent aesthetics to the reader’s judgment and taste
  • Reconnect to Chapter 3’s vocabulary, now applied to the cost/benefit tradeoff of complexity
  • Dispel the misconception: complexity sounds negative in casual speech, yet complex systems (transportation infrastructure, networked comms) deliver far more value than simple counterparts (bicycle, hand-carried letter) — complexity is not inherently bad
  • Sharp distinction: “complicated” ≠ complex — it measures our human ability to perceive/comprehend complexity. High apparent complexity = overwhelms/confuses the “human processor,” regardless of underlying absolute complexity
  • Pyramid/Taj Mahal: structurally pyramids less complicated, same function (entombing royalty) — whether Taj Mahal’s added complicatedness is aesthetically worth it is taste, not something this chapter’s tools adjudicate
  • → Deliberate rhetorical move: leave the value judgment to the reader, keep the technical point about complicatedness clean

Box 13.2 · Principle of Apparent Complexity

  • Two epigraphs deliberately in tension: Mies van der Rohe’s “Less is more” vs. Philip Anderson’s “More is different” (1972 Science article on emergent complexity in physics) — simplicity as virtue vs. new phenomena only emerging at enough scale/complexity
  • Both true simultaneously: minimize apparent complexity where possible, but not at the cost of losing valuable emergent behavior
  • Core claim: modern systems’ complexity exceeds human comprehension, so decomposition/abstraction/hierarchy exist to keep apparent complexity within human range — they don’t reduce actual/absolute complexity, only make it digestible
  • → Warning the next slides build on: complexity can scale combinatorially with # features and # objects — techniques for simple systems don’t naively scale up; new tools are needed (rest of the chapter)

The pyramid is less complicated than the Taj Mahal, even though both perform the same function — entombing royalty. In civil architecture there are aesthetic reasons for “complicatedness.” Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 13.2.

  • Both structures: identical primary function (entombing royalty) — pyramid = simple geometric form, Taj Mahal = far more visually/structurally intricate
  • In civil architecture specifically: genuine aesthetic reasons to want apparent complexity — ornamentation, symmetry, visual richness are deliberate design goals, not simplification failures
  • → Ask students: does the same logic apply to a product they know (luxury car dashboard, jewelry)? Sometimes complicatedness is the point, not a flaw

Reducing Apparent Complexity

  • The word “organized” is fuzzy — it doesn’t reduce to a step-by-step process. We unpack it with Chapter 3’s tools: decomposition and abstraction
  • A cable can be treated as an instance of a class of entities sharing functionality; a bundle of cables tied together can be abstracted as a single bus, releasing the viewer from tracking each cable
  • Is Figure 13.4 at maximum organization? Not necessarily — a layered diagram could abstract out the cable-routing detail entirely, at the cost of “presuming” the cables are routed correctly

Tip

Low-level languages (assembly) are hard even for experienced programmers. High-level languages (JAVA, MATLAB) reduce apparent complexity — MATLAB inverts a matrix in one instruction. For some users this investment is merited; for others it costs performance or scalability.

  • Unpack “organized” mechanically — fuzzy word, no single step-by-step procedure; reach back to Chapter 3’s decomposition and abstraction
  • A cable = instance of a general “cable” class sharing functionality; a bundle of tied cables → abstracted as a single higher-level “bus,” releasing the viewer from tracking every strand
  • Is Figure 13.4 maximum organization? Not necessarily — could go a layer further and abstract out routing detail entirely, but that requires presuming cables are routed correctly. Always a tradeoff: strip apparent complexity vs. implicit trust over verification
  • Software-language example (fragment): assembly = hard even for experienced programmers, low reduction. Java/MATLAB reduce apparent complexity dramatically (MATLAB inverts a matrix in one instruction)
  • → But abstraction isn’t free or universally beneficial — worth it for some users, costs performance/scalability for others (max runtime performance, fine-grained memory control). Reducing apparent complexity is always a deliberate tradeoff, never an unambiguous win

Essential Complexity

  • Complexity is absolute, and the architect must invest in it to deliver more performance and functionality. This begs the question: is there a minimum complexity required to deliver a given function and performance?
  • Yes — we call this the essential complexity. Functionality drives essential complexity: describe the required functionality carefully, then choose a concept that produces low complexity
  • Gratuitous complexity is complexity added over and above the essential complexity for a given robust functionality — it should be avoided
  • Second major axis of complexity, distinct from “apparent” (human perception): essential complexity — about the underlying requirement itself
  • Complexity is absolute (N1–N4), architect must invest in it — natural question: is there a minimum complexity to deliver a given function at a given performance? Yes = essential complexity
  • Practical guidance: functionality drives essential complexity — describe required functionality precisely, choose a concept (Ch. 12) producing the lowest complexity that delivers it robustly
  • Gratuitous complexity = anything invested beyond essential for robust functionality — note “robust”: not cutting every corner to a fragile minimum, essential already accounts for robust operation
  • → Gratuitous complexity is genuinely wasted investment — avoid it wherever identified

Box 13.3 · Principle of Essential Complexity

  • Collection of quotations triangulating the same idea:
    • Joel Moses: “complexity is inherently neither good nor bad” — the chapter’s opening theme, now formalized
    • Einstein: “make everything as simple as possible, but not simpler” — essential complexity is the floor, don’t go below it
    • Occam’s Razor: “choose the simpler solution, all things being equal” — note the qualifier, doesn’t license cutting essential complexity
    • Emerson: highest simplicity of structure comes not from few elements, but from highest complexity tamed to its intersection with elegance
  • Core prescriptive content: essential complexity = minimum needed for robust functionality; gratuitous = anything added beyond that, avoid it
  • → Memorable aphorism: follow the road of simplicity to where it crosses the road of full functionality — that intersection is where elegance lives, neither naive/under-functional nor over-engineered

  • Make essential complexity concrete, pose an open discussion question, not a settled answer
  • Notation: circles = processes, lines = signals — functional architecture of a car’s powered rear liftgate
  • Why this complicated: a truly minimal system (button + simple reversing logic) would miss a safety function — preventing liftgate opening while the vehicle moves — forcing in speed sensing, ignition-status checking, cascading logic. Figure 13.6 · Apparent ≠ Essential

A system’s essential or gratuitous complexity is independent of its apparent complexity. The Concorde’s flight deck is visually overwhelming and required extensive training — high apparent complexity. Given the Concorde’s success, that complexity could be argued essential to delivering its performance with the technology of the era. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 13.6.

  • Decouple two axes students might conflate: apparent complexity vs. essential/gratuitous complexity — genuinely independent, high on one implies nothing about the other
  • Concorde flight deck: visually overwhelming, dials/switches/gauges, extensive specialized pilot training — unambiguously high apparent complexity
  • → But: Concorde succeeded at supersonic passenger flight with 1960s-70s avionics tech — arguably that complexity was essential, not gratuitous, for that performance with that era’s technology. A simpler-looking deck might not have delivered the same performance
  • Hold both ideas at once: something can look complicated (high apparent) while architected efficiently (low gratuitous) — different questions, conflating them → bad architectural judgment

Figure 13.7 · Low Apparent, High Actual

The smartphone’s touchscreen interface has very low apparent complexity — little or no training to operate — while masking substantial internal hardware complexity. No one would argue the Concorde’s interface should be condensed to a menu, four buttons, and scrolling — but is the phone’s hidden complexity essential, or gratuitous? Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 13.7.

  • Mirror-image case to the Concorde — completes the 2×2 of apparent × essential complexity
  • Smartphone touchscreen: extremely low apparent complexity (pick up, use, little/no training) while masking enormous internal hardware complexity (multi-core processors, multiple wireless radios, sensor arrays)
  • Deliberately unresolved question: nobody argues Concorde’s cockpit should be a phone-like menu — flight-safety stakes too different. But is the phone’s hidden complexity essential, or gratuitous (feature creep, legacy chip designs, backward-compatibility)?
  • → Discussion prompt: name a piece of your own phone’s capability you suspect is more complex than strictly essential

Figure 13.8 · Decomposition Has a Price

Five entities, six connections (left). Grouping into two clusters of three and two apparently simplifies the system to one connection (right) — but the decomposition introduces an interface between the groups: the system now has six entities and nine connections (middle)! Reducing apparent complexity can increase actual complexity. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 13.8.

  • The architect must decide whether to invest in actual complexity (to bring down apparent complexity), or not to invest at all
  • One of the most important, counter-intuitive figures in the chapter — walk through all three panels slowly, sketch on board
  • Left: 5 entities, 6 connections — fair amount of apparent complexity already. Tempting: “group into two clusters to simplify”
  • Middle (what actually happens): original 5 entities + 2 new group-boundary entities (6 total); creating those boundaries requires new interfaces to route existing relationships through → connections go up to 9. Looks simpler at group level, but underlying system got more complex
  • Right: only once each cluster is fully collapsed into an abstracted box (internals no longer tracked) → just 1 connection between 2 boxes — lower apparent complexity, achieved by discarding visibility into internals
  • → Central warning: reducing apparent complexity via decomposition can increase actual complexity (decomposition introduces new interfaces) — not a flaw, an unavoidable tradeoff
  • Architect’s job: consciously decide whether to invest in that added actual complexity for lower apparent complexity, or not decompose at all — never assume “simpler-looking” = complexity-free

Box 13.4 · Principle of the 2nd Law

iDrive, a cautionary tale. BMW’s first iDrive consolidated dashboard switches under one twist/angle/press controller to reduce apparent complexity — but the menu ended up five levels deep to reach the heater. Reducing apparent complexity of the switches caused a net increase in apparent complexity of the menu.

  • Two quotations, a nice pairing: C.A.R. Hoare — two ways to build software (so simple there are obviously no deficiencies, or so complicated there are no obvious deficiencies); Norman Augustine — software is like entropy, hard to grasp, weighs nothing, always increases (echoes the actual 2nd Law)
  • Principle: actual complexity always exceeds essential complexity in practice — architect’s job: keep actual close to essential, keep apparent within human understanding
  • Actual complexity = the real, measured complexity of the built system — exceeds essential due to overhead of abstraction/decomposition/hierarchy (Fig 13.8: those tools cost real interfaces), grows further from ongoing gratuitous-complexity “creep”
  • iDrive, vivid real cautionary tale: BMW consolidated dashboard switches into one twist/angle/press controller to reduce apparent complexity (fewer buttons, cleaner cabin) — but the resulting menu ended up 5 levels deep to reach the heater
  • → Net effect: reducing apparent complexity on one axis (switches) caused a net increase on another (menu navigation) — classic “whack-a-mole” failure of naive complexity reduction

Section 13.2 Summary

  • Complexity is absolute and quantifiable (\(C = N_1+N_2+N_3+N_4\)); complicatedness (apparent complexity) measures our ability to perceive it — the two are related but not the same, and can be manipulated somewhat independently
  • Essential complexity is the minimum needed to deliver required functionality; gratuitous complexity is everything invested beyond that, and should be avoided
  • Decomposition and abstraction can reduce apparent complexity, but frequently at the cost of increasing actual complexity (new interfaces) — that investment must be a deliberate choice, not an accident
  • Checkpoint before Section 13.3 — have students explain each bullet in their own words
  • Should be comfortable with: complexity as absolute/quantifiable (N1–N4); complicatedness/apparent complexity as a separate human-perception property; essential vs. gratuitous complexity
  • Trickiest/most important idea: our main tools for reducing apparent complexity (decomposition, abstraction) frequently increase actual complexity via new interfaces — a deliberate investment, never a free lunch
  • → Chapter’s next question: given complexity must be managed not eliminated, what’s the architect’s primary tool? Answer: decomposition, done thoughtfully — very different from decomposition done carelessly

13.3 Managing Complexity

Choosing a Decomposition

  • Chapters 2–3 gave us abstraction, decomposition, hierarchy, and logical relationships (class/instance, type/specialization, recursion) — tools to represent architecture while removing irrelevant detail
  • Modern engineering is entwined with the Bill of Materials — a very concrete decomposition down to purchased components. But it doesn’t necessarily lend itself to functional decomposition of the concept
  • A functional decomposition determines which subsystems accomplish which functions, which functions are necessarily emergent, and carries the architecture’s intent down through the design — objectives not served by a purely formal decomposition
  • Reconnect to Chapters 2-3’s toolkit: abstraction, decomposition, hierarchy, logical relationships (class/instance, type/specialization, recursion)
  • Sharper practical question: given those tools exist, how does an architect actually choose a good decomposition, not an arbitrary one?
  • Bill of Materials (BOM): modern engineering deeply entwined with it — extremely concrete decomposition down to purchased components, needed for procurement/costing/manufacturing. But doesn’t serve functional decomposition — tells you what to buy, not which subsystems deliver which functions, nor which functions will be emergent
  • → Genuine functional decomposition instead: determines which subsystems accomplish which functions, flags emergent functions (needing cross-subsystem coordination), carries architect’s intent down through detailed design — objectives a BOM-style decomposition doesn’t serve

Box 13.5 · Principle of Decomposition

  • A bad example: decomposing operations by nominal get-ready/get-set/go/get-unset/get-unready focuses on the typical case, omitting fault conditions, contingencies, and commissioning
  • Epigraph: “Divide et impera” (“Divide and conquer”) — same ancient idea from Chapter 3, now a formal principle for architecture decomposition
  • Content: decomposition is an active choice, not neutral/automatic — affects performance measurement, organizational structure, supplier value capture, future product evolution
  • Prescriptive guidance: choose the decomposition plane to align as many of these factors as possible, minimizing resulting apparent complexity
  • Bad example: decomposing operations purely by nominal sequence (get-ready/get-set/go/get-unset/get-unready) — focuses on the typical happy-path, silently omits fault conditions, contingencies, commissioning
  • → Leaves the architecture blind to exactly the scenarios where things go wrong — Chapter 2’s emergencies discussion: often where the most serious failures occur

“2 Down, 1 Up”

  • How do we judge the “goodness” of a decomposition? The decomposition at Level 1 cannot be evaluated until we descend to Level 2
  • Analogy: decomposing a class of 30 students into two groups. We won’t know if it’s useful until we see the Level 2 structure — decomposition by height, or by visual impairment, serve very different purposes
  • The real information about how to cluster at Level 1 is present in the structure and interaction two levels down from the reference
  • Central practical question: given decomposition is a real choice with real consequences, how do you judge whether a particular decomposition is good?
  • Non-obvious answer: Level 1 decomposition cannot be evaluated in isolation — must descend to Level 2 first
  • Analogy: 30 students into two groups — “two groups” alone tells you nothing. Grouping by height (seating) vs. visual impairment (accommodation planning) — same Level 1 structure, radically different value depending on Level 2
  • → Punchline: the real, actionable info about how to cluster well at Level 1 is contained in structure/interaction patterns two levels down — genuinely counter-intuitive, let it sink in before next slide’s visual

Figure 13.9 · Illustration of “2 Down, 1 Up”

  • Visual proof of the previous slide’s claim
  • Grid/matrix layout: Level 2 entities and their interconnection pattern — dense clustering in some regions, sparse/absent in others
  • Key insight: once you see which Level 2 elements are tightly interconnected vs. loosely coupled, the “right” Level 1 grouping becomes visually obvious — draw Level 1 boundaries around tightly-clustered Level 2 groups so dense interactions stay within a module, not crossing as an interface
  • → Same underlying logic as Chapter 8’s DSM-based clustering, now as a general principle rather than a specific algorithmic technique

Box 13.6 · Principle of “2 Down, 1 Up”

  • Chapter’s most quotable, most actionable principle — have students write it down verbatim
  • To judge a Level 1 decomposition: descend two levels (to Level 2), examine structure/interaction patterns found there, only then go back up one level to finalize Level 1 groupings — hence “2 Down, 1 Up”
  • → Why order matters: committing to Level 1 first, checking Level 2 later, risks locking in a poor decomposition before you have evidence — the “30 students” trap again. Discipline: defer Level 1 judgment until the Level 2 work justifies it

How Many Elements per Level?

  • Chapter 8’s air transportation service example works exactly this way: start from the Level 0 concept, propose a Level 1 trial decomposition organized by sequence, decompose to Level 2, examine clustering there, and only then finalize Level 1
  • Experience suggests humans manage 7 ± 2 elements comfortably at each level of decomposition
  • More than nine elements inhibits the designer and makes cross-element analysis combinatorially harder; fewer than five forces deeper decompositions (more levels, harder to “think vertically”)
  • Tie “2 Down, 1 Up” to a concrete example: Chapter 8’s air transportation service — Level 0 concept → trial Level 1 (organized by sequence) → decompose to Level 2 → examine clustering → then finalize Level 1. “2 Down, 1 Up” as a worked methodology
  • Miller’s 7±2 (Chapter 2), applied to decomposition sizing per level: humans manage ~7±2 elements comfortably at any level
  • Two failure directions:

    • 9 elements: inhibits reasoning about the whole set, cross-element analysis combinatorially harder

    • <5 elements: forces unnecessarily deep decompositions (more levels), harder to “think vertically”
  • → 7±2 is the sweet spot balancing both failure modes

Modularity and Decomposition

  • The choice of decomposition is linked to the resulting system’s modularity — often called desirable, rarely clearly defined
  • Modularity refers to a system’s interfaces: a system is modular if its interfaces allow old modules to be removed and new ones inserted — most commonly in the context of an open number of variants (Lego’s interface enables an open, if not infinite, number of shapes)
  • Commonality/platforms refer instead to a closed set of variants (e.g., an alternator bracket for three known variants). Commonality focuses on cost-saving part/code sharing; modularity focuses on operational and design flexibility — related but distinct ideas
  • Modularity: invoked constantly as an unqualified virtue, rarely defined precisely — fix that here
  • Choice of decomposition ties directly to modularity: decomposition determines what interfaces exist, interfaces are what modularity is about
  • Precise definition: a system is modular if its interfaces allow old modules removed, new ones inserted. Emphasize “open” — modularity most powerfully means an open, potentially unbounded number of swappable variants
  • Lego: stud/tube connector standard supports an open, essentially unlimited variety of shapes — anyone can design a new brick that plugs in
  • Commonality/platforming (careful distinction, often conflated): a closed, known set of variants — e.g., an alternator bracket for three known car models, not an open standard
  • → Commonality’s motivation = cost savings via part/code sharing across known variants. Modularity’s motivation = operational/design flexibility — swapping in something not yet known. Related, but conceptually distinct — a system can have one without the other

Decomposition Plane: A Worked Example

  • DSMs (Chapter 8) let the architect analyze a system against a clear objective — minimizing interconnections. But is minimizing interconnections always the right principle for decomposing a system?
  • The two key ideas in decomposition are the number of elements/chunks (the easy part) and the plane of decomposition (the hard part)
  • Consider a technical report on three concepts, each subjected to three types of analysis. The chapters could be organized by concept (Figure 13.10) — enabling broad cross-analysis judgment — or by analysis, enabling direct comparison of concepts against a metric. Neither is inherently right; the choice is a lens that enables creativity along one plane while repressing it along others
  • Reconnect to Chapter 8’s DSM tools, then push past them: DSMs analyze against one clear objective — minimizing interconnections. Is that always the right principle for deciding how to decompose? Answer developed this section: no — depends on which plane
  • Two-part structure of any decomposition decision: number of elements/chunks (easy part, Miller’s number) vs. plane of decomposition (the genuinely hard part, where judgment matters)
  • Worked example: a technical report on three concepts, each evaluated against three analysis types (cost, performance, risk)
    • By concept: one chapter per concept, all three analyses inside (Fig 13.10) — supports holistic judgment per concept
    • By analysis type: one chapter per analysis, comparing all three concepts — supports apples-to-apples comparison on one dimension
  • → Neither is inherently more correct — each lens enables one kind of thinking while suppressing another. Choosing a plane = choosing what kind of thinking to make easy

Figure 13.10 · Potential Decomposition Planes

  • The actual grid: 3 concepts × 3 analysis types = 3×3 space of possible report content
  • “By concept” = three row-wise chapters; “by analysis” = three column-wise chapters — same underlying info, two different planes cutting the same 3×3 space
  • → Have students sketch a third plane themselves (by author, by project phase) — discuss what it makes easier/harder to see. Reinforces: the space of possible planes is often larger than the two obvious ones

Box 13.7 · Principle of Elegance

  • The choice of decomposition plane is a strong lever on the elegance of the resulting architecture
  • Formally names the property the chapter’s been building toward: elegance
  • Elegant architectures: decomposition aligns cleanly across multiple planes at once (form, function, supplier, operation) — simple, minimal interfaces, not a tangle of cross-cutting connections
  • → Formal payoff of Section 13.3 so far: 2 Down 1 Up gives a method for judging a decomposition locally good; elegance is the broader property — planes reinforcing each other, not fighting
  • Choice of which plane(s) to decompose along = one of the architect’s strongest levers for achieving (or failing) elegance — sets up the next slides’ catalog of planes

Form vs. Function Decomposition

  • Form decomposition allocates one or more elements of form to each Level 1 entity — form is clearly divided (e.g., Hybrid Car by body, chassis, drivetrain, interior). Advantage: concrete, and properties like mass sum linearly, without emergence
  • Function decomposition lists system functions as the Level 1 entities (support/comfort passengers, store energy, produce power, isolate passengers from vibration…). This highlights which types of emergence the decomposition will force the architect to confront
  • There is no reason form and function are the only two decomposition planes — a firm’s competitive advantage might instead hinge on the interface between two dissimilar suppliers
  • Two most common planes (not the only ones) — Hybrid Car from Chapter 12 as running example
  • Form decomposition: allocates physical form elements per Level 1 entity (body, chassis, drivetrain, interior). Advantage: concrete, tangible — properties like mass sum linearly, no emergence complicating bookkeeping (form aggregates additively, Chapter 3)
  • Function decomposition: lists actual functions as Level 1 entities (support/comfort passengers, store energy, produce power, isolate from vibration). Opposite value: highlights which emergence the architect must confront — function is where emergent, non-additive behavior shows up
  • → No principled reason form/function are the only planes — e.g., a firm’s competitive advantage might hinge on the interface between two dissimilar suppliers. Previews Box 13.8’s longer catalog

Figure 13.11 · Form vs. Function

  • Visual companion to the previous slide: form-decomposition tree (Hybrid Car by body/chassis/drivetrain/interior) side-by-side with function-decomposition tree (support/comfort passengers, store energy, produce power, isolate from vibration)

Conway’s Law

Conway’s Law (1968): “Organizations which design systems are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations.”

  • The modularity of a system is inextricably intertwined with the organization that develops it — cited as a storyline for the Mars Climate Orbiter failure (two teams, metric vs. imperial units, wrong orbital-insertion altitude)
  • The best formulation: product and organization are coupled, not simply “product mirrors organization” or vice versa. Modern research finds strong evidence for a causal link between organization and product modularity in software
  • Conway’s Law = another decomposition plane, the “organizational” one — usually invisible to engineers thinking purely technically
  • 1968 formulation: organizations designing systems produce designs that copy their own communication structures. Plainly: three siloed teams → product architecture likely ends up with three correspondingly separate modules, whether or not that’s technically ideal
  • Mars Climate Orbiter: one team’s software in metric, another in imperial for the same trajectory calculations — mismatch (byproduct of organizational division) → wrong orbital-insertion altitude, spacecraft lost. Conway’s Law as mission failure, not just inefficiency
  • → More nuanced formulation: product architecture and organization are genuinely coupled, each influences the other — not simply “product mirrors organization” or the reverse. Modern empirical research (esp. software engineering) finds a real causal link in both directions — active research area, not settled history

The Cost of Modularity

  • It is easy to argue more modularity is better. But modularity carries a cost — recall Figure 13.8: even where interfaces already exist, building modularity into an interface means meeting a range of performance goals (extra structural loads, unused connector pins, and so on)

Tip

Alan Perlis: “Wherever there is modularity there is the potential for misunderstanding: hiding information implies a need to check communication.”

  • Box 13.8 catalogs 12 potential decomposition planes — by no means exhaustive — with guidance on each. Alignment of these planes can produce more elegant decompositions, where possible
  • Push back on “more modularity is always better” — students likely absorbed this uncritically elsewhere
  • Modularity carries a real cost — recall Fig 13.8: building genuine modularity into an interface (accommodating variants/replacements not yet built) means meeting a wider range of performance goals than strictly needed now — extra structural loads for unknown future modules, unused connector pins, etc. Investment in future flexibility, paid for in present cost/complexity
  • Alan Perlis quote, unpack it: “wherever there is modularity there is the potential for misunderstanding: hiding information implies a need to check communication” — modularity hides information behind a clean interface (the point), but that risks both sides misunderstanding what’s hidden; real communication/spec discipline is needed because of modularity, not despite it
  • → Box 13.8: catalog of a dozen potential decomposition planes, explicitly non-exhaustive, brief guidance each — a checklist for future architecture work, run down before finalizing a decomposition to see if aligning more planes improves elegance

Box 13.8 · Potential Planes for Decomposition (1/4)

Plane Guidance
Delivered function & emergence Don’t spread key delivered functions across many elements — it complicates managing emergence.
Form & structure Cluster high-connectivity elements; avoid placing interfaces where connectivity is high.
Design latitude & change propagation Group tightly-coupled designs to maximize latitude and limit change propagation.
Changeability & evolution Place interfaces so modules can combine into platforms and support evolution.
  • First group of four planes — ground each concretely, don’t just read the guidance
  • Delivered function & emergence: don’t spread an externally-delivered function across too many elements — recall Chapter 5, primary function crosses the system boundary/delivers value; scattered implementation makes emergence harder to reason about
  • Form & structure: direct DSM-style rule from Chapter 8 — cluster high-connectivity elements, avoid interfaces where connectivity is highest
  • Design latitude & change propagation: group tightly-coupled designs together so a change doesn’t propagate outward across a module boundary
  • Changeability & evolution: place interfaces to let modules recombine into platforms, supporting evolution across future product generations, not just current requirements

Box 13.8 · Potential Planes for Decomposition (2/4)

Plane Guidance
Integration transparency Create interfaces that enable easy testing and visibility at the interface.
Suppliers Interface points let suppliers work independently — they can define modularity, or defy it.
Openness Balance third-party innovation/network effects against information-sharing drawbacks.
Legacy components Reuse of legacy components constrains decomposition and challenges interface design.
  • Second group of four planes
  • Integration transparency: interfaces designed for easy testing/visibility at the boundary — an untestable interface hides integration problems until they’re expensive
  • Suppliers: reconnects to the Saturn V case study ahead — interfaces let suppliers work independently. Subtle: suppliers can define modularity for you (their own boundaries dictate yours) or defy it (resist a clean interface that doesn’t match how they sell)
  • Openness: strategic tradeoff — third-party innovation/network-effect benefits vs. the drawback of sharing more information than you’d like
  • Legacy components: reusing legacy components — their pre-existing boundaries constrain feasible decomposition/interface designs; not a blank slate

Box 13.8 · Potential Planes for Decomposition (3/4)

Plane Guidance
Clockspeed of technology change Let technologies evolving at different rates be changed out asynchronously.
Marketing & sales Enable differentiating features and cosmetic refreshes without architectural change.
Operations & interoperability Delineate operator touch points and wear parts for easy training, maintenance, repair.
  • Third group of three planes
  • Clockspeed of technology change: interfaces let components evolving at different rates (fast-moving electronics vs. slow-moving mechanical chassis) be swapped out asynchronously, each on its own upgrade cycle
  • Marketing & sales: decomposition supports the business side — differentiating features and cosmetic refreshes (trim levels, colors, feature bundles) without deep architectural change
  • Operations & interoperability: delineate operator touch points and wear parts clearly, so training/maintenance/repair are straightforward, not requiring specialized internal-boundary knowledge

Box 13.8 · Potential Planes for Decomposition (4/4)

Plane Guidance
Timing of investment Modularize to phase development spending across time.
Organization Match the modularization of the system to the organization (Conway’s Law).
  • Last two planes
  • Timing of investment: modularize to phase development spending over time — an initial module delivers value/returns before later modules need their own investment, rather than funding/building everything at once
  • Organization: closes the loop to Conway’s Law — match modularization to the organization that builds/maintains it, since a mismatch resolves itself eventually anyway, usually at an unplanned cost
  • → Wrap up Box 13.8: these dozen planes are explicitly not exhaustive — a starting checklist, not a closed taxonomy. Message: be aware many planes exist beyond form/function, look to align several simultaneously — that’s what produces elegance

Section 13.3 Summary

  • The decomposition of a system is one of the architect’s most powerful decisions — a poor decomposition can break lines of communication or hurt future modularity
  • Test a decomposition’s goodness by the Principle of 2 Down, 1 Up: descend to Level 2, examine the aggregation, and only then finalize Level 1
  • Many planes of decomposition are possible (form, function, supplier, operation, and Box 13.8’s twelve more) — the architect must judge which emphasis matters and align planes where possible, in pursuit of elegance
  • Checkpoint for all of Section 13.3, mirroring the 13.2 summary
  • Students should state: decomposition = one of the architect’s most consequential decisions (poor choice breaks communication lines / forecloses future modularity)
  • “2 Down, 1 Up” = the test for whether a decomposition is good — can’t judge Level 1 without Level 2’s structure
  • Many valid planes (form, function, supplier, operation, Box 13.8’s twelve) — no universally correct choice; architect judges which matter most, aligns as many as possible → elegance

13.4 Chapter Summary

  • Managing complexity is a central task of the architect. Apparent complexity (complicatedness) is distinct from essential complexity, which is driven purely by required functionality
  • The architect invests in abstraction, hierarchy, decomposition, and recursion to reduce apparent complexity — potentially at the cost of increasing actual complexity
  • The goodness of a decomposition is judged by testing it two levels down (2 Down, 1 Up); many decomposition planes are possible, and the architect must choose deliberately

Tip

Reference: Crawley, E., Cameron, B., & Selva, D. (2016). System Architecture: Strategy and Product Development for Complex Systems. Pearson. Chapter 13.

  • Chapter’s overall closing statement before the case study
  • Managing complexity is a central, ongoing architect task, not a one-time early step
  • Restate the two core distinctions: apparent complexity (human perception) vs. essential complexity (driven by required functionality)
  • Architect’s tools (abstraction, hierarchy, decomposition, recursion) reduce apparent complexity but potentially increase actual complexity via new interfaces — always a deliberate, examined choice
  • Takeaway: judge decomposition goodness via 2 Down, 1 Up; many planes are possible — real skill is choosing deliberately, aligning several, not defaulting to the obvious one
  • → Transition to case study: everything so far is abstract/principle-level — the case study makes it concrete by contrasting two real aerospace programs, one where alignment worked (Saturn V), one where it didn’t (Space Station Freedom) — real, measurable consequences for a multi-billion-dollar program’s success

Case Study: Decomposition of Saturn V and Space Station Freedom

Box 13.9 · Two Complex Space Systems

  • We compare two complex space systems: the Saturn V launch vehicle (1960s) and Space Station Freedom (proposed, 1980s) — to illustrate the importance of alignment across planes of decomposition
  • Thesis: the Saturn V shows good alignment across decomposition planes; the Space Station presents a far more coupled, complex picture — and the relative success of the two programs can be partly attributed to this difference
  • Set up the case study’s structure and thesis before diving in
  • Both large, genuinely complex space systems, different eras: Saturn V (NASA, 1960s Apollo), Space Station Freedom (proposed 1980s, never fully built) — comparison illustrates alignment across the decomposition planes from Section 13.3
  • Thesis: Saturn V shows good alignment across decomposition planes; SSF presents a far more coupled, tangled picture where planes work against each other
  • → Careful causal claim, book is careful too: relative success can be partly attributed to this alignment difference — a real, demonstrable contributing factor, not the sole cause

Saturn V: The Launch Vehicle

The Saturn V, developed by NASA 1962–1968, launched the Apollo Command Service Module, Lunar Module, and crew from Earth’s surface into orbit and toward the Moon. Its solution-neutral function: accelerate a payload by a change in velocity (Delta-ν) in each of three stages. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 13.12.

  • Ground the case in facts that make the tables interpretable
  • Developed by NASA 1962-1968 — overlaps Chapter 1’s opening story (June 1962 lunar-orbit-rendezvous decision) — nice callback
  • Launched Apollo Command/Service Module, Lunar Module, and crew from Earth’s surface into orbit and toward the Moon
  • Solution-neutral function (Chapter 7 vocabulary): accelerate a payload by change in velocity (Delta-ν), across three separate stages
  • → Three-stage structure is the key to the coming tables: Saturn V’s Level 1 decomposition = Stage 1, Stage 2, Stage 3. Next two slides: how do the stages relate across function, supplier, operation planes?

Why the Saturn V Succeeded

  • The Solution-Neutral Function, Supplier, and Operation arrays each suggest a sparse, uncoupled relationship among elements of form, and a strong potential alignment of decomposition planes — the Internal Function array is the deliberate exception
  • The key architectural decision: replicate each principal internal function (thrust, guidance, structural load, aerodynamic loads) in every stage — the only important internal interface was the structural-load path (L) transmitting force up the stack
  • This replication created some sub-optimality (a single guidance system could have served the whole vehicle) — but let each supplier integrate and test its stage nearly independently, with dead-simple inter-stage interfaces (a bolt pattern plus a few wires)

Tip

The Saturn V was the largest launch vehicle ever built and had a perfect launch record — alignment of decomposition planes, simple interfaces, decentralized testing, and simple integration were major contributors.

  • Synthesize the four arrays into the actual conclusion
  • 3 of 4 arrays (Solution-Neutral Function, Supplier, Operation) independently point to the same clean, sparse, uncoupled Stage 1/2/3 decomposition — that’s alignment: multiple independent planes agreeing on the same boundaries
  • Internal Function = deliberate, isolated exception: architects chose to replicate each principal internal function (thrust, guidance, load, aero) in every stage rather than share (e.g., one guidance system for the whole vehicle) — so the only important internal interface was the structural-load path
  • Honest about the cost: not perfectly efficient — a shared guidance system could have saved mass/cost, a real accepted sub-optimality. In exchange: each supplier (Boeing, North American, McDonnell) integrated/tested nearly independently, with dead-simple interstage interfaces (bolt pattern + a few wires)
  • → Payoff: largest launch vehicle ever built, perfect launch record. Careful causal claim: alignment + simple interfaces + decentralized testing + simple integration were major contributors — not the only factor, but real and identifiable

Space Station Freedom

Space Station Freedom (SSF), a semi-permanent Earth-orbiting station announced in 1984, was never built — over a decade it underwent design changes and eventually evolved into the International Space Station (1993). Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 13.13.

  • Solution-neutral function: a laboratory for micro-gravity science and Earth observation, a base for space operations, and — a strong Cold-War political function — cementing international relationships (elements from Europe, Canada, Japan)
  • Contrasting case — register the key fact immediately: SSF, announced 1984, never actually built as such — a decade of design changes, evolved into the International Space Station (1993). Already a very different outcome from Saturn V
  • Solution-neutral function, far more heterogeneous than Saturn V’s single clean Δν: lab for micro-gravity science and Earth observation, base for space operations, and — worth emphasizing, not a technical requirement — a Cold-War political function of cementing international relationships (elements from Europe, Canada, Japan, US)
  • → That third function is a Chapter 5 example: value-related function driven by non-technical stakeholder interests. Previews why the coming decomposition tables look so much messier than Saturn V’s

A Crisis in Decomposition

  • The Solution-Neutral Function array is dense (ISM everywhere) — the function plane gives almost no guidance for modularization. The Internal Function array shows power and structural load extending to every element
  • Each piece of form was part of an element (truss, laboratory), part of a system (thermal control, power), and part of a launch package (flight 1, flight 2) — four separate NASA center/supplier teams were responsible across both elements and systems
  • The Operation array (1987 manifest) defied every other alignment — assembly flights were sequenced to maximize payload utilization, with mating components not planned to meet until on-orbit

Ten years of SSF development, roughly $10 billion spent, only modest progress. In 1993 the program was dramatically restructured under a single prime contractor (Boeing) and a single NASA center — aligning launch packages with elements and functions with elements.

  • Pull the diagnosis together — one coherent narrative, why SSF struggled
  • Solution-Neutral Function array completely dense → function plane provides almost no useful guidance for modularizing, no natural fault line
  • Internal Function array compounds this: power and structural load extend to nearly every element, not neatly contained
  • Organizational root cause: each physical form piece was simultaneously part of an element (truss, lab module), a system through it (thermal control, power), and a launch package (assembly flight) — three overlapping ownership schemes for the same hardware, four NASA/supplier teams holding responsibility across boundaries, no one with clean end-to-end ownership
  • Operation array (1987 manifest) actively defied whatever alignment other planes suggested — flights sequenced to maximize payload utilization, mating components sometimes not scheduled to meet until on-orbit
  • → Sobering outcome: ~10 years, ~$10 billion, only modest progress. 1993 restructuring: reorganized under a single prime contractor (Boeing) and single NASA center, forcing alignment between launch packages/elements and functions/elements — the fix this whole chapter teaches, learned the hard way

Lessons from Alignment

  • Elegant architectures (Box 13.7) have decompositional alignment that allows modularization of all factors in the same way, producing simple interfaces
  • Good architectures, like the Saturn V, accommodate some irregularities (recall the single guidance system that could have served the whole vehicle) while still keeping most planes aligned
  • Poor architectures have no alignment, and require difficult choices about what to modularize and what to leave as a more complex set of interfaces — as in Space Station Freedom
  • The lesson generalizes well beyond aerospace: whenever possible, choose the plane of decomposition to align form, function, supplier, and operation — elegance and manageable complexity follow from that alignment, not from chance
  • Close the case study — return to Box 13.7’s vocabulary, generalize beyond these two examples
  • Elegant architectures: decompositional alignment across all relevant factors, genuinely simple interfaces — Saturn V, even though not perfectly optimal (accepted sub-optimality of replicated guidance)
  • Good architectures (Saturn V): tolerate some irregularities, still keep most planes aligned — useful middle category, real systems are rarely perfectly elegant
  • Poor architectures: no meaningful alignment, forcing difficult reactive choices about what to modularize vs. accept as tangled interfaces — SSF before 1993
  • → Final generalizable takeaway: whenever possible, choose the decomposition plane to align form, function, supplier, and operation — elegance and manageable complexity follow from deliberate alignment, not chance
  • Connects back to the chapter’s opening theme: complexity is neither good nor bad, it’s an investment — this case study shows, with real dollars and launch records, that how you decompose has enormous measurable consequences for whether that investment pays off
