What Are Complex Systems?

Or, How Does Behavior Emerge?

Aykut C. Satici

Beyond a Single Discipline

Definition: Emergence

System-level behavior that arises from the interaction of many simple parts, and that cannot be predicted by analyzing those parts in isolation. It is the central phenomenon this entire course is organized around.

The science of complex systems does not belong exclusively to physics, biology, or the social sciences. It emerges from a combination of all three, forming a new discipline in its own right.

From physics
mathematical rigor, quantitative modeling, predictability

From biology
adaptation, evolution, self-organization

From social science
agent interaction, decision-making, collective behavior

Example: Traffic Flow

Behaves like a physical system (density, flow), evolves like a biological one (drivers adapt to congestion), and reflects social behavior (incentives, risk tolerance).

  • Name the central phenomenon: emergence — whole-system behavior you can’t predict from the parts alone, however well you understand each part
  • Everything in these two lectures is an extended answer to: how does emergent behavior arise, and when can we expect it even if we can’t derive it?
  • Complex systems science lives outside any one discipline — physics, biology, social science each explain part, none alone is sufficient
    • From physics: mathematical rigor, testable quantitative models
    • From biology: adaptation, evolution, self-organization
    • From social science: agents who decide, respond to incentives, interact strategically
  • Traffic example (students have direct intuition): physical (density/flow), adaptive (drivers learn routes), social (risk tolerance, incentives, information)
  • → No single lens explains traffic jams — that gap is where this course lives

What Physics Contributes

Physics is the quantitative, predictive science of matter and interactions: mathematical description, testable predictions, controlled experiments, repeatability.

It excels at isolated systems, linear cause-and-effect, and predictable outcomes.

Honeybees on comb: thousands of simple agents, densely interacting.

Knowing the physics of a single neuron does not explain consciousness. Knowing the aerodynamics of one car does not explain a traffic jam.

  • Give physics its due first: Newton’s laws (projectile), Ohm’s law (circuit) — precise, falsifiable, isolated systems, few parts, linear cause-and-effect. Real predictive power — complex systems science builds on it, doesn’t discard it
  • But: 10,000 densely interacting bees = many components, nonlinearity, feedback loops, emergent properties — outside what isolated-system physics handles
  • Memorable pair: single neuron’s biophysics → tells you nothing about consciousness. Single car’s aerodynamics → tells you nothing about a traffic jam
  • → Component physics is necessary but nowhere near sufficient

Biology: How Systems Adapt

Biology explains adaptation, learning, and survival over time. Not just interaction, but change.

Example (honeybee colony): individual bees follow simple rules; the colony adapts foraging strategy to food availability, and evolves behavior to improve survival. Poor strategies die out; successful ones persist.

A honeybee swarm relocating: a colony-level decision made with no central controller.

Biology answers: “How does the system change over time to improve fitness?”

  • Physics = “how do components interact right now.” Biology adds the time dimension: “how does the system change to survive better”
  • Running example for the lecture: honeybee colony. No single bee knows foraging strategy, food availability, or colony fitness — each follows simple local rules
  • Colony as a whole adapts foraging when conditions change; selection mechanism behind it (Darwinian-like) — strategies that work persist, others don’t
  • Photo: swarm mid-decision — colony outgrows hive, bees cluster, scout bees “dance” for candidate sites, decentralized vote converges on one site, no bee sees the whole picture
  • → Come back to swarm decision-making again in a few slides

Social Science: How Agents Decide

Social sciences focus on decision-making, incentives, and interactions among agents.

Example: Drivers in Traffic

Speed chosen by risk tolerance; lane changes chosen by perceived advantage; route chosen by experience or navigation apps. Behavior varies by culture, incentives, and information.

Social sciences answer: “Why do agents choose certain actions?”

  • Third lens completes the triangle: physics = how components interact; biology = how systems change over time; social science = why agents choose the actions they do
  • Only makes sense once “components” have preferences, incomplete information, strategic reasoning
  • Driving example: two drivers, identical road/density, behave differently — risk tolerance, trust in nav-app routing, cultural norms on following distance/lane discipline
  • None of that variation comes from vehicle physics or learned-route biology — it’s agents deciding under incentives/information
  • → Complex systems science needs all three lenses at once (pitch of next slide)

All Three, Integrated

No single discipline alone explains urban traffic jams, cascading power-grid blackouts, or emergency-department overcrowding.

  • Diagram = visual summary of last 3 slides: physics + biology + social science → complex systems science, studying emergence from interaction + adaptation + decision-making together
  • Three integrated examples to walk through out loud (only traffic pictured):
    • Urban traffic: physics (vehicle dynamics, road capacity) + adaptation (drivers learn routes) + social (incentives, norms) → jams appear with zero accidents, small disruptions cascade
    • Power grid: physics (power flow, voltage stability) + adaptive control systems (biology-like) + social (consumption patterns, markets) → cascading blackouts
    • Healthcare: physics-like (equipment reliability, patient-flow capacity) + biology-like (disease progression, population health) + social (patient/clinician/admin decisions) → ED overcrowding, unintended policy consequences
  • → In every case, no single discipline alone explains the emergent behavior

Why Interactions Matter

Honeybees (10,000 bees): possible pairwise interactions = 10,000 × 9,999 ≈ 100 million

Non-social bees (10 bees, e.g. mason or carpenter bees): 10 × 9 = 90

Same order of magnitude difference in population, six orders of magnitude difference in the space of possible interactions.

  • Small arithmetic, big conceptual payoff: why honeybees are a paradigm complex system and a solitary bee species is not
  • Not about how many individuals — about how many potential interactions
  • 10,000 bees → ~100 million pairwise interactions. 10 non-social bees (mason/carpenter, no true colony) → only 90
  • Explicit takeaway: complexity scales combinatorially, not linearly, with number of interacting entities
  • → Element count is a poor complexity proxy; connectivity is what matters (resurfaces later as networks)

Emergence from Interactions

Simple components, connected in complex ways, cause behavior to emerge.

Emergent behavior is impossible to predict analytically, but it can be expected algorithmically.

Simple local crossing rules generate a rich family of distinct global patterns.

  • “Emergence” = the single most important word in this course — define precisely, keep returning to it
  • Emergent = collective/system-level behavior that cannot be derived analytically (pencil-and-paper, first principles) from the parts
  • Not magic/unknowable: still expected algorithmically — simulate simple local rules forward, observe what patterns reliably show up
  • Animation (visual metaphor, not a literal complex system): simple local crossing rules → rich family of distinct global patterns; no single rule “contains” any one pattern
  • → Essence of emergence: global structure from local rules, no central blueprint. Every example this semester (bee swarms, traffic, blackouts) is a version of this

What Are Complex Systems?

Definition

Complex systems exhibit emergent behavior that cannot be predicted by analysis of constituent parts, but emergent patterns can be expected algorithmically.

Identifying characteristics:

  • High sensitivity to boundary conditions (uncertainty)
  • Behavior dominated by power laws (path dependence, outliers)
  • Representable as networks of connected elements (abstractability)

Studying complex systems can pay off enormously: the Honey Bee Algorithm, inspired by swarm decision-making, has had an estimated $50B economic impact.

  • Formal definition slide — have students write it down: emergent behavior resists analytical prediction from parts, but can be expected algorithmically (simulated, not derived)
  • Three fingerprints to recognize a complex system in the wild:
    • High sensitivity to boundary/initial conditions → irreducible uncertainty
    • Power-law-dominated behavior (not bell-curve) → path dependence, heavy-tailed outliers
    • Representable as a network of connected elements
  • Complex systems nest: bees → hive → beekeeping union → society → global economy — each level itself complex, made of complex parts
  • → Concrete payoff: Honey Bee Algorithm (inspired by forager allocation) credited with ~$50B economic impact

Representing Systems as Networks

Complexity science shifted focus from isolated components to interactions, networks, and co-evolution.

Network theory explains connectivity patterns in:

  • Social networks
  • Power grids
  • Biological & genetic-regulatory networks

Impact

Structure matters as much as components.

  • Begins the historical arc that closes the lecture: why does any of this matter beyond the examples?
  • Before complexity science: explanation focused on isolated components + universal, equilibrium-based laws
  • Complexity science’s first big methodological move: structure (who’s connected how) carries as much explanatory weight as component properties
  • Network theory = the formal tool from this shift — underlies social networks (who influences whom), power grids (cascading failures), biological/genetic-regulatory networks (genes switching via mutual interaction)
  • → Repeat to students: structure matters as much as components — identical components, different wiring, completely different behavior

Emergence, Criticality, Beyond

Self-organized criticality: many systems naturally evolve to critical states without external tuning. Small events trigger large-scale consequences (earthquakes, market crashes).

Evolution as computation: genetic algorithms mimic evolution to solve optimization problems. Evolution becomes a general problem-solving mechanism, not just a biological process.

Power laws: hidden regularities behind apparently chaotic phenomena, such as city sizes, wealth distribution, and earthquake magnitudes.

Beyond equilibrium: complexity science extends physics to driven, non-equilibrium systems, applicable to biology, economics, and social systems.

  • Compresses several historical contributions — pace this slide slowly
  • Self-organized criticality (concrete sand-pile example next lecture): systems drift on their own toward a critical state where one small trigger (grain of sand, failed line, bad trade) cascades into large-scale consequence → challenges old linear cause-and-effect assumption
  • Evolution-as-computation: genetic algorithms = natural selection as general-purpose optimization, not just biology (engineering design, ML, materials discovery)
  • Power laws: same statistical signature across city sizes, wealth, earthquakes, internet connectivity — looked like unrelated curiosities, complexity science found a common generative mechanism
  • Beyond equilibrium: classical stat mech assumes settling to thermal equilibrium; complexity science applies physics-style rigor to systems perpetually far from it — organisms, economies, societies

The Big Picture Takeaway

Complexity science has:

  • Shifted science from parts to interactions
  • Extended physics beyond equilibrium
  • Unified evolution, networks, and information
  • Explained emergence, power laws, and systemic risk
  • Created tools applicable across biology, economics, and cities

It represents a paradigm shift, not just a new topic.

  • Close with one unifying claim: last two slides aren’t a grab-bag of bolted-on topics — it’s a genuine Kuhnian paradigm shift (change in what questions are askable, what counts as adequate explanation)
  • Before: characterize parts, assume equilibrium, expect linear cause-and-effect
  • After: characterize interactions/structure, expect systems driven far from equilibrium, expect small causes → disproportionate emergent effects
  • → Sets expectations for the course: no formula that outputs “the architecture” — instead, learn to recognize a complex system and reason with this paradigm’s tools (networks, emergence, power laws, adaptive robustness), not classical component-by-component analysis
