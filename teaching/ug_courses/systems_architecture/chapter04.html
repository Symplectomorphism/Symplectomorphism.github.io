Starting Part 2: Analysis of System Architecture

  • Part 2 works with progressively larger systems, but always showcases methods and diagrams that can directly represent architecture
  • We begin with form — the most concrete aspect of a system — and work toward function (Ch. 5) and their mapping into architecture (Ch. 6)
  • This is the direction of reverse engineering: assume the architecture exists, and analyze it. Learning analysis first creates the conditions for successful synthesis.

Tip

This text follows Dov Dori’s Object-Process Methodology (OPM) throughout, our tool of choice for representing architecture.

Open Part 2 by telling students explicitly how this half of the course differs from Part 1. Chapters 1–3 built the general vocabulary of system thinking; Chapters 4 through 8 now apply that vocabulary rigorously, chapter by chapter, to the two fundamental attributes of any system’s architecture: form and function. Chapter 4 tackles form first — deliberately, because form is the more concrete of the two attributes, easier to point at and describe, which makes it the natural starting point before function’s more abstract, transformation-oriented character in Chapter 5. Chapter 6 then shows how form and function combine into architecture itself, defined precisely as the mapping between them.

Name the overall analytical strategy explicitly, since it explains why every example in Chapters 4 and 5 will be an existing system rather than something being newly designed: this is reverse engineering — we assume an architecture already exists and analyze it, rather than synthesizing something new from scratch (that’s Part 3’s job, Chapters 9–13). The pedagogical logic, worth stating plainly: mastering how to analyze existing architecture well is what creates the conditions for being able to synthesize good architecture later — you can’t reliably design something you can’t first competently critique.

The fragment restates the course’s OPM-only policy from Chapter 3, worth reinforcing here since Chapter 4 is where OPM notation really starts getting used in earnest, with real graphical notation (triangles, boxes, arrows) introduced over the next many slides.

Box 4.1 · Definition: Form

Form is the physical or informational embodiment of a system that exists, or has the potential for stable, unconditional existence, for some period of time, and is instrumental in the execution of function. Form includes the entities of form and the formal relationships among the entities. Form exists prior to the execution of function.

  • It is surprisingly difficult to separate form from function — try describing a coffee cup, a pencil, or a notebook without using function words like “handle” or “eraser”
  • Staying entirely in the form domain: “flat cardboard half-circle,” “rubber cylinder,” “metal spiral”
  • Form is what the system is. It is implemented, built, written, composed, manufactured, or assembled

This is the chapter’s central definition, worth reading slowly and unpacking clause by clause, since every later slide leans on it. First clause: form can be physical or informational — this is a deliberately broad definition meant to cover software and organizations just as well as hardware, and it’s why the chapter ends with a software example (bubblesort) rather than stopping at physical systems. Second clause: form “exists, or has the potential for stable, unconditional existence, for some period of time” — this is the first test for form, existence itself; note the careful phrasing “for some period of time” is included specifically so we can discuss the form of historical systems (already existed) or system designs (will exist in the future) using the same language. Third clause: form is “instrumental in the execution of function” — this is the second test, and it’s what connects form to the rest of the course; form isn’t just “stuff that exists,” it’s stuff that enables something to happen. Fourth: “form exists prior to the execution of function” — form comes first, temporally and logically; you need the instrument before you can use it.

Do the class exercise live, since it’s genuinely illuminating: ask students to describe a coffee cup, a pencil, or a spiral notebook out loud, using no function words at all. They’ll find it’s remarkably hard — most people naturally reach for words like “handle” (which describes something’s function, to be held) rather than staying in pure form language. The book’s own examples of staying purely in the form domain are useful models: “flat cardboard half-circle” instead of “handle,” “rubber cylinder” instead of “eraser,” “metal spiral” instead of “binding.” This exercise makes the abstract definition viscerally concrete — form and function are conceptually distinct, but our everyday language constantly blends them together, and separating them cleanly takes real, deliberate effort.

Figure 4.2 · Form Can Be Informational

An airline emergency instruction card — informational architecture, not physical, but still form: it exists, and is instrumental in the function of instructing passengers. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 4.2. © Cabin Safety International Ltd.

This figure is doing important conceptual work: it’s the chapter’s first proof that “form” isn’t limited to physical hardware. The emergency instruction card is form, full stop — it exists (satisfying the first test), and it’s instrumental to the function of instructing passengers what to do in an emergency (satisfying the second test) — even though what it’s made of, physically, matters far less than the information it encodes. Point out the structural detail worth remembering for later: the individual pictures on the card are organized in sequence (reinforced visually by numbering), and at a higher level, groups of sequenced pictures are prefaced by other, smaller pictures or letters and separated into blocks by horizontal rules. This sequencing and grouping is itself a kind of structure — the formal relationships among the card’s elements — which foreshadows Section 4.4 later in the chapter. Also flag that this card will come back explicitly as a metaphor when we reach software form near the end of the chapter — a set of encoded objects that, when “operated” (read) by a human, are interpreted as instructions leading to function, which is exactly the relationship between pseudocode and a computer.

Box 4.2 · Definition: Object

An object is that which has the potential for stable, unconditional existence for some period of time.

  • We represent form as objects and the formal relationships (structure) among them
  • Objects can be informational — anything comprehended intellectually: ideas, thoughts, arguments, instructions, conditions, data
  • Objects have attributes — physical, electrical, or logical characteristics. Some attributes have states: a house’s construction attribute goes from “not built” to “built”

Introduce “object” as the basic representational unit for form, and be precise about a subtlety the book itself flags: this definition of “object” was deliberately phrased to mirror only the first half of the Box 4.1 definition of form (the “exists” part) — not the second half (being instrumental to function, existing prior to function). That means every form is built from objects, but not every object automatically counts as form — an object that doesn’t meet form’s additional criteria (instrumental to function, existing prior to it) is instead what we’ll call an operand in Chapter 5. For now, just remember: objects are the building blocks we use to represent form, and by convention, object names are generally nouns.

Note explicitly that this is not the same “object” as in object-oriented programming, even though the terminology overlaps — a point worth flagging directly since many students will have programming backgrounds and might otherwise assume a one-to-one correspondence. Explain informational objects concretely: Dori (OPM’s creator, whom we’ll credit properly in a moment) defines an informational object as anything that can be comprehended intellectually, which implies existence even without physical tangibility — ideas, thoughts, arguments, instructions, conditions, and data are all examples, and fields like control systems, software, mathematics, and policy make extensive use of purely informational objects.

Introduce attributes and states carefully, since this vocabulary recurs on the next slide’s figure. Objects have characteristics called attributes (physical, electrical, or logical), and some of those attributes can be considered states — values the attribute takes on that can change over the object’s existence. Walk through the book’s own example: as the beach house is being built, its “construction” attribute moves through states from “not built” to “built”; once people move in, its “occupancy” attribute moves from “unoccupied” to “occupied”; once the furnace runs, its “temperature” attribute moves from “cooler” to “warmer.” The combination of all these state values at any moment describes the object’s overall configuration.

Figure 4.3 · OPM Representations of Objects

Left: a simple object. Center: an object with states shown directly. Right: an object with an explicit attribute (linked by the “is characterized by” double-triangle), which itself has states. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 4.3.

Walk through all three OPM object representations shown here carefully, since this is the first real graphical notation students will need to reproduce themselves. On the left: the simplest possible representation, an object shown as a plain rectangle — just naming that something exists, with no further detail. In the center: the same kind of object, but now with its possible states shown directly inside small ovals nested at the bottom of the box — this is a shorthand for “this object has an attribute, and here are its possible state values,” used when you don’t need to name the attribute itself explicitly. On the right, the fullest version: the object box is connected to a separate “Attribute” box via a double-triangle symbol, read as “is characterized by” — and that Attribute box, in turn, has its own state ovals nested beneath it. This third form is more verbose but more precise, since it explicitly names the attribute (not just its states).

Point out the practical guidance for when to use which: the fully explicit right-hand form is preferred when the name of the attribute itself matters to the discussion (for instance, distinguishing a “temperature” attribute from a “construction” attribute); the more compact center form is fine when the states alone are self-explanatory enough that naming the attribute separately would just be visual clutter. It’s also worth a brief aside, since the book mentions it directly: SysML represents objects differently depending on whether they’re physical objects, human operators, or something else — a reminder that OPM’s uniform box notation, treating all objects the same way regardless of type, is one of its distinguishing simplifications relative to SysML’s more differentiated notation.

Decomposition of Form

  • Form is concrete — it decomposes easily, and the aggregation of form traces the decomposition in a simple way
  • OPM represents decomposition with a black triangle: System 0 decomposes into Level 1 objects, which correspondingly aggregate into System 0

Tip

Form will be modeled throughout as objects of form, plus the formal structure among them.

Connect this directly back to Chapter 3’s decomposition and hierarchy tools, now with a specific graphical notation attached. Point out why form specifically decomposes so cleanly, in contrast to function (which we’ll see in Chapter 5 is trickier): form is concrete, so breaking it into pieces, and reassembling those pieces back into the whole, both trace the decomposition in a simple, additive way — no emergence complications the way function has.

Walk through the OPM decomposition diagram precisely, since this exact filled-black-triangle symbol will recur constantly for the rest of the course: a solid black triangle sits between “System 0” (the Level 0 object) and its Level 1 elements, with the triangle’s point touching System 0 — read top-to-bottom as “System 0 decomposes into Element 1.1, Element 1.2, …” and read bottom-to-top as “these elements aggregate into System 0.” This is the graphical vocabulary for exactly the decomposition/aggregation relationship discussed abstractly back in Chapter 2’s Table 2.3 discussion.

The closing fragment is the chapter’s core methodological commitment, worth stating as a standalone sentence students should remember: from here forward, form will always be modeled as a combination of two things — objects of form, and the formal structure (relationships) among those objects. Everything in the rest of the chapter is just working out, in progressively more detail, exactly what those objects and that structure look like for real systems.

The Centrifugal Pump

  • Our reference case for analyzing form: a real engineering system, the centrifugal pump
  • The turning impeller does work on the fluid; the housing diffuses the flow, converting kinetic energy into pressure
  • Chosen because it’s modular: nine parts, none absolutely discrete (like a team) nor fully integral (like the heart) — a genuinely “simple system,” with atomic parts at Level 1

Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 4.5. Source: PumpBiz.com.

Introduce the chapter’s running worked example, and explain the physics briefly before getting into the architecture, since understanding roughly how the pump works makes the later structural relationships more intuitive. A centrifugal pump increases the energy of fluid passing through it: the flow enters along the axis of rotation through the hole in the cover, the turning impeller flings the flow radially outward, doing work on it and increasing its velocity inside the pump; the surrounding housing then contains a passage that slows the flow back down, converting that gained kinetic energy into higher static pressure. Mass is conserved (same fluid mass exits as enters); energy is conserved (electrical power into the motor shows up as increased pressure in the outlet flow).

Explain explicitly why the book selects this particular example, since it’s a deliberate pedagogical choice worth naming: the pump sits at a useful middle point on the “trivially distinct / modular / integral” spectrum from Chapter 2 — not absolutely discrete the way Team X’s three separate people were, but also nowhere near as tightly integral as the human heart. It has exactly nine parts, none sharing functions in a way that would make them hard to pull apart, so — per the sizing guideline from Chapter 3 — it counts as a genuinely “simple system,” with atomic parts already reached at Level 1. Be candid with students about the pedagogical tradeoff here, echoing the book’s own acknowledgment: analyzing something this simple with the full machinery of system architecture can feel like using a sledgehammer on a thumbtack — but that’s exactly the point. Working through every one of the six Table 4.1 questions on a genuinely simple, fully-graspable system lets students see the method clearly, before applying it to something they can’t hold entirely in their heads at once.

Defining the System (Question 4a)

  • The procedure: examine the system and create an abstraction of form that conveys the important information and implies a boundary
  • For the pump, we create the abstraction “Pump” — surfaces the idea of something that moves fluid, hides all detail of motors and impellers
  • We could have chosen “centrifugal pump” instead — a more specific abstraction (other pump types include axial-flow and positive-displacement pumps)

This gives us a specialization relationship: Pump ← (unfilled triangle) → Centrifugal Pump.

Start working through Table 4.1’s six questions in earnest, beginning with Question 4a. State the general procedure precisely, since it applies to every system students will ever analyze, not just the pump: examine the system, and create an abstraction of form that conveys the important information while implying a boundary consistent with the more detailed boundary we’ll draw later, when answering Question 4e.

For the pump specifically, we create the abstraction simply called “Pump” — this surfaces the essential idea (something that moves fluid) while hiding all the internal detail about motors, impellers, housings, and so on; it also implicitly draws a system boundary around the parts shown in the earlier exploded-view figure, separating the pump itself from things like the connecting hoses, mechanical supports, and the motor’s power/control supply, which sit just outside.

Point out that “Pump” wasn’t the only reasonable choice — we could equally have chosen the more specific abstraction “centrifugal pump” directly, rather than the more general “Pump.” Mention the two other major pump types named in the book for concreteness — axial-flow pumps and positive-displacement pumps — to make clear “Pump” really is a genuine generalization with multiple real specializations, not an arbitrary label. The fragment names this relationship formally using vocabulary from Chapter 3: Pump is the generalization, Centrifugal Pump is one specialization of it, and the next slide shows exactly how OPM represents that graphically.

Identifying the Entities of Form

  • Question 4b: start with a reference parts list, then combine or eliminate elements, and use hierarchy to find the most important ones
  • The motor has a non-rotating element and a rotating motor shaft — different enough functionally that we split it into two objects, even though the parts list says “motor”
  • A careful look reveals five screws — but we abstracted just one class, “Screw,” with five instances

Tip

Hierarchy can rank the ten elements too: cover/impeller/housing/motor shaft/motor carry the most scope and function; O-ring/seal/water-slinger are mid-rank; screws and locking nut are least important.

This is Question 4b, and give the general procedure first: start with a reference parts list as your initial set of abstractions, then combine or eliminate elements where appropriate, and use hierarchy to identify which elements are actually most important. For the pump, the manufacturer’s own parts list (the numbered items in the exploded-view figure) is a natural starting point — but it isn’t used completely unmodified.

Explain the motor/motor-shaft split concretely, since it’s a genuinely instructive example of thoughtful abstraction-building rather than blind parts-list copying: the parts list simply says “motor,” but a motor actually contains both a non-rotating housing element and a rotating shaft, and — anticipating function, which we haven’t formally covered yet but can reason about informally — these two parts clearly play different functional roles (some other elements connect to the stationary motor body, others connect to the spinning shaft). Calling all of this a single object named “the motor” would hide too much important information, violating the “surface important detail” abstraction guideline from Chapter 2. So we deliberately create two separate objects instead, even though the manufacturer’s parts list lumps them together.

Explain the opposite move with the screws, since it’s the mirror-image lesson: a careful look at the exploded view reveals five separate physical screws, yet the decomposition in the previous figure identifies only one abstraction, “Screw.” This is the class/instance relationship from Chapter 3 being used implicitly: we created a class called “Screw,” and the five actual screws are simply five instances of that one class — there’s no architectural value in treating them as five individually-tracked, differently-named objects.

The fragment previews that hierarchy (also from Chapter 3) can be layered on top of this same set of ten elements to further prioritize them: cover, impeller, housing, motor shaft, and motor carry the most scope and functional importance; the O-ring, seal, and water slinger form a middle rank; and the screws (as a class) and the locking nut are least important. This ranking is the basis for the simplified five-element structural diagrams coming up shortly in Section 4.4 — we’ll deliberately focus analysis on just these top five elements rather than tracking all ten.

Section 4.2–4.3 Summary

  • Analyzing form requires creating an abstraction that conveys the important information without too much detail, and implies a system boundary
  • The elements of form can be represented as a hierarchic decomposition — a set of objects representing first- and second-level abstractions
  • Level 1 abstractions are not unique — the pump assembly / motor assembly split could just as easily have been rotating / non-rotating components

Use this as a checkpoint before the chapter shifts from “what are the elements of form” (Questions 4a and 4b, just covered) to “how are those elements related” (Question 4c, structure, coming up next in Section 4.4). By this point students should be comfortable creating a top-level abstraction of any system’s form, decomposing it hierarchically into first- and second-level elements, and — this is the point worth re-emphasizing, since it recurs as a theme throughout the whole course — recognizing that the specific choice of Level 1 grouping is never the only valid one. The pump/motor-assembly split versus a rotating/non-rotating split is a clean, memorable illustration students can hold onto: both are legitimate, useful abstractions of the exact same underlying ten elements, chosen for different analytical purposes.

4.4 Formal Relationships

Box 4.4 · Definition: Formal Relationships (Structure)

Formal relationships, or structure, are the relationships between objects of form that have the potential for stable, unconditional existence for some duration of time and may be instrumental in the execution of functional interactions.

  • Structure is not conveyed by a decomposition diagram alone — a random pile of parts doesn’t tell you how to assemble them
  • Structure shows where the elements of form are located and how they are connected
  • Formal relationships often carry functional interactions — if A supplies power to B, there is likely a connecting wire (the structure)

This section-header slide moves us to Question 4c: what is the formal structure? Give the definition carefully, and note it deliberately echoes Box 4.1’s definition of form itself — structure, like form, is about things that exist stably for some duration, not things that happen or change moment to moment. The phrase “may be instrumental in the execution of functional interactions” is the crucial link forward to Chapter 6, foreshadowed here: structure often, though not always, exists specifically to enable something functional to happen.

Make the “decomposition alone isn’t enough” point vivid with a concrete image: if you were simply handed a random pile of mechanical parts, or a set of loose lines of code, with only a decomposition list telling you what the pieces are — you still wouldn’t know how to assemble them into a working whole. What’s missing is exactly the structural information: where each element of form is physically located, and how it’s connected to the others. This is precisely why Chapter 4 needs a dedicated section on structure, distinct from the decomposition work already covered — decomposition tells you the parts, structure tells you how they fit together.

The last bullet previews the crucial practical reason we care about structure at all, beyond mere description: formal relationships frequently serve as the carrier for functional interactions (Chapter 5’s subject). If object A supplies electrical power to object B, there’s almost certainly a physical wire connecting them — that wire is the structure, and the power flow is the function it enables. This is exactly the “Formal Enables Functional” idea from Chapter 2, now given a name (structure) and about to be broken down into specific types over the next several slides.

Two Kinds of Structural Relationship

Spatial / topological

Where things are: location or placement — above/below, near/far, within, adjacent to, encircling. Implies only placement, not the ability to transmit anything.

Connectivity

What is connected, linked, or joined to what. Explicitly creates the ability to transfer or exchange something between objects — a wire, a shared address, a bearing.

Tip

The key question for either type: “Is this relationship key to some important functional interaction, or to the successful emergence of function and performance?”

Introduce the chapter’s two main structural-relationship categories, and be precise about the distinction, since it’s genuinely subtle in physical systems (though sharper in some other domains). Spatial/topological relationships describe where things are, relative location and placement — above, below, near, far, within, adjacent to, encircling, and so on. Crucially, they say nothing on their own about the ability to transmit or exchange anything — two objects can be spatially near each other, even touching, without that implying any functional coupling at all. Note that “spatial” (capturing absolute or relative location/orientation, sometimes with reference to a coordinate system) and “topological” (capturing placement-type relationships like within, surrounded by, adjacent to) are subtly distinct concepts, but in practice the book treats them together as one combined category, since the difference is often very fine-grained.

Connectivity relationships, by contrast, explicitly describe what is connected, linked, or joined to what — and this category genuinely does create the ability to transfer or exchange something between the connected objects: a wire that current can flow through, a shared memory address that data can be written to and read from, a bearing that allows rotational force to be transmitted. Give students a good discriminating example for the fine distinction between the two: two houses in a neighborhood can be spatially adjacent without being connected (no shared plumbing, wiring, etc.); conversely, a computer and a remote server can be connected (over a network) despite having entirely unspecified relative location.

The fragment gives the single practical filter for deciding, for any given pair of objects, whether a spatial or connectivity relationship between them is even worth including in your analysis at all — echoing the “focus” principle from Chapter 2. Holistically, you could claim some spatial relationship exists between literally every pair of objects in a system (everything is somewhere, relative to everything else) — that’s far too much information to be useful. The actual filtering question to apply, pairwise, is whether this particular relationship is key to some important functional interaction, or to the successful emergence of function and performance. If not, leave it out of the diagram.

Figure 4.13 · OPM Structure Notation

A binary link, drawn as a single-headed arrow with a label: “the housing surrounds the impeller.” The direction of the arrow is arbitrary — it implies no exchange, interaction, or causality, just a relationship that exists. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 4.13.

Introduce the graphical convention students will use for every structural relationship diagram from here forward: a single-headed arrow drawn directly from one object to another, labeled in plain language describing the relationship. Walk through the pump example concretely, grounding it back in the exploded-view figure: examining the pump, we observe that the housing surrounds the impeller without directly touching it — this is drawn as an arrow from “Housing” to “Impeller,” labeled “Surrounds,” read naturally as “the housing surrounds the impeller.”

Emphasize the point that trips students up most often with this notation, since the book itself flags it explicitly: the direction of the arrow is entirely arbitrary and carries no implication of causality, exchange, or interaction whatsoever — it’s simply a relationship that exists, full stop. We could just as validly have drawn the reverse arrow, from Impeller to Housing, labeled “Is within,” and it would mean exactly the same thing (“the impeller is within the housing”). By convention we pick one direction to draw explicitly and leave the reverse reading implicit, but the underlying relationship is symmetric in meaning even when the label wording differs between the forward and backward readings (sometimes the same word works both ways too — “the cover is aligned with the motor shaft” reverses cleanly to “the motor shaft is aligned with the cover”). This arbitrariness of direction is a genuinely important conceptual point to contrast against functional relationships in Chapter 5, where direction will matter (a process really does act on an operand in a specific direction) — so it’s worth flagging now as something to watch for the contrast later.

The Design Structure Matrix (DSM)

DSM: an N-squared matrix used to map the connections between one element of a system and the others. Read down the column to the relationship at the row heading.

  • The DSM contains the same information as the graphical OPM view — just tabulated instead of drawn
  • Graphical views are easier to develop and visualize; matrix views handle complexity without visual clutter, and are easier to compute on
  • We’ll use both throughout this course

Introduce the DSM as the tabular counterpart to the graphical OPM diagrams just covered, connecting directly back to the N-Squared tables from Chapter 2. Be precise about what the acronym stands for and its reading convention, since students will use this repeatedly: DSM generally stands for Design Structure Matrix, though the same acronym is sometimes used for Decision Structure Matrix or Dependency Structure Matrix depending on context — it’s an N-by-N matrix, N being the number of elements, and the reading convention this text consistently uses is: read down the column to find the relationship at the row heading — meaning things conceptually “flow” from the column heading to the row heading.

Emphasize that a DSM built from the same pairwise analysis as an OPM diagram contains exactly the same underlying information, just represented differently — a DSM version of the simplified five-element pump structure (cover, impeller, housing, motor, motor shaft) would show precisely the same “touches,” “aligned with,” and “close to” relationships as Figure 4.14, just as matrix cells instead of arrows. It’s worth noting, since it comes up on a later slide, that structural DSMs like these are symmetric except when the forward and backward relationship labels genuinely use different words (like “slid onto” versus “had slid onto”) — since structure just exists, without direction or causality.

Give students the practical tradeoff between the two representations plainly, since we’ll use both throughout the rest of the course depending on the situation: graphical (OPM) representations are generally easier to develop and to visualize at a glance, especially for smaller systems — but they get visually cluttered fast as the number of elements and relationships grows. Matrix (DSM) representations handle that same complexity without visual clutter (they just get bigger, not messier), and they’re considerably easier to operate on computationally — sorting, clustering, and searching a matrix is straightforward in a way that untangling a messy diagram isn’t. Neither representation is strictly better; which one to reach for depends on the system’s size and on whether a human needs to read it directly or a computer needs to process it.

Other Formal Relationships

Beyond spatial/topological and connectivity, several other relationship types simply exist:

  • Address — where something can be found (a memory address, a mailing address)
  • Sequence — a static ordering (statement 2 is always after statement 1 in the code)
  • Membership — being part of a group or class
  • Ownership — a static relationship between an owner and the owned
  • Human relationships — trust, liking, bonds between people (uniquely, not always reciprocal)

Tip

All formal relationships are static at any instant — but they can change: connections made and broken, addresses reassigned, membership revoked.

Round out the catalog of formal relationship types beyond the two main categories (spatial/topological and connectivity) already covered — these five are less central to the pump example but matter a great deal in other domains, especially software and organizational systems, so it’s worth giving each a moment. Address describes where something is in an encoded sense — it’s really a specialized, encoded form of spatial relationship, common enough to be worth naming separately, and it matters especially in software, where memory addresses and registers are constantly referenced. Sequence describes a static ordering among entities — if statement 2 is always positioned after statement 1 in a piece of code, that ordering relationship holds true regardless of whether the program is currently executing; note carefully, since this is a subtle but important point the book makes: in an imperative programming language, statement 2 being written after statement 1 in the source implies it also executes after statement 1 — but that implication doesn’t necessarily hold in a declarative language, where source-code ordering and execution ordering can differ. What’s always true, regardless of language paradigm, is the purely structural fact that “2 follows 1 in the written code” — that’s a formal (structural) statement, distinct from the functional claim about execution order.

Membership describes being part of some group or class — “A is a member of Group 1” is itself an abstraction signifying group belonging; it may or may not lead to actual interaction or responsibility (contrast this against the “class/instance” logical relationship from Chapter 3, which membership resembles but isn’t identical to). Ownership is a similarly abstract but very common relationship: “if you own something, it is yours” — the book poses a nice thought-provoking question worth asking students directly: what really separates your land from your neighbor’s land, or your money from someone else’s? Only, ultimately, that it was at some point in the past purchased by you or granted to you — but once that transaction has happened, ownership becomes a genuine static formal relationship between owner and owned item, existing independent of any ongoing action. And human relationships — trust, liking, the various bonds between people — are worth calling out as genuinely unique among all these relationship types: unlike every other formal relationship discussed, human relationships are not necessarily reciprocal. You might trust a public figure who has no knowledge of you at all; that’s a one-directional bond in a way that “is connected to” or “is aligned with” never could be for a physical connection.

The closing fragment ties the whole catalog together with an important qualifier: every formal relationship discussed in this section — spatial, connectivity, address, sequence, membership, ownership, human bonds — is static at any given instant in time: it simply exists, or doesn’t, at that moment. But “static at an instant” doesn’t mean permanent — all of these relationships genuinely can change over time: spatial location can be changed by physically moving something; connections can be made and broken (a lamp plugged into, then unplugged from, an outlet); addresses can be reassigned; sequence can be changed by reordering; group membership exists until members quit; ownership changes through gift-giving or sale. And — worth noting as a memorable closing detail — human bonds are described as the single most fickle of all formal relationships, capable of deteriorating in an instant, in a way none of the more mechanical relationship types typically do.

Section 4.4 Summary

  • Form consists of objects and structure — the formal relationships among them. Both must be considered in analysis.
  • Three broad types of formal relationship: connection (carries functional interaction), location/placement, and intangible (membership, ownership, human bonds)
  • Formal relationships inform and influence the nature of functional interaction and the emergence of function and performance
  • Formal relationships are static in that they exist — although they can be changed

Use this as the checkpoint closing out Section 4.4, before the chapter moves outward to context in Section 4.5. Reorganize the whole section’s content, one more time, into the book’s own three-way grouping of formal relationships, since this is a cleaner summary taxonomy than the slide-by-slide sequence we just worked through: connection-type relationships (which create the formal channel over which functional interaction can take place — this covers connectivity specifically), location-and-placement-type relationships (spatial/topological relations, address, and sequence — these describe where things are, in either a literal or an encoded sense), and intangible relationships (membership, ownership, human bonds — abstractions that don’t correspond to any physical channel at all).

Reinforce the two big-picture takeaways one more time before moving on: formal relationships matter specifically because they inform and influence what functional interactions are possible and how function and performance emerge (this is the forward-looking link to Chapter 5 and Chapter 6) — and, distinctly, formal relationships are fundamentally about existence, not occurrence; they simply are, at any given moment, even though — as the previous slide’s closing fragment established — they absolutely can and do change over time.

4.5 Formal Context

Accompanying Systems and the Whole Product System

  • Another application of holistic thinking: take an increasingly broad view of the system and its context
  • The accompanying systems: objects not part of the product/system, but essential for it to deliver value
  • The sum of the product/system and its accompanying systems is the whole product system

Tip

For the pump: the inflow and outflow hoses, the pump support structure, and the power/controller are all accompanying systems — without them, the pump delivers no value.

This section-header slide addresses Questions 4d and 4e together, and it’s explicitly framed as another application of the Principle of Holism from Chapter 2 — now specifically applied to form, expanding outward from the system itself to its surrounding context, one deliberate step at a time. The procedure: look holistically at objects of form that are near the system, or connected to it, and ask “are these essential to the delivery of product/system value?” If yes, they’re accompanying systems, and we create abstractions for them and identify the formal structure connecting them to our system, exactly the way we did for the system’s own internal elements.

Define the key vocabulary precisely: accompanying systems are objects that are not part of the product/system itself, but are essential for it to actually deliver value — they must be present, connected, and working, for the product/system to function as intended. The sum of the product/system plus its accompanying systems together is called the whole product system.

Ground this immediately in the pump example, via the fragment: on their own, the ten elements of the centrifugal pump we’ve analyzed so far can’t do anything useful — there’s no value delivered by a pump sitting disconnected on a shelf. At minimum, you need the inflow and outflow hoses to actually carry fluid to and from the pump, a pump support structure to hold the assembly in place, and a power/controller to supply and manage the motor’s electricity. None of these are part of the pump itself, but without all of them present and connected, the pump delivers zero value — which is exactly what makes them accompanying systems rather than mere context.

Figure 4.17 · The Pump Whole Product System

The dashed line is the product/system boundary. Including the operator reminds the architect to consider human interaction with the system. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 4.17.

Walk through this diagram as the concrete payoff of the previous slide’s discussion. The pump’s whole product system decomposes into six elements total: the centrifugal pump itself (shown with a dashed border, marking it specifically as the product/system boundary within this larger diagram), plus five accompanying systems — the inflow hose, outflow hose, pump support, power/controller, and — notably — an operator.

Spend a moment specifically on why the operator is included here, since it’s a deliberate and instructive choice: most of the time, delivering real value from a system requires a human involved somewhere in the loop, and the book recommends it as good practice to explicitly include the operator in diagrams like this one, specifically as a reminder to the architect not to neglect human interaction with the system when reasoning about value delivery — an easy thing to overlook if you’re purely focused on the hardware.

Point out that this decomposition-style view, while useful for enumerating what the accompanying systems are, deliberately shows no structural information at all — no connections, no spatial relationships between these six elements. That’s exactly the gap the next figure (referenced but not separately slotted as its own slide here — Figure 4.18 in the book) fills, by redrawing this same whole product system as a connectivity diagram instead, showing explicitly how the pump, hoses, support, controller, and operator are all physically linked to each other and where the product/system boundary crossings — the interfaces — actually occur. It’s worth mentioning to students that drawing the product/system boundary clearly, as the dashed line does here, is good practice generally: it reduces ambiguity and makes every interface the architect needs to manage explicit rather than implicit.

The Use Context

  • One more step outward: the use context — objects normally present when the system operates, but not necessary for it to deliver value
  • Use context informs the function of the product/system — it gives place to the system and informs its design
  • Even though the architect is only responsible for the product/system, they will likely be held accountable for the function of the whole product system regardless

It’s important to understand about two levels down in decomposition — and about two levels out in context: the whole product system and the use context.

This is Question 4f, the last and outermost of Table 4.1’s six questions, and it’s genuinely distinct from the accompanying-systems idea just covered — make that distinction explicit, since it’s easy to blur the two. Accompanying systems (previous slide) are essential for value delivery — the pump literally cannot deliver value without its hoses, support, and power/controller. Use context, by contrast, is one step further outward still: objects that are normally present when the whole product system operates, but that are not strictly necessary for it to deliver its value. Use context matters for a different reason than accompanying systems do — it informs the function of the system (previewing Chapter 5), gives the system its place, and informs good design choices, even though removing it wouldn’t literally stop the system from working.

Apply this to the pump directly, being honest with students about a genuine limitation the book itself acknowledges: since the drawings available for this reference example give no hints at all about actual use context, we have to speculate. The pump might plausibly be an industrial sump pump used for saltwater removal, or it might be part of a home dishwasher — and the design requirements would differ meaningfully between those two use contexts (saltwater corrosion resistance versus food-safety and noise considerations, for instance), even though the core pumping function and the accompanying-systems analysis would look identical either way.

The middle bullet raises an important, slightly uncomfortable practical point worth dwelling on: even though the architect is formally responsible only for the product/system itself (the pump, not the hoses or the operator), in practice they will very likely be held accountable for the function of the whole product system regardless of that formal boundary. The book’s own example: if a third-party-supplied inflow hose connects poorly to an otherwise excellent, expensive new pump, it’s far more likely that the pump supplier gets blamed for the resulting bad interface than the hose supplier does — accountability tends to flow toward whoever built the more prominent or more expensive piece, fairly or not.

The closing fragment gives students a clean, memorable rule of thumb that nicely mirrors Chapter 3’s decomposition-depth guidance: just as it’s important for an architect to understand roughly two levels down into a system’s decomposition, it’s equally important to understand roughly two levels out into its context — the whole product system (one level out) and the use context (two levels out). Push your understanding exactly that far outward, in both decomposition and context, and you’ll generally have covered what matters most.

4.6 Form in Software Systems

The Bubblesort Algorithm

We review the same procedure on a software system: bubblesort, which sorts an array by successively swapping adjacent out-of-order entries.

1  Procedure bubblesort (List array, number length_of_array)
2      for i = 1 to length_of_array - 1
3          for j = 1 to length_of_array - i
4              if array[j] > array[j+1] then
5                  temporary = array[j+1]
6                  array[j+1] = array[j]
7                  array[j] = temporary
8              end if
9          end of j loop
10     end of i loop
11     return array
12 End procedure

This section-header slide launches the chapter’s second running example, and it’s worth being explicit up front about why the book includes it at all: everything so far has been demonstrated on physical mechanical systems (the pump), and this final section deliberately tests whether the exact same six-question procedure from Table 4.1 also works cleanly on a purely informational system. Be candid, echoing the book’s own framing: bubblesort is, like the pump, a genuinely simple system — far simpler than the tools of system architecture are really intended for — but working through it carefully surfaces some important observations about form that only become visible in the software domain.

Walk through what the algorithm actually does, since some students may not have seen bubblesort before: it sorts an array from smallest to largest by repeatedly comparing adjacent entries and swapping them if they’re out of order, sweeping through the array multiple times until everything settles into place — hence “bubble,” because larger values gradually “bubble up” toward the end of the array with each pass. The nested loop structure shown in the pseudocode (an outer i loop, an inner j loop, with a conditional swap inside) is the entire procedure; we’ll return to specific line numbers from this pseudocode repeatedly over the next several slides, so it’s worth making sure students can follow the control flow before moving on.

What Is the “Object” in Software?

  • Question 4a: the system is obviously the pseudocode itself — but what is the object of form?
  • Box 4.1: form “exists… is instrumental in the execution of function… and exists prior to the execution of function”
  • The code is the form — it exists, it’s implemented (written), and when operated (executed), it’s interpreted as instructions that lead to function
  • The emergency instruction card (Figure 4.2) is a metaphor: a set of objects that, when “operated” by a human reader, are interpreted as instructions

Apply Question 4a to bubblesort, and use this slide to surface a genuinely subtle point the book spends real effort clarifying. Identifying what the system is here is easy — obviously, it’s the pseudocode listed on the previous slide. The harder question is identifying the object of form that represents this system, and this requires students to think carefully about what a “software object” actually is in this book’s framework — explicitly warn students this is not the same thing as a “software object” in the object-oriented-programming sense, even though the terminology overlaps confusingly.

Walk through the reasoning by re-applying Box 4.1’s definition directly: form is that which exists, is instrumental in the execution of function, and exists prior to that function’s execution. Applied to software: the code (or here, the pseudocode) itself is the form. It exists as written text; it’s “implemented” in the sense of having been written down; and when it’s operated — meaning executed by a processor — that form is interpreted as a sequence of instructions that ultimately leads to function (in this case, a sorted array). This is a genuinely useful reframing for students to internalize: source code isn’t a description of the system’s form, the way a floor plan describes a house’s form — the code itself literally is the system’s form, in the same direct sense that the pump’s physical metal parts are the pump’s form.

Reconnect explicitly to Figure 4.2 from earlier in the chapter, the airline emergency instruction card, which was deliberately planted then as a metaphor for exactly this idea: the card is a set of objects (pictures, symbols) that exist, that have been implemented (printed), and that, when “operated” — meaning read and interpreted by a human passenger — lead to function (correct emergency behavior). Software code works exactly the same way, just with a processor doing the interpreting instead of a human eye.

Box 4.7 · Principle of Dualism

“Dualism in philosophy, mind/body, free will/determinism, idealism/materialism appear as contradictory only because of underdeveloped formulation of the concepts involved.” — Hegel’s dialectic, Science of Logic (1812–1816)

  • All built systems inherently and simultaneously exist in the physical and the informational domain
  • “Information systems” are just abstractions of physical objects that store and process information
  • Poems are in print; thoughts are encoded in neural patterns; images are pixels — informational form must always be encoded in some physical form

Introduce this principle as the philosophical payoff of the previous slide’s careful reasoning about software form, and it’s worth reading the Hegel quote slowly, since students may find the philosophical framing initially puzzling — the point is that apparent contradictions between paired concepts (mind/body, physical/informational) often dissolve once you formulate the concepts more carefully, and that’s exactly what this principle does for form.

State the principle’s core claim directly: every built system inherently and simultaneously exists in both the physical domain and the informational domain at once — this isn’t an either/or choice, it’s a genuine duality, both being true descriptions of the same system at the same time. What we casually call “information systems” aren’t a fundamentally different category of thing from physical systems — they’re simply abstractions of underlying physical objects that happen to be organized around storing and processing information (the electrical charges in memory, the printed ink on a page, the optical pits on a disc).

Walk through the examples given, since they’re a nice, varied illustration of the same underlying point across very different physical substrates: poems exist informationally as words and meaning, but that information is always encoded physically — in print, on a page. Thoughts exist informationally as ideas, but are physically encoded in neural firing patterns in a brain. A DVD movie exists informationally as a sequence of frames, but is physically encoded as optical markings on a disc surface. Digital images exist informationally as a picture, but are physically encoded as pixels (numeric values in memory). The unifying lesson, worth stating as the closing takeaway: informational form must always be encoded in some physical form — there is no such thing as pure, disembodied information; when we talk about “information” as a concept, we’re really talking about patterns encoded in some physical substrate, patterns that can be encoded and decoded by whoever (or whatever) needs to use them. This directly explains and justifies the previous slide’s claim that bubblesort’s code — pure information — is nonetheless legitimately “form,” on exactly the same footing as the pump’s physical steel parts.

Structure in Software

  • Question 4b: the principal elements of form are the lines of pseudocode — decomposing further (e.g., into individual characters) adds no useful architectural information
  • Question 4c: for software, the most important spatial/topological relationship is sequence — what executes before what — and containment — what’s nested within what (e.g., inside the if block, inside the j loop)

Move through Questions 4b and 4c together for the bubblesort example, since they’re handled fairly briskly in the book once the software-form idea from a couple of slides ago is established. For Question 4b, the principal elements of form are simply the individual lines of pseudocode — mention as an aside that we could alternatively abstract the code at a coarser grain (grouping the “procedure/end procedure” wrapper, the “if/end if” block, the “for j/end j loop,” and the “for i/end i loop” as four higher-level elements instead of tracking every individual line), but decomposing further than individual lines — down to individual characters, say — adds no additional useful architectural information; it’s past the atomic-part threshold from Chapter 3’s “call it a part if it loses meaning when you take it apart” rule for informational systems.

For Question 4c, the key insight — worth stating as the chapter’s headline observation about software structure — is that in software, spatial/topological relationships take on a more abstract character than in physical systems, since there’s no literal physical geometry to speak of; structure instead shows up implicitly through indentation, code conventions, and language syntax. The single most important spatial/topological relationship in imperative languages specifically is sequence: which lines of code execute before which other lines. A closely related second relationship is containment: what’s nested structurally within what — for instance, the swap logic (lines 5 through 7) is contained within the if conditional test, which is itself contained within the j loop. Both of these — sequence and containment — are genuinely structural (formal) claims about the static, written-down code, distinct from (though closely related to) the functional claim about actual execution order at runtime, a distinction the next figure makes visually explicit.

Figure 4.20 · Structure of Bubblesort

The “precedes” relationship informs transfer of control; “contains” informs what executes conditionally. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 4.20.

Walk through this diagram as the direct graphical payoff of the sequence/containment discussion just covered, using the exact same single-headed-arrow OPM notation from Figure 4.13 earlier, now applied to lines of code instead of physical pump parts. Trace the relationships shown: the if statement block “contains” the three lines that perform the actual swap (temporary = array[j+1], then array[j+1] = array[j], then array[j] = temporary) — and within that contained block, each of those three lines “precedes” the next in a simple sequential chain.

Reinforce precisely what each relationship type tells us functionally, since this is the payoff worth making explicit: the “precedes” (sequence) relationship informs the transfer of control during actual execution — line 5 running before line 6 before line 7. The “contains” relationship informs which lines execute only conditionally — everything inside the if block only runs at all if the surrounding condition (array[j] > array[j+1]) evaluates true. This is a good moment to point students back to Table 4.8 in the book (referenced structurally but not separately slotted as a slide here), which shows this same spatial/topological structure as a full DSM, using “F” for follows, “P” for precedes, “W” for within, and “C” for contains — extending exactly this figure’s relationships across the entire bubblesort procedure, not just the four lines shown here.

Section 4.6 Summary

  • The objects of form for a software system are the code, which (when operated) is interpreted as instructions
  • Software form decomposes into modules, procedures, and eventually lines of code
  • Software structure consists of spatial/topological relationships (informing control flow) and connectivity relationships (informing data/variable flow)
  • The whole product system for software includes the compiler, calling routine, processor, and input/output — and its use context informs requirements for quality, reliability, and maintainability

Use this as the closing checkpoint for the software-form section, and take a moment to fill in the one piece not covered on the immediately preceding slides: connectivity structure in software. Mention explicitly, since it’s genuinely useful information the slides so far haven’t stated directly: the connectivity relationships for bubblesort are associated with the exchange of data or variables — which lines of code share the array itself, the array’s length, the temporary variable, or the loop indices i and j. Point out the book’s own observation, worth passing on: comparing bubblesort’s spatial/topological structure against its connectivity structure shows less overlap between the two than we saw for the pump — in general, it’s possible for spatial/topological and connectivity structure to be more clearly distinct from each other in informational systems than they typically are in physical systems, where things usually have to be physically adjacent in order to be connected at all.

Close by summarizing the whole-product-system and use-context ideas (Questions 4d, 4e, 4f) applied to software, briefly: bubblesort’s accompanying systems include, at minimum, the calling routine that invokes it and the compiler that translates the pseudocode into executable machine instructions — and going further outward, the processor that actually executes those instructions, and the input/output devices used to load and retrieve the arrays. The use context — is this bubblesort meant as a teaching example for students, a component of a production software library, or embedded safety-critical code? — genuinely changes what matters: requirements around quality assurance, reliability, and maintainability would differ substantially across those different use contexts, even though the core sorting algorithm and its form would be identical in every case. This closing point is a nice bridge back to the pump example’s own use-context discussion — the exact same “same system, different context, different requirements” logic applies whether the system is physical or purely informational.
