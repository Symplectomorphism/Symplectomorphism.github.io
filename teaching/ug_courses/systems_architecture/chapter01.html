Systems Architecture · Chapter 1

Aykut C. Satici

A Formative Decision

  • June 1962: NASA commits to a dedicated descent capsule — the Lunar Module — instead of landing the Command/Service Module itself
  • Decided in Apollo’s first year, seven years before the maneuver would fly — before most staff were hired or design contracts awarded

Note

This is architecture: one early decision eliminated countless possible designs and gave every downstream team a starting point.

Apollo 11 LM ascent stage, 1969. Credit: NASA / Michael Collins (public domain).

Open with the story cold, before defining anything. In June 1962 — barely a year into the Apollo program — NASA chose lunar-orbit rendezvous over the two other serious options on the table: direct ascent (a giant single rocket lands the whole stack directly on the Moon) and Earth-orbit rendezvous. The decision meant building a dedicated descent vehicle, the Lunar Module, whose entire job was to separate from the Command/Service Module, land, and then rendezvous back with it in lunar orbit for the ride home.

Make sure students feel how early this was: seven years before the actual landing, before most of the engineers who’d build the LM were even hired, before a single design contract for it existed. Nobody knew the detailed mass budget, the descent engine performance, or the crew’s exact procedures — yet this decision was made anyway, and it could not be undone later without unraveling everything built on top of it.

That’s the whole point of this course in one story: architecture is not the detailed design. It’s the early, hard-to-reverse decision that constrains and enables everything that comes after — made under uncertainty, by people who don’t yet know most of the details. By 1968 the Apollo program consumed over 4% of the entire federal budget — a decision this size, made this early, is exactly the kind of choice we’re going to learn to recognize, evaluate, and make deliberately rather than by accident.

We Build Big, Complex Things

  • Container ships: 480 containers (1950) → 18,000 containers today
  • Cars: ~70 processors on board, connected by up to 5 buses at 1 Mbit/s — vs. 160 bit/s a generation ago
  • Oil platforms: $200–800M each; 39 delivered between 2003 and 2009

Emma Mærsk, once the world’s largest container ship. Credit: Nils Jepsen, CC BY-SA 2.5.

Ground the abstract claim “we build complex things” in numbers students can picture. Container ship capacity grew nearly 40x in about 65 years — from carrying 480 containers in 1950 to 18,000 today; the Emma Mærsk pictured here was once the largest in the world. Modern cars carry roughly 70 onboard processors linked by as many as five separate communication buses running at 1 megabit per second — compare that to automotive electronics a generation ago, communicating at a mere 160 bits per second, literally four orders of magnitude slower. And offshore oil platforms cost between $200 and 800 million apiece, with 39 delivered in just six years (2003–2009) — this is not a niche industry building one-offs, it’s routine, industrial-scale production of enormously complex systems.

The point isn’t just “wow, big numbers” — it’s that conceiving, designing, implementing, and operating systems at this scale is now routine across many industries. That routineness is exactly why we need a discipline, not just intuition, for handling the early decisions that shape these systems.

The heavy-lift ship MV Blue Marlin transporting the 36,000-metric-ton drilling platform SSV Victoria. Source: Dockwise / Rex Features / Associated Press. Reproduced from Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture, Pearson (2016), Fig. 1.1.

This photo is the book’s cover image for a reason — it visually captures “big and complex” in one frame: a heavy-lift transport ship carrying a 36,000-metric-ton drilling platform as literal cargo. Pause on it for a moment; let students absorb the scale before moving on. It’s also a nice segue: this system is big, but is it complex just because it’s big? That’s exactly the distinction the next slide draws.

Complex ≠ Just Big

  • BMW: 1.5 billion potential configurations offered to customers (2004)
  • Augustine’s Law: unit cost of fighter aircraft rose exponentially, 1910–1980 — extrapolated, one aircraft would consume the entire 2053 U.S. defense budget
  • 30 years later, it’s holding up: F-22 Raptor, 2010 — $160M flyaway, $350M including development

F-22 Raptor, 2010. Credit: U.S. Air Force / SrA Gustavo Gonzalez (public domain).

Now sharpen the point: complexity isn’t just physical size. BMW in 2004 calculated it offered its customers 1.5 billion potential configurations — that’s complexity from combinatorial configurability, not from being physically large. Norm Augustine (a former Lockheed Martin CEO) made a satirical but data-grounded observation in his “Augustine’s Laws”: plot the unit cost of a fighter aircraft from 1910 to 1980, and it rises exponentially — extrapolate that trend and by 2053, the entire U.S. defense budget would buy exactly one aircraft. It’s a joke, but it’s a joke built from a real regression on real data.

The punch line the book adds: thirty years after Augustine’s original prediction, it’s still tracking. The F-22 Raptor cost $160 million per unit flyaway in 2010, or $350 million per unit if you amortize the development cost across the fleet — right in line with the extrapolated curve. Ask students: why would cost rise exponentially like this? It’s not that the plane got exponentially bigger — it’s that each generation demands more function, more performance, more configurability, layered onto the last, and that compounds. This is complexity growing from what we ask of a system, independent of its physical size — precisely the theme the course returns to constantly.

Do These Systems Deliver?

Do they meet stakeholder needs and deliver value?

Do they integrate easily, evolve flexibly, and operate simply and reliably?

Well-architected systems do.

This slide is a hinge point — a rhetorical question that turns the conversation from “look how big and complex these systems are” to “so what makes some of them succeed and others fail?” Ask it to the room, let it sit for a second before revealing the fragment. Some complex systems meet stakeholder needs, integrate smoothly, evolve gracefully as requirements change, and operate reliably day to day — and some don’t, despite comparable scale and comparable engineering talent. The book’s answer, which becomes the working thesis of the entire course: well-architected systems do, and the difference traces back to how — and how deliberately — those early, hard-to-reverse decisions were made.

What Is Architecture?

Architecture: an abstract description of the entities of a system and the relationships between those entities.

  • A system’s architecture can be represented as a set of decisions
  • Systems are more likely to succeed if we are careful about identifying and making the decisions that establish that architecture
  • The field of system architecture grew out of practitioners’ attempts to capture expert wisdom from past designs

Give the course’s working definition of architecture precisely, since everything else builds on it: an abstract description of a system’s entities and the relationships between them. Notice this definition doesn’t mention drawings, requirements documents, or CAD models — it’s about entities and relationships, full stop, which is deliberately abstract enough to apply to a building, a piece of software, or an organization equally well.

The premise of the whole text, worth stating explicitly here: architecture can be represented as a set of decisions, and — this is the actionable claim — systems are more likely to succeed if we’re careful about identifying and making those decisions well. That reframes “architecture” from a noun (a static diagram) into a verb (a disciplined process of decision-making).

Historically, this field didn’t emerge from a single academic theory — it grew out of practitioners trying to capture and pass on the hard-won wisdom from past designs, successes and failures both. That’s also why the course leans so heavily on case studies (NPOESS shortly, and many more to come) rather than pure abstraction.

Bet the Company

  • Boeing is half of a global duopoly for large passenger aircraft
  • Boeing “bet the company” on the 787 and its composite-material technology — one product, concentrated risk
  • The mobile device market is far more diversified — yet BlackBerry and Ericsson still declined

Boeing 787-9 Dreamliner. Credit: Anna Zvereva, CC BY-SA 2.0.

Good architectural decisions create competitive advantage. Bad ones can hobble a firm from the outset.

Now connect architecture to business consequence, not just engineering elegance. Boeing operates in a duopoly (with Airbus) for large passenger aircraft — a market where a small number of enormous, multi-year product bets determine a firm’s fate for a decade or more. The 787 program was Boeing effectively betting the company on a new architectural approach: extensive use of composite materials, a new manufacturing and supply-chain model, an all-new fuselage concept. One product, concentrated risk — if the 787’s architecture had failed to deliver, the consequences for Boeing would have been severe.

Contrast that with mobile devices — a market that looks far more diversified, where any one company’s individual product is a much smaller share of total revenue, so in principle the risk of any single bad bet should be more diluted. And yet: BlackBerry and Ericsson, two firms that were once market leaders, still went into serious decline. Ask students why — the book’s implicit answer is that market diversification alone doesn’t protect you if your product architectures repeatedly make the wrong bets (BlackBerry misjudging the shift to touchscreen/app ecosystems is the classic example). The lesson to land on: good architectural decisions create competitive advantage; bad ones can hobble a firm from the very outset of a product’s life, regardless of how diversified the surrounding market looks.

Decisions Cascade

Every system built by humans has an architecture. Early decisions convey how the whole product is organized.

↓
Gearbox
Drivetrain
Suspension
Passenger compartment

Hidden constraints, too: John Deere’s crop sprayer width is constrained by the column separation at the manufacturing site — obvious to the team, but easy to miss in the productivity equation.

Make the cascading-consequences idea concrete with a decision every student can picture: whether to mount a car’s engine transversely (sideways) or longitudinally (front-to-back). That single early choice has direct implications for how the gearbox connects, how the drivetrain routes power to the wheels, how the suspension geometry is laid out, and even how much room is left for the passenger compartment. None of those downstream teams get to design their piece independently of that one early decision — the architecture “conveys a great deal about how the product is organized,” in the book’s words, often before anyone has detailed knowledge of the eventual scope.

The John Deere example brings in a second, subtler idea: hidden constraints from downstream consumption of the product. A crop sprayer’s width is constrained by nothing about the sprayer itself, but by the column separation (the spacing between support columns) at the manufacturing facility where it’s built — a constraint that’s completely obvious to the people working at that site every day, but easy for someone doing a top-down productivity analysis to overlook entirely, because it’s not written into any requirements document. Part of doing architecture well is going and finding these obvious-to-someone, invisible-to-you constraints before they force late and expensive redesign.

Structured Creativity

“Structured creativity is better than unstructured creativity.”

Architecting a system is a soft process — part science, part art. No formula guarantees an optimal solution.

But focusing on decisions (not the underlying designs) lets architects trade off options and order decisions by their leverage on system performance.

State the book’s central methodological assertion plainly, because it explains why this course is organized the way it is. Architecting a system is what the authors call a “soft” process — part science, part art — and they’re explicit that there is no formula, no algorithm, that guarantees an optimal architecture. If you came in hoping for a plug-and-chug procedure, this is the moment to reset that expectation.

What the book does offer instead is a discipline centered on decisions rather than on the underlying designs those decisions represent. Why does that distinction matter? Because talking in terms of decisions — “should we use lunar-orbit rendezvous or direct ascent,” rather than “here is a detailed CAD model of the lander” — lets architects directly trade off broad options against each other, and order those decisions by how much leverage each one has on overall system performance, before committing to any detailed design work. That’s “structured creativity”: not creativity constrained into a formula, but creativity organized around explicit, comparable choices instead of scattered, premature detail. This framing is the thread that ties together everything from Chapter 2 onward.

Case Study: NPOESS

  • 1994: merges two operational weather-satellite programs (civilian + military) — a $1.3B cost-consolidation opportunity
  • The VIIRS instrument: expected to combine three historical instruments’ capability, with less mass and volume than one — assuming complexity would scale linearly. It didn’t.
  • No system architect was appointed to manage the trade

VIIRS “Blue Marble,” Suomi NPP. Credit: NASA/NOAA/GSFC/Norman Kuring (public domain).

Canceled in 2010 — $8.5B over the original $6.5B estimate.

This is the chapter’s cautionary case study, and it’s worth walking through in some detail because it shows what happens when the ideas on the previous slides are ignored. NPOESS — the National Polar-Orbiting Environmental Satellite System — was created in 1994 by merging two existing, separately operated weather-satellite programs: one civilian (run by NOAA), one military (run by the Air Force). The rationale wasn’t crazy — combining them was projected to save $1.3 billion through consolidation, and both programs collected genuinely related weather and cloud-cover data.

Early in the merged program, though, a decision was made to include a superset of instrument capability drawn from both heritage programs. The flagship example is the VIIRS instrument (Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite), which was expected to combine the capability of three separate historical instruments — while using less mass and volume than any one of the three alone. The underlying assumption was that functional complexity would scale roughly linearly with the sum of the two heritage programs’ complexity. It didn’t — combining functions doesn’t just add them up, it creates new integration challenges and emergent difficulties (a preview of “emergence,” which is central to Chapter 2).

A second compounding decision made things worse: rather than deriving needs and concepts directly from the heritage instruments’ architecture, the program listed new functions independently of any system concept — which trapped the architecture’s performance in a corner of the design space that was never reachable with the mass and volume available. And critically: no single system architect was ever appointed to manage this ambitious trade across the merged program. The result: NPOESS was canceled in 2010, at $8.5 billion over its original $6.5 billion estimate — essentially all cost, no working system. Use this as the emotional anchor for “why does this course exist” — bad early architectural decisions, compounded by nobody being responsible for managing the trade, can sink an entire multi-billion-dollar program before a single satellite reaches orbit.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you should be able to:

  • Use system thinking in a product and a system context
  • Analyze and critique existing architectures
  • Distinguish architectural from non-architectural decisions
  • Create the architecture of new or improved systems
  • Place architecture in the context of value and competitive advantage
  • Drive ambiguity out of the upstream process
  • Manage the evolution of system complexity
  • Critically evaluate current modes of architecting

Walk through these as a roadmap for the whole semester, not just a slide to skim. “Use system thinking in a product and a system context” and “analyze and critique existing architectures” are Part 1 and Part 2 territory (Chapters 1–8) — the analytical half of the course. “Distinguish architectural from non-architectural decisions” is a skill students will need constantly: not every design choice is architectural, and part of the discipline is recognizing which few decisions actually deserve this level of scrutiny.

“Create the architecture of new or improved systems” and “manage the evolution of system complexity” point toward Part 3 (Chapters 9–13) — the synthesis half, where students move from analyzing existing systems to designing new ones. “Place architecture in the context of value and competitive advantage” ties back directly to the Boeing/BlackBerry discussion a few slides ago. “Drive ambiguity out of the upstream process” previews a theme that becomes explicit starting in Chapter 9 — early in any program, needs and goals are inherently ambiguous, and part of the architect’s job is systematically reducing that ambiguity. And “critically evaluate current modes of architecting” is an invitation for students not to take even this textbook’s methods as gospel — one stated goal of the course (see the next slide) is for students to develop their own principles as they gain experience, not just memorize the authors’.

Give students the map of the whole course so they know where each week fits. Part 1, System Thinking (Chapters 1–3, where we are right now), introduces the principles of system thinking and the tools for managing complexity, expressed through running examples we’ll reuse constantly: an amplifier circuit, the human circulatory system, a design team, and the solar system. These are deliberately simple and diverse — built, evolved, informational, organizational, mechanical, and natural systems — precisely so we can practice the ideas without getting lost in domain-specific detail first.

Part 2, Analysis of System Architecture (Chapters 4–8), works backwards — starting from the architecture of existing systems and deconstructing form, then function, then introducing solution-neutral function and concept, and finally analyzing full architectures of real systems. We deliberately start with very simple systems here too, not because we think students can’t handle complexity, but to fully master each method before scaling it up.

Part 3, Creating System Architecture (Chapters 9–13), flips direction: it’s the forward process, from identifying needs all the way through choosing an architecture, with no legacy system to analyze — this is where ambiguity is highest, and the part organized around three ideas: reducing ambiguity, applying creativity, and managing complexity.

Part 4, Architecture as Decisions (Chapters 14–16), explores computational methods and tools to help the architect reason through decisions more rigorously — but the book is explicit that no model replaces the architect; models are decision support, not decision replacement.

Principles, Methods, Tools

  • Principles: underlying, long-enduring fundamentals — always (or nearly always) valid
  • Methods: ways of organizing approaches and tasks — solidly grounded on principles, usually or often applicable
  • Tools: contemporary ways to facilitate the process — applicable sometimes

“Principles are general rules and guidelines, intended to be enduring and seldom amended, that inform and support the way in which an organization sets about fulfilling its mission.”

— U.S. Air Force, Principles for Information Management, 1998

This vocabulary — principles, methods, tools — recurs throughout the entire textbook, so it’s worth pinning down precisely now. Principles are the underlying, long-enduring fundamentals of the field: things that are always, or nearly always, true, regardless of domain or era. Methods are ways of organizing approaches and tasks to reach a concrete end — they’re grounded on principles but are only usually or often applicable, since they need to be adapted to context. Tools are the most contingent of the three: contemporary techniques that facilitate the process, applicable only sometimes, and the most likely of the three to become obsolete as technology changes.

The U.S. Air Force quote on the slide captures why principles specifically are called out as special: they’re meant to be enduring and seldom amended. Worth mentioning a second quote the book pairs with it, from James Fenimore Cooper’s 1838 book The American Democrat: “Principles become modified in practice by facts.” That’s a useful tension to leave students with — principles are supposed to be enduring, yet even enduring principles get refined by contact with real experience. One of the book’s explicit goals (and this course’s) is for students to develop their own principles of system architecture as they progress and gain hands-on experience — the frameworks presented here are a scaffold, not a cage.

Chapter 2 formalizes “system thinking” as a discipline built around four sequential tasks, applied to simple systems first: identify the system itself along with its form and function; identify its constituent entities, each of which also has its own form and function; identify the relationships — both formal and functional — among those entities and across the system’s boundary; and finally, identify what emergent properties result from all of that. These four tasks will structure the entire next lecture, and we’ll use the same four running examples introduced a few slides ago — the amplifier circuit, the design team, the circulatory system, and the solar system — to make each task concrete before we scale up to genuinely complex systems in Chapter 3.
