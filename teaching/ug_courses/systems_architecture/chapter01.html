A Formative Decision

  • June 1962: NASA commits to a dedicated descent capsule — the Lunar Module — instead of landing the Command/Service Module itself
  • Decided in Apollo’s first year, seven years before the maneuver would fly — before most staff were hired or design contracts awarded

Note

This is architecture: one early decision eliminated countless possible designs and gave every downstream team a starting point.

Apollo 11 LM ascent stage, 1969. Credit: NASA / Michael Collins (public domain).

  • Open with the story cold, before defining anything
  • June 1962, barely a year into Apollo: NASA chose lunar-orbit rendezvous over direct ascent (one giant rocket lands the whole stack) and Earth-orbit rendezvous
  • Meant building a dedicated descent vehicle — the Lunar Module — whose only job: separate, land, rendezvous back in lunar orbit
  • Make students feel how early this was: seven years before the landing, before most LM engineers were hired, before any design contract existed — nobody knew mass budget, engine performance, or crew procedures, yet the decision was locked in and couldn’t be undone later without unraveling everything built on top
  • Discussion: What made this decision architectural rather than merely technical? Look for breadth of downstream consequences, longevity, and difficulty of reversal.
  • Discussion: How can an architect make a responsible commitment when many details are still unknown? The chapter’s answer is not to wait for certainty, but to identify and scrutinize the high-leverage decisions explicitly.
  • → Whole course in one story: architecture isn’t detailed design — it’s the early, hard-to-reverse decision that constrains and enables everything after, made under uncertainty. By 1968 Apollo consumed over 4% of federal outlays — this is exactly the scale of decision we’re learning to recognize, evaluate, and make deliberately.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, p. 16. - Image: NASA / Michael Collins, Apollo 11 Lunar Module ascent stage (public domain).

We Build Big, Complex Things

  • Container ships: 480 containers (1950) → 18,000 containers today
  • Cars: ~70 processors on board, connected by up to 5 buses at 1 Mbit/s — vs. 160 bit/s a generation ago
  • Oil platforms: $200–800M each; 39 delivered between 2003 and 2009

Emma Mærsk, once the world’s largest container ship. Credit: Nils Jepsen, CC BY-SA 2.5.

  • Ground “we build complex things” in numbers students can picture
  • Container ships: capacity grew ~40x in ~65 years — 480 containers (1950) → 18,000 today; Emma Mærsk pictured was once the world’s largest
  • Cars: ~70 onboard processors, up to 5 buses at 1 Mbit/s — vs. 160 bit/s a generation ago, four orders of magnitude slower
  • Oil platforms: $200-800M each, 39 delivered in just six years (2003-2009) — routine, industrial-scale, not one-offs
  • These examples express three different burdens: physical scale (ships), distributed informational complexity (cars), and capital intensity (platforms). Avoid collapsing all three into a single idea of “bigness.”
  • Discussion: Which burden creates the hardest architectural decisions in each example? Which one is least visible to a customer or operator?
  • → Not “wow, big numbers” — conceiving, designing, implementing, and operating at this scale is now routine across industries. That routineness is why we need a discipline, not just intuition, for the early decisions shaping these systems.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, p. 16. - Image: Nils Jepsen, Emma Mærsk (CC BY-SA 2.5).

Extraordinary Scale Has Become Routine

The heavy-lift ship MV Blue Marlin transporting the 36,000-metric-ton drilling platform SSV Victoria. Source: Dockwise / Rex Features / Associated Press. Reproduced from Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture, Pearson (2016), Fig. 1.1.

  • What the book actually provides: Figure 1.1 is identified only by its caption; the chapter does not give the image a separate explanatory paragraph. Its placement visually reinforces the surrounding claim that enormous, expensive systems are now conceived, built, transported, and operated almost routinely.
  • Heavy-lift ship MV Blue Marlin is carrying the 36,000-metric-ton drilling platform SSV Victoria as cargo. Pause before explaining; the image should establish scale first.
  • Architectural reading of the image: two independently useful complex systems temporarily become one coupled transport system. The mission depends on their interface — deck footprint, load paths, stability, ballast, clearances, loading sequence, and operating environment.
  • The interface constrains both parties even though neither system’s primary function is “be part of the other.” This previews later ideas about boundaries, context, relationships, and emergence.
  • Discussion: Where is the system boundary while the platform is in transit? Is the system the ship, the platform, the combined transport configuration, or the wider logistics operation? The answer depends on the question being analyzed.
  • → Segue: physical magnitude is striking, but complexity also comes from configuration, interaction, and what stakeholders demand — the distinction developed on the next slide.

Complex ≠ Just Big

  • BMW: 1.5 billion potential configurations offered to customers (2004)
  • Augustine’s Law: unit cost of fighter aircraft rose exponentially, 1910–1980 — extrapolated, one aircraft would consume the entire 2053 U.S. defense budget
  • 30 years later, it’s holding up: F-22 Raptor, 2010 — $160M flyaway, $350M including development

F-22 Raptor, 2010. Credit: U.S. Air Force / SrA Gustavo Gonzalez (public domain).

  • Sharpen the point: complexity isn’t just physical size
  • BMW, 2004: 1.5 billion potential configurations offered to customers — complexity from combinatorial configurability, not physical size
  • Augustine’s Law: unit cost of fighter aircraft, 1910-1980, rises exponentially — extrapolated, one aircraft consumes the entire 2053 U.S. defense budget. Satirical, but a real regression on real data
  • Punch line: 30 years later, still tracking — F-22 Raptor, 2010, $160M flyaway / $350M with amortized development, right on the curve
  • Treat Augustine’s extrapolation as a warning about a trend, not a physical law or literal forecast.
  • Discussion: Why might cost grow faster than visible size? Candidate mechanisms include tighter performance envelopes, more interfaces, verification burden, novelty, and the compounding cost of integration.
  • Discussion: BMW’s configurations are not 1.5 billion completely independent designs. What architectural modularity and interface discipline make that variety producible?
  • → Complexity grows from what we ask of a system, not only how large it is — a theme the course returns to constantly.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, pp. 16-17. - Image: U.S. Air Force / SrA Gustavo Gonzalez, F-22 Raptor (public domain).

Do These Systems Deliver?

Do they meet stakeholder needs and deliver value?

Do they integrate easily, evolve flexibly, and operate simply and reliably?

Well-architected systems do.

  • Hinge point — rhetorical question turning “look how big/complex” into “what makes some succeed and others fail?” Ask the room, let it sit before revealing the fragment
  • Some complex systems meet needs, integrate smoothly, evolve gracefully, operate reliably — some don’t, despite comparable scale and talent
  • Use the four verbs as evaluation lenses: deliver value, integrate, evolve, and operate. A system may excel on one and fail on another.
  • Discussion: Name a system that performs its primary function well but is difficult to integrate, evolve, or operate. Would you still call it well architected?
  • The authors later add an important limit: getting the architecture wrong can sink a system, but getting it right only creates a platform on which execution can flourish or flounder. Architecture is necessary, not sufficient.
  • → Working thesis of the whole course: well-architected systems are more likely to succeed because their early, consequential decisions are identified and made deliberately.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, pp. 16-19.

What Is Architecture?

Architecture: an abstract description of the entities of a system and the relationships between those entities.

  • A system’s architecture can be represented as a set of decisions
  • Systems are more likely to succeed if we are careful about identifying and making the decisions that establish that architecture
  • The field of system architecture grew out of practitioners’ attempts to capture expert wisdom from past designs
  • Give the working definition precisely — everything else builds on it: architecture = an abstract description of a system’s entities and the relationships between them
  • No mention of drawings, requirements docs, or CAD models — just entities and relationships, deliberately abstract enough for a building, software, or an organization alike
  • Premise of the whole text: architecture can be represented as a set of decisions — systems are more likely to succeed if we’re careful about identifying and making those decisions well
  • Abstraction is purposeful: the entities and relationships worth showing depend on the question. An architecture is not “all information about the system.”
  • The chapter notes that analysis and computation opened a tradespace of options that, in many areas, grew faster than our ability to understand it. The architect’s job includes making that tradespace comprehensible.
  • Discussion: Can two different architectural descriptions of the same system both be valid? Yes, if they expose different entities and relationships for different decisions — but each should state its purpose and boundary.
  • → Reframes “architecture” from a static diagram toward disciplined decision-making, while the resulting description remains a noun that communicates those decisions.
  • The field grew out of practitioners capturing hard-won wisdom from past designs, not from a single universal theory — one reason the course leans on cases.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, p. 17.

Bet the Company

  • Boeing is half of a global duopoly for large passenger aircraft
  • Boeing “bet the company” on the 787 and its composite-material technology — one product, concentrated risk
  • The mobile device market is far more diversified — yet BlackBerry and Ericsson still declined

Boeing 787-9 Dreamliner. Credit: Anna Zvereva, CC BY-SA 2.0.

Good architectural decisions create competitive advantage. Bad ones can hobble a firm from the outset.

  • Connect architecture to business consequence, not just engineering elegance
  • Boeing: half of a duopoly (with Airbus) for large passenger aircraft — a few enormous multi-year bets determine a firm’s fate for a decade+
  • 787 program = Boeing betting the company on a new architecture: composites, new manufacturing/supply-chain model, all-new fuselage concept — one product, concentrated risk
  • Contrast: mobile devices look far more diversified, any one product a smaller revenue share, risk should be diluted — yet BlackBerry and Ericsson, once market leaders, still declined
  • The market adds simultaneous pressures: innovate the offering, incorporate novel technology, serve multiple markets, minimize manufacturing cost, and coordinate multi-tier supply chains. Architecture is where many of those tensions meet.
  • Discussion: Which 787 choices are product-architecture decisions, and which are enterprise or supply-chain architecture decisions? Where do those layers interact?
  • Do not overclaim the mobile example: the chapter cites BlackBerry and Ericsson’s declines as evidence that diversified product markets provide little comfort; it does not diagnose a single architectural cause.
  • → Good architectural decisions can create competitive advantage; bad ones can hobble a development from the outset, regardless of how diversified the market appears.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, pp. 17-18. - Image: Anna Zvereva, Boeing 787-9 Dreamliner (CC BY-SA 2.0).

Decisions Cascade

Every system built by humans has an architecture. Early decisions convey how the whole product is organized.

Mount the engine transversely
↓
Gearbox
Drivetrain
Suspension
Passenger compartment

Hidden constraints, too: John Deere’s crop sprayer width is constrained by the column separation at the manufacturing site — obvious to the team, but easy to miss in the productivity equation.

  • Concrete cascading-consequences example: mount a car engine transversely vs. longitudinally
  • One early choice → direct implications for gearbox connection, drivetrain routing, suspension geometry, passenger-compartment room
  • No downstream team designs their piece independently of that one decision — architecture “conveys a great deal about how the product is organized,” often before anyone knows the eventual scope
  • John Deere example — subtler idea: hidden constraints from downstream consumption. Crop sprayer width constrained not by the sprayer itself but by column separation at the manufacturing site — obvious to people working there daily, invisible to a top-down productivity analysis since it’s in no requirements document
  • The book broadens the consequences of early decisions: they can constrain the performance envelope, restrict manufacturing sites, and determine whether suppliers can capture aftermarket revenue.
  • Discussion: Draw the causal chain from engine orientation to one stakeholder outcome such as cabin volume, serviceability, assembly sequence, or supplier responsibility. Where does a technical choice become a value choice?
  • Discussion: Who knows the “column spacing” facts in your own projects, and how does that knowledge reach the architect early enough?
  • → Doing architecture well means finding these obvious-to-someone, invisible-to-you constraints before they force late, expensive redesign.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, p. 18.

Structured Creativity

“Structured creativity is better than unstructured creativity.”

Architecting a system is a soft process — part science, part art. No formula guarantees an optimal solution.

But focusing on decisions (not the underlying designs) lets architects trade off options and order decisions by their leverage on system performance.

  • State the book’s central methodological assertion plainly — explains the whole course’s organization
  • Architecting = a “soft” process, part science part art — no formula/algorithm guarantees an optimal architecture. Reset any plug-and-chug expectation now
  • Instead: a discipline centered on decisions, not the underlying designs
  • Why it matters: “lunar-orbit rendezvous or direct ascent” (a decision) vs. “here’s a detailed CAD model” (a design) — decisions let architects trade off broad options and order them by leverage on system performance, before committing to detail
  • Architectures are rarely selected in one stroke; the authors describe them as iteratively defined by a series of choices. Ordering decisions by leverage helps determine what must be addressed first and what can remain open.
  • Discussion: What structure helps creativity without prematurely closing the design space? Examples include explicit decisions, alternative concepts, evaluation criteria, assumptions, and staged commitments.
  • Discussion: When does structure become a cage? Watch for methods that silently exclude unconventional concepts or treat uncertain criteria as fixed facts.
  • → “Structured creativity”: not creativity forced into a formula, but creativity organized around explicit, comparable choices instead of scattered, premature detail.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, p. 18.

Case Study: NPOESS

  • 1994: merges two operational weather-satellite programs (civilian + military) — a $1.3B cost-consolidation opportunity
  • The VIIRS instrument: expected to combine three historical instruments’ capability, with less mass and volume than one — assuming complexity would scale linearly. It didn’t.
  • No system architect was appointed to manage the trade

VIIRS “Blue Marble,” Suomi NPP. Credit: NASA/NOAA/GSFC/Norman Kuring (public domain).

Canceled in 2010 — $8.5B over the original $6.5B estimate.

  • The chapter’s cautionary case study — walk through in detail, shows what happens when previous slides’ ideas are ignored
  • NPOESS (National Polar-Orbiting Environmental Satellite System), 1994: merges civilian (NOAA) + military (Air Force) weather-satellite programs — projected $1.3B consolidation savings, genuinely related data
  • Decision: include a superset of instrument capability from both heritage programs. VIIRS instrument = combine three historical instruments’ capability with less mass/volume than one — assumed complexity scales linearly with combined heritage complexity. It didn’t — combining functions creates new integration challenges, emergent difficulties (preview of “emergence,” central to Chapter 2)
  • Compounding decision: listed new functions independently of any system concept instead of deriving from heritage architecture — trapped performance in a corner of the design space unreachable with available mass/volume
  • Critically: no system architect appointed to manage the trade
  • The problem was not simply “too many requirements.” It was the coupled set of architectural decisions: merge programs, demand the union of capabilities, define functions apart from a feasible concept, compress three instruments into less mass and volume than one, and leave the trade without clear architectural ownership.
  • Discussion: At what point could the program still have changed course? Which decision would have been most valuable to reopen?
  • Source caution from the authors: government failures are overrepresented in public case studies because more information is available. The point is to learn from the failure, not to claim public programs are inherently worse than private ones.
  • → The program was canceled in 2010, reported by the text as $8.5B over the original $6.5B estimate. Keep the lesson precise: interacting early decisions and absent trade ownership can put detailed design on an infeasible path.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, pp. 18-19 and footnote 1. - Image: NASA/NOAA/GSFC/Norman Kuring, VIIRS “Blue Marble” from Suomi NPP (public domain).

Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you should be able to:

  • Use system thinking in a product and a system context
  • Analyze and critique existing architectures
  • Distinguish architectural from non-architectural decisions
  • Create the architecture of new or improved systems
  • Place architecture in the context of value and competitive advantage
  • Drive ambiguity out of the upstream process
  • Manage the evolution of system complexity
  • Critically evaluate current modes of architecting
  • Open with the authors’ framing: “This is a text on how to think, not what to think.” The goal is judgment, not memorization of a procedure.
  • The architect’s role is not confined to concept selection. The text places architecting across development, deployment, operation, and evolution.
  • Walk through as a semester roadmap, not a skim slide
  • “Use system thinking” + “analyze and critique existing architectures” → Parts 1-2 (Ch. 1-8), the analytical half
  • “Distinguish architectural from non-architectural decisions” → a constant-use skill: not every design choice is architectural, part of the discipline is recognizing which few decisions deserve this scrutiny
  • “Create the architecture of new/improved systems” + “manage evolution of complexity” → Part 3 (Ch. 9-13), the synthesis half — analyzing to designing
  • “Place architecture in context of value/competitive advantage” → ties back to Boeing/BlackBerry
  • “Drive ambiguity out of the upstream process” → previews Ch. 9 onward: needs/goals are inherently ambiguous early on, architect’s job is systematically reducing that
  • Additional objectives in the prose include creating system concepts, locating architecting within a firm’s development process, and developing guiding principles for successful architecting.
  • Discussion: Which objective is hardest to assess with an exam? What evidence would show that someone can actually architect rather than merely describe architecture?
  • → “Critically evaluate current modes of architecting”: invitation not to take even this textbook as gospel — the course goal is developing your own principles with experience.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, pp. 19-20.

Principles, Methods, Tools

  • Principles: underlying, long-enduring fundamentals — always (or nearly always) valid
  • Methods: ways of organizing approaches and tasks — solidly grounded on principles, usually or often applicable
  • Tools: contemporary ways to facilitate the process — applicable sometimes

“Principles are general rules and guidelines, intended to be enduring and seldom amended, that inform and support the way in which an organization sets about fulfilling its mission.”

— U.S. Air Force, Principles for Information Management, 1998

  • Vocabulary recurs throughout the textbook — pin it down precisely now
  • Principles: underlying, long-enduring fundamentals — always/nearly always true, regardless of domain or era
  • Methods: ways of organizing approaches/tasks — grounded on principles, usually/often applicable, adapted to context
  • Tools: most contingent — contemporary techniques, applicable only sometimes, most likely to become obsolete
  • USAF quote: why principles specifically are called special — enduring, seldom amended
  • Pair with Cooper (1838), The American Democrat: “Principles become modified in practice by facts” — useful tension: enduring, yet refined by real experience
  • Discussion: Take one familiar practice and classify it. Is “modularity” a principle, a method, or a tool? The answer depends on how it is stated; force the class to distinguish the enduring rationale from a particular implementation.
  • Discussion: What goes wrong when a tool is mistaken for a principle? Teams may preserve a notation, template, or optimization technique after its assumptions no longer fit the system.
  • → Course goal: develop your own principles of system architecture with experience — the frameworks here are a scaffold, not a cage.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 1, p. 20. - Quotation: U.S. Air Force, Principles for Information Management (1998), as reproduced in Crawley et al.

Next: System Thinking

Chapter 2 introduces four tasks for thinking about any system:

  1. Identify the system, its form, and its function
  2. Identify the entities of the system, their form and function
  3. Identify the relationships among the entities
  4. Identify the emergent properties of the system

Chapter source

Crawley, E., Cameron, B., & Selva, D. (2016). System Architecture: Strategy and Product Development for Complex Systems. Pearson. Chapter 1.

  • Preview exactly where we’re headed next lecture
  • Chapter 2 formalizes “system thinking” as four sequential tasks, applied to simple systems first:
    1. Identify the system, its form, and function
    2. Identify its entities, each with their own form and function
    3. Identify relationships (formal and functional) among entities and across the boundary
    4. Identify emergent properties resulting from all of that
  • Important qualification: the tasks are explained sequentially, but the book says real reasoning is rarely sequential and is more often iterative. New relationships or emergent behavior may force us to redraw the boundary or reinterpret function.
  • Discussion: Apply the four tasks quickly to the Blue Marlin image. Notice how the chosen boundary changes the entities, relationships, and emergent properties we care about.
  • → The same running examples (amplifier circuit, design team, circulatory system, solar system) make each task concrete before scaling to genuinely complex systems in Chapter 3.

[Sources] - Crawley, Cameron & Selva, System Architecture (2016), Ch. 2, pp. 22-23.
