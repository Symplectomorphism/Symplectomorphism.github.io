Systems Architecture · Chapter 5

From Form to Function

  • Chapter 4 analyzed form — what a system is
  • This chapter analyzes function — what a system does
  • Function is the more abstract, dynamic attribute — harder to pin down than form, but it’s the reason systems exist at all

Tip

Once we can analyze both form and function, Chapter 6 shows how they combine into system architecture.

Open by explicitly naming this chapter as the complement of Chapter 4, and reassure students that the topical ordering (form first, then function) doesn’t imply any strict sequence in real analysis — recall zigzagging from Chapter 3: an architect works in one domain for a while, then switches to the other. We simply have to present the material in some order, and form happened to come first.

Emphasize why function is harder to pin down than form: function is about activity — operations, transformations, actions — whereas form is about existence, something static you can point to. You can photograph form; you cannot photograph function in the same simple way (this becomes very concrete in a couple of slides with Figure 5.1). Function is also, in a real sense, the entire reason systems exist — nobody builds a pump because they want an assemblage of metal parts; they build it because they want the pressurizing that emerges from it. All of the magic of systems, and nearly all of the difficulty in designing them, is found in the functional domain, not the formal one.

The closing fragment previews exactly where this is headed: once we have rigorous ways to analyze form (Chapter 4, already done) and function (this chapter), Chapter 6 shows that architecture itself can be defined precisely as the mapping between the two — which instrument objects of form enable which internal processes of function. Everything in this chapter is building the vocabulary that mapping will need.

Box 5.1–5.3 · Definitions

Function is the action for which a system exists.

An operand is an object that need not exist prior to the execution of function, and is in some way acted upon by the function.

A process is the application of an instrument of form on an operand, changing it in some way — creating, destroying, or affecting it.

Function = process + operand. A process needs an instrument (form) to execute it.

Walk through all three definitions carefully, since this vocabulary is used precisely for the rest of the chapter — and the whole course. Box 5.1 defines function formally: the activity, operation, or transformation that causes or contributes to performance. In designed systems, function is the actions for which a system exists, ultimately leading to the delivery of value (a term we’ll define properly in a few slides). Note explicitly, since it’s easy to state loosely otherwise: function is executed by form, which is instrumental in function — form enables function, but is not itself function.

Box 5.2 defines operand precisely: an operand is an object, and therefore — like all objects — has the potential for stable, unconditional existence for some period of time. But operands specifically are objects that need not exist prior to the execution of the function; they may be created, modified, or consumed by the process. Point out something the book stresses and that surprises many students: operands are usually not supplied by the architect or builder of the system. The house’s architect doesn’t supply the resident; the instruction-card designer doesn’t supply the passenger. Operands typically appear at the time of operation, from other sources entirely, and are almost never shown on a conventional representation of a system — they’re easy to overlook precisely because nobody “builds” them.

Box 5.3 defines process: a pattern of transformation undergone by an object, generally involving the creation, destruction, or change of an operand. Contrast process sharply with objects (both operands and form): process is transient and dynamic, unfolding along a timeline, whereas objects have stable existence at a point in time. This distinction — and why it makes process so hard to visually represent — is exactly what the next slide illustrates.

The closing fragment restates the compact formula introduced back in Chapter 2 (function = process + operand) and adds the piece that completes the picture: a process needs an instrument — some element of form — in order to actually execute. This sets up the full “canonical model” of a system a few slides from now: operand, process, and instrument, all three, working together.

Figure 5.1 · Function Is Hard to Draw

Process is inherently dynamic — a static image always struggles to capture it. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.1. Photo (b) Miguel Angel Salinas Salinas/Shutterstock, (c) Screwy/Shutterstock.

Use this figure to make the “process is transient and dynamic” point from the previous slide viscerally concrete, not just abstract. You cannot take a photograph of process — you need a video; no single frame reveals the process, only the entire sequence of frames viewed together. The book’s own illustration: imagine a video of a sprinter crossing a finish line. If we look at a single isolated frame — or even at the international pictogram symbol for “running,” shown in the middle of this figure — we can only infer dynamics from a static shape; we can’t directly observe motion. Point out the cartoon on the right too: cartoonists have developed visual tricks (motion lines, exaggerated poses) specifically to hint at motion within a single static frame, and even so it’s ambiguous — is the character on the left of panel (a) taking the wheel cover off, or putting it on? A single static image is fundamentally ambiguous about the direction of a process. This is exactly the representational problem the rest of the chapter solves by introducing an explicit, unambiguous notation (OPM) for process — starting on the very next slide.

OPM Notation for Process and Operand

Three ways a process and operand can relate in OPM:

Affect
(double arrow)
The process changes an attribute of the operand, but doesn’t create or destroy it.

Consume
(arrow into process)
The operand no longer exists in its original form after the process.

Produce
(arrow out of process)
The operand didn’t exist before the process, and does after.

Introduce OPM’s precise, unambiguous notation for process — the fix to the ambiguity problem the previous slide just raised. In OPM, the process is drawn as an oval labeled with the process’s name; the operand is drawn as a rectangle (because it’s an object) labeled with the operand’s name. Function itself, notice, is made up of these two OPM elements together but has no separate symbol of its own — it’s the combination, not a third shape.

Walk through the three relationship types shown in the figure, since precisely which arrow you draw changes the meaning substantially. A single-headed arrow running from the process to the operand means the process produces (creates) the operand — the book’s examples: a factory creates a car, or a team develops a design; the object simply didn’t exist before the process and does exist after. A single-headed arrow running from the operand to the process means the process consumes the operand — a factory consuming parts to build a car, or lungs consuming oxygen from inhaled air. Consumption implies the operand’s original abstraction no longer exists in its original form or place after the process executes — though note carefully, as the book does, that in the car-factory example the parts still physically exist afterward, they’ve simply become part of a new abstraction called “the car.” A double-headed arrow means the process affects the operand without consuming or producing it — residents were people both before and after moving into a house; only their attribute of “being housed” changed, not their existence.

Point out the examples on the right side of the figure, since they show the same three relationship types applied to genuinely different systems: a passenger being informed (affected — their knowledge state changes, but the passenger still exists as a passenger); a decision being made (produced — the decision didn’t exist before); oxygen in the air being absorbed (consumed — from the perspective of the air, though again, from the tissue’s perspective it’s produced). This three-way distinction — affect, consume, produce — is the fundamental notational grammar for representing function precisely, and it recurs on every remaining diagram in this chapter.

Figure 5.3 · The Canonical Model

Function is a process affecting an operand; form is the instrument object enabling the process. This single diagram is the seed of every architecture model in this course. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.3.

This is arguably the single most important diagram in the entire course, worth telling students explicitly to remember it — the book calls it “the canonical model of a system.” It brings together everything covered so far in this chapter: OPM reserves a distinct notation — a round-headed arrow — specifically to link the instrument object (an element of form) to the process it enables. The process then connects, via the appropriate pointed arrow (create, destroy, or affect, from the previous slide), to the operand it acts on.

Walk through the three examples shown: an instrument object enables a process called “Processing,” which acts on an “Operand.” Team X (the instrument, i.e., the form) enables the process “Making,” which acts on the operand “Decision.” An “Instruction card” (instrument/form) enables the process “Informing,” which acts on the operand “Passenger.” In every case, the diagram has exactly the same three-part shape: form on the right, enabling function (process + operand) on the left — and this three-part shape is genuinely the seed from which every more elaborate architecture diagram in this course grows. Everything that follows — internal functions, functional architecture, the full architecture of Chapter 6 — is this same basic pattern, repeated and elaborated at greater scale and detail.

Making States Explicit

  • A more detailed OPM view shows the operand’s states directly, and which state the process moves it from and to
  • This is often the most useful representation: it shows exactly what “changes” means for a given operand

Extend the canonical model one level further by making the operand’s states explicit — this refinement turns out to be one of the most practically useful moves in the whole chapter, so spend real time on it. Rather than just drawing a box for “Operand,” draw the box for “Operand” containing two (or more) named states — State 1 and State 2 — with an arrow showing which state the process moves the operand from and to. In the passenger example: the operand “Passenger” has states “Not know” and “Know,” and the process “Informing” moves the passenger from “Not know” to “Know.”

Point out why this view earns its keep: previously, “affecting the operand” was a fairly abstract idea — you knew something changed, but not precisely what. Showing states explicitly answers exactly that question: it shows precisely what “change” means for this particular operand, in terms a stakeholder could verify (did the passenger actually come to know the safety instructions, yes or no?). This state-based view is also, worth flagging ahead of the next slide, structurally very close to how SysML represents function — via its state machine diagrams — even though OPM and SysML otherwise organize information quite differently.

5.3 External Function and Value

Table 5.1 · Questions for Defining Function

Question Produces
5a. What is the primary externally delivered function? What is value? The value-related operand, its state, and the process that changes it
5b. What are the internal functions? The internal processes and operands
5c. What is the functional architecture? The functional interactions among internal processes

This table is the chapter’s roadmap, exactly parallel to Table 4.1’s role in Chapter 4 — four questions, each answered by its own section, each producing a specific analytical output. Question 5a — what is the primary externally delivered function, and what is value — is answered in the rest of this section (5.3), and produces the value-related operand together with its state and the process that changes it. Question 5b — what are the principal internal functions — is Section 5.4’s job, producing the internal processes and operands. Question 5c — what is the functional architecture, how do internal functions connect into a value pathway — is Section 5.5’s job, producing the functional interactions among processes. And Question 5d — what other secondary value-related functions exist — is Section 5.6’s job.

It’s worth explicitly pointing out the structural echo with Chapter 4’s four questions about form, since students should recognize the pattern by now: first identify the primary/externally-visible thing, then decompose internally, then understand how the internal pieces interact, then look for secondary/additional considerations. This four-step pattern — primary, internal, interactions, secondary — recurs constantly through the rest of Part 2 of the course.

The Primary Externally Delivered Function

  • The primary externally delivered function emerges when a process acts across the system boundary, at an interface
  • The value-related operand is the one whose change in state is the reason the system exists
  • To find it, ask: What is the most specific operand the system acts on to deliver value? What attribute and state change is associated with that value? What process changes it?

Tip

Pump: operand is water, attribute is pressure, value-state is high, process is pressurizing.

Introduce the crucial “primary externally delivered function,” which is the concept anchoring the rest of Section 5.3. Two conditions both have to hold, and it’s worth unpacking the word “externally delivered” carefully, since it’s doing real work: first, the function must genuinely cross the boundary of the system and influence something out in the context — a function that stays entirely internal doesn’t count. Second, among possibly many externally delivered functions, there’s a primary one — the function for which the system was originally built.

It’s worth noting a subtlety from the book here, since a sharp student might object: aren’t there systems built “only for their form” — art, trophies, coins, collectibles? The book’s answer: even these specialized forms have functions, they’re just tied to human reaction rather than physical transformation — pleasing (+ the viewer), impressing (+ others), satisfying (+ a need to collect). The form still delivers a function; it’s just less obviously mechanical.

The value-related operand is the specific operand whose change in state is the reason the system exists at all — not just any operand the system happens to touch. The book gives a precise three-part diagnostic question, worth writing on the board verbatim: what is the most specific operand the system acts on to deliver value; what attribute of that operand changes, and to what state; and what specific process performs that change? Ground this immediately with the pump example the fragment reveals: the operand is water, the value-related attribute is pressure, the value-state is “high” (not “low”), and the process is pressurizing. It’s genuinely useful to ask students why the pump isn’t better described as “consuming low-pressure water” — the answer, covered in more depth on Figure 5.9 shortly, is that we build pumps to produce high-pressure water, not to consume the low-pressure kind; framing determines which operand counts as “value-related.”

Figure 5.7 · Increasing Levels of Detail

Three equally valid OPM diagrams of the same external function — increasingly explicit about the operand’s states. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.7.

Use this figure to make an important methodological point: there is no single “correct” level of detail for representing an external function — all three diagrams shown here are simultaneously valid, they just make different amounts of information explicit. The book’s own worked example is the operational amplifier: the leftmost, simplest diagram just shows “Amplifying” acting on “Output signal,” full stop. The middle diagram adds explicit states to the output signal — “Lo” and “Hi” — showing amplifying as the process that moves the signal from Lo to Hi. The rightmost, most detailed diagram goes one level further still, explicitly decomposing the operand “Output signal” into its attribute “Amplitude,” itself shown with its own Lo/Hi states, and shows the amplifying process acting specifically on that attribute.

The practical lesson: which of these three levels of detail you should actually draw depends entirely on your audience and purpose — a quick sketch for a stakeholder conversation might use the simplest version; a rigorous internal architecture document might need the most explicit one. None is more “true” than the others; they’re progressively more detailed projections of the exact same underlying function.

Figure 5.9 · The Pump’s Value-Related Operand

Water is the operand; pressure is the value-related attribute; pressurizing is the process that moves it from Lo to Hi. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.9.

This figure resolves the question left open a couple of slides ago: how do we know the high-pressure water, not the low-pressure water, is the value-related operand? The diagram shows the pump consuming low-pressure water and discharging (producing) high-pressure water. The book’s reasoning, worth stating explicitly since it generalizes well beyond pumps: we don’t build pumps to consume low-pressure water — we build them specifically to produce high-pressure water. The direction of “why does this system exist” tells you which side of the transformation is the value-related operand. This is a good moment to have students apply the same diagnostic to a system of their choosing — ask “why does this system exist,” and check that the answer names the operand’s produced or desired state, not merely any state it happens to pass through.

Box 5.4–5.5 · Value and Benefit

Value is the benefit received relative to the cost incurred.

“Design is not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works.” — Steve Jobs

“Form and function should be one, joined in a spiritual union.” — Frank Lloyd Wright

Value = benefit at cost. Architecture is function enabled by form. Good architectures (desired function for minimal form) are nearly synonymous with the delivery of value.

Define value precisely, since this term gets used loosely in everyday business language and the book wants a specific, disciplined meaning: value is benefit at cost. Benefit is synonymous with the worth, importance, or utility created by a system, and — this is worth emphasizing — benefit is judged subjectively, by an observer (who is not the architect, but rather the customer, beneficiary, or user; we’ll return to this distinction properly in Chapter 11). Cost is a measure of the contribution that must be made in exchange for that benefit. This means good value can be created two different ways: by high benefit at modest cost, or by modest benefit at very low cost — value is always a ratio or relationship between the two, never benefit alone. Flag explicitly, since it’s a common point of confusion: some people use “value” as a simple synonym for “benefit” — this book deliberately does not; value always means benefit relative to cost.

The two quotes are worth contrasting for what each emphasizes: Steve Jobs is making almost exactly this chapter’s central point — design (and by extension, good architecture) isn’t primarily about how something looks (form, in isolation) but about how it works (function). Frank Lloyd Wright’s quote takes the complementary angle — form and function shouldn’t be treated as separable at all, but as a unified whole.

The closing fragment ties value directly back to architecture as this course defines it: architecture is function enabled by form. A genuinely good architecture, therefore, is one that delivers the desired function using the least (most efficient, most minimal) form necessary — and that combination, desired function for minimal form, is nearly synonymous with delivering good value. This is a nice bridge line to preview Chapter 6, where the formal mapping between form and function is the definition of architecture itself.

Section 5.2–5.3 Summary

  • Function is a process acting on an operand, enabled by an instrument of form
  • The primary externally delivered function emerges when a process acts on the value-related operand across the system boundary
  • Benefit materializes as a consequence of a process acting on the value-related operand — analyze the operand, its value-related attribute and state change, and the process

Use this as a checkpoint before moving from external function to internal function. By this point students should be comfortable with the full OPM notational grammar (objects as boxes, processes as ovals, the affect/consume/produce distinction, the round-headed instrument arrow), the canonical model of a system (instrument enabling process acting on operand), and the specific procedure for identifying a system’s primary externally delivered function: find the value-related operand, its value-related attribute, the state that attribute moves to, and the process responsible. Everything from here forward assumes fluency with this vocabulary, since Section 5.4 immediately turns to asking the same kinds of questions about what happens inside the system boundary, in order to explain how that external function actually comes about.

5.4 Internal Function

Identifying Internal Functions

  • Internal functions are the processes and operands inside the boundary that lead to the value-related external function
  • No shortcut exists — this requires domain knowledge: reverse-engineering the elements of form, applying standard blueprints for your field, or using metaphors
  • The three techniques from Chapter 2 for predicting emergence apply here too: precedent, experiments, and analysis

Tip

Pump: increments kinetic energy (accelerating), then trades it for pressure (diffusing) — in addition to inflowing and outflowing.

Move from the external, boundary-crossing function to what’s actually happening inside the system to produce it — Question 5b from Table 5.1. Be honest with students, echoing the book’s own honesty here: there’s no mechanical shortcut for identifying internal functions. It genuinely requires domain knowledge, and the book gives three concrete techniques for acquiring or applying that knowledge. Reverse-engineering the elements of form: look at the physical (or informational) pieces already present, and reason about what they must be doing — for the operational amplifier’s three components, a modest understanding of active analog circuits reveals that the feedback resistors set the gain and the op-amp itself does the amplifying. Standard blueprints: sometimes functions naturally unfold into a stable, well-known sequence of internal steps that changes remarkably little over long periods of time — the book’s own vivid example compares riding a horse from house to town 150 years ago (get the horse out of the barn, mount, ride while navigating, arrive, stable the horse) against driving a car to the office today (get the car out of the garage, drive while navigating, arrive, park) — the underlying blueprint of “prepare vehicle, navigate while traveling, arrive, secure vehicle” is remarkably stable across a century and a completely different technology. Metaphors: comparing an unfamiliar system to a familiar one with analogous internal function — the book compares the circulatory system to a home heating system, since both have a working fluid (blood/water) that absorbs something (oxygen/heat) and gets pumped to a delivery point.

Also point out that the three techniques from Chapter 2 for predicting emergence — precedent, experiment, and modeling/analysis — apply directly here too, since identifying internal functions is really a special case of that same general problem. And don’t forget the humbler technique: sometimes just directly observing operations in progress is genuinely valuable and shouldn’t be skipped in favor of more elaborate methods.

Ground all this with the pump example, continuing the running case: applying domain knowledge (this does require some fluid-mechanics background) reveals that the pump doesn’t just “pass water through” — it first increments the water’s kinetic energy (the internal process of accelerating), then trades that kinetic energy for pressure (the internal process of diffusing), in addition to the more obvious inflowing and outflowing at its two ports. Four internal functions in total for what looked, externally, like one simple pressurizing action.

Figure 5.10 · Internal Functions of Bread Slicing

Mixing produces dough, baking produces bread, cutting produces slices — inferred from a standard cooking blueprint. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.10.

Walk through the bread-slicing example as a clean illustration of the “standard blueprint” technique in action. The value-related external function was “making sliced bread”; applying a standard cooking blueprint (mix ingredients, apply heat, then portion the result) — nearly the same blueprint you’d use to infer the steps of almost any baked good — gives us three internal processes: mixing (which produces dough from raw ingredients, not shown yet on this simpler diagram), baking (which produces bread from dough), and cutting (which produces slices from bread). Point out this diagram is deliberately still incomplete: it shows outputs cascading forward (dough to bread to slices) but doesn’t yet show the raw ingredient inputs feeding into mixing — that gap is exactly what gets filled in a few slides from now, once we move from “internal function” to the fuller “functional architecture.”

Figure 5.11 · Internal Functions of Bubblesort

Looping affects the index (doesn’t create/destroy it); exchanging affects array entries; testing uses array elements as an instrument, without changing them. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.11.

Bring in the software example, continuing this course’s running case of the bubblesort algorithm (recall from Chapter 4 that the value-related external function is producing a sorted array from an unsorted one). Applying the “reverse-engineer the blueprint” technique to the pseudocode itself — treating the code as the blueprint to be read — reveals four internal functions: looping over the I index and looping over the J index (each of which affects its respective index variable, incrementing it, but doesn’t create or destroy it — the index object persists across the whole sort), testing whether a pair of array entries is out of order, and exchanging (conditionally swapping) two array entries when needed.

Point out something genuinely interesting and worth dwelling on: testing uses the array elements as an instrument, not as something it changes — testing reads their values to make a decision, but the values themselves are untouched by the testing process. This is a nice concrete illustration of an operand object being reused as an instrument of a different process than the one that created it: array elements are operands with respect to whatever process ultimately sets their values, but they’re instruments with respect to the testing process that merely reads them to make a comparison. Objects that are operands can, and often do, later serve as instruments of subsequent processes — a subtlety worth flagging since it slightly complicates the clean instrument/operand distinction from earlier in the chapter.

5.5 Functional Interactions

Functional Architecture

The exchanged or shared operands are the functional interactions. The functions plus the functional interactions are the functional architecture.

  • This is the key step in analyzing architecture: it’s through the interaction of internal functions that the externally delivered function emerges
  • Comparing the internal-function view to the functional-architecture view usually reveals additional input/output operands that were implicit before

Move to Question 5c from Table 5.1 — arguably the most important question in the whole chapter, worth telling students so directly. Having identified the individual internal functions (Section 5.4), we now need to understand how they connect. The definition on the slide is precise and worth repeating verbatim: the exchanged or shared operands are the functional interactions between processes — this echoes Chapter 2’s relationship vocabulary directly (recall functional relationships are also called interactions). The full set of internal functions plus their functional interactions together constitute the functional architecture of the system.

Emphasize why this step matters more than any other single step in the chapter: it is specifically through the interaction of internal functions — not the functions in isolation — that the externally delivered function emerges. This is the direct payoff of Chapter 2’s emergence discussion, now applied concretely to function analysis: knowing the individual internal processes (mixing, baking, cutting) doesn’t yet tell you how “making sliced bread” as a whole emerges; you need to see how those processes’ operands connect and flow into one another.

The second bullet flags something students should watch for as a genuine diagnostic technique, not just an incidental observation: when you go from an internal-function diagram (like Figure 5.10 or 5.11) to a full functional-architecture diagram, you almost always discover additional input or output operands that were only implicit before — because drawing the connections between processes forces you to notice what’s missing at the edges. The very next slide, on bread slicing, is exactly this discovery in action.

Figure 5.12 · Functional Architecture of Bread Slicing

Adding the raw-ingredient input reveals the full functional architecture — compare to Figure 5.10. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.12.

Have students explicitly compare this diagram to Figure 5.10 from two slides ago, since the difference is the whole point. Figure 5.10 showed only mixing, baking, and cutting cascading forward. This figure adds the raw-ingredient inputs — flour, water, salt, and yeast — flowing into mixing at the very top, exactly the “additional input operand” the previous slide predicted would surface. Trace the full chain out loud: flour, water, salt, and yeast are consumed by mixing, which produces dough; dough is consumed by baking, which produces bread; bread is consumed by cutting, which produces slices — the external value-related operand, crossing the system boundary at the bottom.

Point out the specific language the book uses to describe this pattern: the operands here (dough, bread) are simultaneously being created by one process and consumed by the very next one — this constitutes the interaction between those processes. And note the general shape of this particular example: it’s what the book calls a simple flow-through system — a single chain, each operand created upstream and consumed immediately downstream — a very common and pleasingly simple pattern that shows up constantly in engineering (piping systems, control-system block diagrams, SysML activity diagrams). It’s worth flagging for students, since the pump example just ahead complicates this simple picture: not every system is this cleanly flow-through, and treating one that isn’t as if it were can actually obscure what’s really happening.

Figure 5.14 · The Pump, Redrawn with States

  • Figure 5.13 (not shown) makes the pump look like a simple flow-through system — but water isn’t really destroyed and recreated at each stage
  • Figure 5.14 captures what’s really happening: the state of the water changes as it moves through the system

Tip

The interpretation of internal functions is never unique — the real test is whether it makes emergence easy to understand and predict.

This slide makes a genuinely subtle and important point, so slow down here. If you apply the simple flow-through pattern mechanically to the pump — following exactly the same procedure that worked cleanly for bread slicing — you get a diagram (the book’s Figure 5.13, referenced but not separately shown in our deck) where inflowing “creates” an internal low-pressure-flow operand, accelerating “destroys” that and “creates” a high-velocity-flow operand, diffusing “destroys” that and “creates” a high-pressure-flow operand, and outflowing “destroys” that to produce the external output. It’s a valid diagram, and it’s not wrong, exactly — but it’s misleading, because water is not actually being destroyed and recreated as brand-new water at every single stage; that’s an artifact of forcing a flow-through create/destroy pattern onto a physical process that’s really continuous.

Figure 5.14 fixes this by representing what’s physically really happening: there’s a single, continuous internal-flow operand whose state changes as it moves through the pump — its velocity changes (raised by accelerating, then lowered by diffusing) and its pressure changes (raised by diffusing, and it’s this final high-pressure state that gets discharged by outflowing). This is the states-explicit style of representation introduced earlier in the chapter (recall “Making States Explicit”), now applied at the internal-function level rather than just to the single external operand.

The fragment states the chapter’s most important methodological caveat plainly, and it’s worth having students internalize this as a general rule for the rest of the course: the interpretation of internal functions and processes is genuinely not unique — unlike decomposition of form, which tends to be relatively tangible and definite, there are often several equally defensible ways to define a system’s internal processes and operands. The real test for choosing among them isn’t “which one is objectively correct” but “which one makes the system’s emergence easiest to understand and predict.” The flow-through version of the pump (destroy/create at each stage) and the states-explicit version (one continuous operand changing state) are both valid representations of the identical physical pump — but the states-explicit version more accurately captures the underlying physics and is arguably the more useful one for real engineering reasoning.

The Value Pathway

  • The value pathway is the chain of internal processes and operands that leads to the primary externally delivered function
  • Not every operand and process is on it — some are:
    • Not contributing to any desired function (unwanted side-effects, poor design, legacy — a source of gratuitous complexity, Ch. 13)
    • Supporting processes and form (even further from the value pathway — Ch. 6)

Introduce a term that recurs constantly for the rest of the course, so make sure it’s precisely understood: the value pathway is the specific chain of internal operands and processes that runs from some initial input, progressively through internal transformations, until it reaches the value-related external output. In flow-through architectures (bread slicing, the pump), the value pathway is usually readily evident just by tracing the diagram’s main chain — but the book cautions that in other, less linear types of architecture, more careful reasoning may be necessary to spot it.

The second half of this slide is worth real emphasis, since it corrects a natural assumption that everything inside a system must be on the value pathway — it isn’t. Two distinct categories of things sit off the pathway. First, operands and processes that don’t contribute to the emergence of any desired externally delivered function at all — these are sometimes unwanted side-effects, sometimes the result of poor design choices, sometimes just legacy cruft carried forward from an earlier design — and the book flags these explicitly as a source of gratuitous complexity, a concept that gets its own full treatment in Chapter 13. Second, supporting processes and form — things that are genuinely needed for the system to function, but are one step further removed from the value pathway itself (the book’s example, elaborated on the next slide: the powering process that the kitchen provides isn’t part of “making sliced bread” per se, but the whole operation couldn’t happen without it) — this second category is picked up again in Chapter 6’s discussion of architecture more fully.

Figure 5.15 · Entities Not on the Value Pathway

Frying and growing mold aren’t part of making sliced bread — unwanted or irrelevant. Powering (the kitchen) is a supporting function, one step further removed. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.15.

Walk through this figure as the concrete payoff of the previous slide’s two-category distinction, using the bread-slicing example one more time, now elaborated with extra entities specifically to illustrate what’s excluded from the value pathway. The value pathway itself (dough → baking → bread → slicing → slices) is the same chain from Figure 5.12. But the elaborated diagram also shows frying (producing fried dough) and growing (producing mold) — both connected to entities in this system, but neither contributing to the goal of making sliced bread at all. Frying might represent an entirely different, unrelated recipe someone could make with the same dough; growing mold is a clearly unwanted side effect of letting bread sit too long — exactly the “unwanted side-effects” and “poor design/legacy” categories from the previous slide, sources of gratuitous complexity that a careful architect should recognize and, where possible, design away.

Separately, the diagram shows an oven and a slicer (elements of form) enabling baking and slicing respectively, and a kitchen (form) enabling a powering process that both the oven and slicer ultimately depend on. Point out explicitly why powering sits even further from the value pathway than frying or mold-growing: powering is not unwanted or a design flaw — it’s a genuinely necessary supporting function — but it’s still one layer removed from the actual value-delivering chain of mixing-baking-cutting. This distinction between “on the value pathway,” “off the pathway and unwanted (gratuitous),” and “off the pathway but genuinely supporting” is worth having students practice classifying on a system of their own choosing.

Emergence and Zooming

  • Emergence: the “smaller to larger” whole-part relationship for process — internal processes combine into the emergent external function
  • Zooming: the reverse, “larger to larger” — reasoning from the emergent function back down into the internal processes that produce it

Reconnect two terms from Chapter 2 — emergence and zooming — now applied specifically to process, alongside a reminder that process, exactly like entities of form, also has a whole-part relationship. Correct a likely typo risk in your own delivery here: zooming is the “larger to smaller” direction (not “larger to larger”) — reasoning from the emergent, whole-system function down into the internal processes that produce it; emergence is the reverse, “smaller to larger” direction — internal processes combining upward into the emergent external function. Keep the directionality crisp for students, since this exact pair of terms (emergence going up, zooming going down) was first introduced with entities of form back in Chapter 2’s Table 2.3, and this slide is simply confirming the identical relationship holds for process too.

Walk through the figure concretely with the pump example: the left side shows all the internal processes (inflowing, accelerating, diffusing, outflowing) individually, each connected to its associated internal operands — this is the “zoomed in” view. The right side shows the same system with internal processes suppressed, collapsed into a single emergent process — “Pressurizing” — with only the internal and external operands still attached to that single emergent process. Reading the figure from left to right (suppressing detail, combining upward) is emergence; reading it from right to left (expanding the single emergent process back into its constituent internal processes) is zooming. This bidirectional relationship — being able to move fluently in both directions — is exactly the skill Section 5.4 and 5.5 have been building toward.

Functional Architecture in Software

  • Software functions are of two types: computational statements (A = B + C) and functions that dynamically allocate control (if... then...)
  • Control functions need operands too — control tokens, more implicit than explicit data variables
  • This produces two parallel kinds of functional interaction: data interactions and command (control) interactions

Extend the general framework specifically to software, since software systems have a genuine wrinkle worth calling out on its own. In principle, software’s functional architecture can be represented exactly the same way as any general system’s — but in practice, software engineers, while entirely comfortable with the functional domain conceptually (they implement code, i.e., form, purely because it satisfies functional demands), find information systems genuinely challenging to conceive as static architectures, precisely because they’re so dynamic in structure and behavior.

The book identifies two distinct types of function that appear in software. Computational statements are the more obvious kind — something like A = B + C, a straightforward transformation of data operands. But there are also functions that dynamically allocate control — conditional logic like if... then... constructs, which don’t transform data directly but instead determine which subsequent process actually executes.

If control functions are genuine functions (and they are), then per our function = process + operand rule, they must have operands too — and the book introduces the idea of control tokens to fill this role: more implicit, less directly visible than the explicit data variables used in ordinary computation, but operands nonetheless. This gives rise to two genuinely parallel kinds of functional interaction running simultaneously through any piece of software: ordinary data interactions (explicit variables passed between computational processes) and command or control interactions (control tokens passed between conditional/control processes) — worth telling students this parallelism between control and data interaction is a completely general feature of information systems of all kinds, not a bubblesort-specific quirk.

Figure 5.19 · Bubblesort with Command Operands

Control tokens pass between Looping, Testing, and Exporting — the command interactions running in parallel with the data interactions. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.19.

Walk through this as the fully reverse-engineered functional architecture of bubblesort, building on Figure 5.11’s internal-function identification from earlier in the chapter. Applying the systematic procedure (start with internal processes and operands already identified, then look for missing processes like importing/exporting, and missing operands like the array length variable and the external unsorted/sorted arrays) reveals the complete picture: importing brings in the external array and array length; looping over I and looping over J both increment their respective indices and — this is the new piece this slide adds — each creates a control token that governs what happens next; testing compares array entries (using them as an instrument, per Figure 5.11) and, based on that comparison, produces its own control token; exchanging conditionally swaps two array entries, guided by testing’s control token; and exporting finally delivers the sorted array across the system boundary.

Point out the specific control-token pathway the book traces explicitly, since it’s a nice concrete illustration of the “command interactions running in parallel with data interactions” idea from the previous slide: J looping creates a token that passes control either to testing or back to I looping; I looping, in turn, passes control to either J looping or to exporting. This is genuinely the same logical structure as the nested-loop control flow any student who’s written bubblesort in code would recognize — but now made fully explicit as a functional-architecture diagram, with control tokens drawn as dashed-line operands running in parallel with the solid-line data operands (the array elements, the indices). This is a good moment to ask students to compare this diagram to whatever nested-loop bubblesort pseudocode or code they may have written themselves, and identify which lines correspond to which OPM elements.

Section 5.5 Summary

  • The entities of internal function, the internal processes, and the interactions of operands among processes establish the functional architecture
  • Representation of internal function is not unique — it depends on interpretation and style
  • The functional architecture generally contains a value pathway; we zoom the external function to identify it
  • Diagrams can be simplified using projections, where interactions between processes are indicated by shared operands alone

Use this as the checkpoint for Section 5.5 before moving to secondary functions. Reinforce the core definition one more time: functional architecture is internal processes plus their interactions (shared/exchanged operands). Repeat the non-uniqueness caveat explicitly, since it was one of the chapter’s subtler and more important points (illustrated by the pump’s flow-through vs. states-explicit representations) — there’s no single correct way to represent internal function, only more or less useful ones, judged by whether they make emergence easy to understand and predict.

The value-pathway bullet ties back to the “Emergence and Zooming” and “Value Pathway” slides — the functional architecture typically contains this identifiable chain running from input to value-related output, and we find it by zooming the external function down into its constituent internal operands and processes. The last bullet references a technique the book covers in more analytical depth (via Box 5.8’s projection methodology, not separately included in this deck, but worth mentioning it exists): diagrams and PO — process-operand — arrays showing full functional interaction detail can be simplified by projecting the representation onto just the processes, with the shared operands connecting them left implicit rather than drawn out explicitly. This is analogous to the DSM-projection techniques used elsewhere in the book for managing large, detailed matrices — a preview that this kind of matrix-based analytical machinery reappears later in the course (particularly around Chapter 13’s discussion of decomposition).

5.6 Secondary Functions

Secondary Value-Related Functions

  • Once a system delivers its primary function, there’s no reason it shouldn’t deliver other value-related functions too
  • Team X could also produce reports; the circulatory system also returns CO₂; an op-amp could also filter noise
  • These matter: customers expect them, and they’re a source of competitive advantage
  • Procedure: repeat the same reasoning used for the primary value-related function

Turn to the chapter’s final question, 5d from Table 5.1: what other important secondary value-related external functions does the system deliver? The key insight to establish up front: once a system is already delivering its primary function, there’s no architectural reason it can’t also be designed to deliver other, secondary value-related functions at the same time. Give the three examples on the slide as a quick tour across the recurring examples: Team X’s primary function is developing a design, but the team could also secondarily produce reports documenting that decision, delivering separate value to the organization; the circulatory system’s primary function is supplying oxygen, but it also returns carbon dioxide to the lungs — a second, genuinely valuable function running in parallel; an operational amplifier’s primary function is amplifying, but it could also be designed to filter high-frequency noise as a bonus.

Stress why identifying secondary functions matters practically, not just as an intellectual completeness exercise: customers genuinely expect these secondary functions in modern products, and they’re frequently a real source of competitive advantage — a product that nails its primary function but ignores obvious secondary value that customers expect will lose out to a competitor who delivers both. (This is a nice callback to Chapter 1’s Apple iPhone case-in-passing, where a phone case that interfered with the radio antenna — a secondary, but expected, function — became a real embarrassment despite the phone nailing its primary function.)

The procedure for identifying secondary functions, worth stating plainly since it’s refreshingly simple: it’s just the same reasoning process used to identify the primary value-related function, applied repeatedly. Ask the same three-part diagnostic (what operand, what attribute/state, what process) for each additional value-related output the system might plausibly deliver.

Figure 5.20 · Secondary Functions of the Pump

A “slinger” deflects leaked water away from the motor; a sensor measures outlet pressure. Neither is on the primary value pathway, but both add real value. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 5.20.

Close out the secondary-functions discussion with the pump example one final time. Real centrifugal pumps very often include a “slinger” — a rotating component that flings water leaking through the rotating seal safely away from the electric motor, protecting it from water damage. This is a genuine secondary value-related function: the operand is the leaked water, the process is “deflecting,” and it delivers real value (protecting the motor) even though it’s completely separate from the primary pressurizing function. The pump might also include a pressure sensor that measures the outlet pressure — directly measuring the very property (pressure) associated with the delivery of the pump’s primary value, but doing so via its own distinct secondary function (“measuring”), producing a separate operand (a pressure-measurement signal) that has its own value to an operator monitoring the pump.

Point out, using the figure, that these two secondary functions relate to the value pathway differently: the measuring function taps directly into the primary pathway (it reads the pressure state that’s already part of the primary chain) — while the deflecting function runs on an entirely separate pathway of its own (dealing with leaked water, which was never part of pressurizing at all). This is worth using as a final example for students to practice classifying: not every secondary function relates to the primary pathway in the same way, and recognizing that difference is itself useful architectural insight.

Chapter 5 Summary

  • Function is a process (enabled by an instrument of form) acting on an operand
  • The primary externally delivered function emerges where a process crosses the system boundary and changes the value-related operand
  • Internal functions require domain knowledge to identify; their interactions (shared/exchanged operands) form the functional architecture
  • The value pathway is what predicts emergence — everything else is either gratuitous or merely supporting
  • Systems can deliver secondary value-related functions too, beyond the primary one

Use this final summary slide to walk students back through the whole chapter’s arc in order, tying it to Table 5.1’s four questions one more time. We started by defining function precisely as process-plus-operand, with process requiring an instrument of form to execute — the canonical model of a system. We then asked what’s externally delivered (Question 5a): the primary externally delivered function emerges specifically where a process crosses the system boundary and changes the value-related operand, and value itself means benefit relative to cost. We then asked what’s happening internally (Question 5b): internal functions require real domain knowledge to identify — reverse-engineering form, applying standard blueprints, or using metaphors — there’s no shortcut. We then asked how those internal functions connect (Question 5c): their interactions, meaning shared or exchanged operands, together constitute the functional architecture, within which the value pathway is the specific chain that predicts how the primary emergent function comes about — with everything off that pathway being either gratuitous (unwanted, poor design, legacy) or merely supporting. And finally we asked what else the system might deliver (Question 5d): secondary value-related functions, identified by repeating the exact same reasoning applied to the primary one.

Point forward to the closing slide’s table (in the original chapter, Table 5.6, contrasting form and function feature by feature) as a good self-study tool, and make sure students see clearly why this chapter had to come before Chapter 6: we now have rigorous, precise ways to talk about both form (Chapter 4) and function (this chapter) — which is exactly the pair of ingredients Chapter 6 needs in order to define architecture itself as the mapping between them.
