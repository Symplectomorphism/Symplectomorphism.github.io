Solution-Neutral Function and Concepts

Systems Architecture · Chapter 7

Aykut C. Satici

From Reverse to Forward Engineering

  • Chapters 4–6 pursued reverse engineering: the system already exists, so we analyzed its form, function, and architecture
  • Using centrifugal pumps and bubblesort to teach architecture was “a sledge hammer to a thumb tack” — simple systems don’t need the full toolkit
  • This chapter shifts to forward engineering: two new ideas, solution-neutral function and concept, plus two higher-complexity systems — an air transportation service and a home data network

Tip

Table 7.1 amends the Chapter 5 questions and adds a new one (7a) — collating everything into a method for synthesis, not just analysis.

Open by naming the pivot this chapter makes, since it’s a genuine change in direction from everything so far. Chapters 4 through 6 were all reverse engineering: in every case (the pump, bubblesort, bread slicing) the system already existed, fully formed, and our job was purely analytical — take it apart and describe its form, function, and architecture. The book is candid, and it’s worth repeating the line verbatim because it’s memorable: using genuinely simple systems like a centrifugal pump or bubblesort to teach the full apparatus of system architecture was “like taking a sledge hammer to a thumb tack” — those systems don’t really need this much machinery, but using them let us learn the machinery without complexity getting in the way.

Now the chapter does two things at once: it shifts from reverse to forward engineering — starting with nothing built yet, and working forward toward a design — and it introduces two new intellectual tools needed for that shift: solution-neutral function and concept. It also introduces two new running examples explicitly chosen to be more realistic in complexity than the pump or bubblesort: an air transportation service (chosen to show how architecting applies to services, not just physical products) and a home data network (described as “a microcosm of global information networking”).

The fragment previews the bookkeeping device the chapter uses to organize all of this: Table 7.1, which takes the analytical questions from Chapter 5 and amends them with bracketed new material, plus adds an entirely new question (7a) specific to solution-neutral function — collating everything built so far into one coherent method that now supports synthesis, not just analysis. We’ll return to this table shortly.

Box 7.1 · Principle of Solution-Neutral Function

“We cannot solve our problems with the same thinking we used when we created them.” — Albert Einstein

  • Poor system specifications frequently contain clues about an intended solution, function, or form — and those clues narrow the architect’s options before exploration even begins
  • Use solution-neutral functions where possible, and use the hierarchy of solution-neutral statements to scope how broad an exploration should be

Introduce the chapter’s first named principle and connect the Einstein quote to something very concrete: the danger of specifications that smuggle in a solution disguised as a requirement. Point out to students that this happens constantly in real practice, often invisibly — a specification that says “add a corkscrew to the kitchen drawer inventory” has already silently decided the solution (a corkscrew, using pulling) before anyone asked what the actual need was (getting the cork out of the bottle, or even more broadly, accessing the wine). Once a specification contains such a clue, it narrows the architect’s exploration before it even starts, foreclosing entire classes of alternative concepts (like using gas pressure or suction, both explored on the next couple of slides) that might have been better.

The prescription is simple to state, and worth having students repeat back: state functions solution-neutrally wherever possible — describing what needs to happen, never how — and use the fact that solution-neutral statements form a hierarchy (more on this in a few slides) to deliberately scope how broad or narrow an exploration should be. This sets up the entire rest of the chapter, which is essentially a rigorous methodology for doing exactly this.

Figure 7.1 · Concepts for Removing a Wine Bottle Cork

  • A corkscrew opens a wine bottle. But can we name other concepts for the same job?
  • We describe concepts as an operand-process-instrument set: cork (operand) pushing (process) injected gas (instrument)
  • Pulling alone is insufficient to describe a concept — “cork pulling with a screw” is a corkscrew

Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.1.

Work through this figure as the chapter’s running worked example — it’s simple enough that everyone in the room has direct physical intuition for it, which is exactly why the book leads with it. Everyone defaults immediately to “a corkscrew” when asked how to open a wine bottle — but push the question: how would you describe a corkscrew in a general fashion? A corkscrew uses a screw to pull the cork. Is pulling the only way? If we get creative: we could glue a tab to the cork’s top and pull on the tab (no screw needed); we could avoid touching the cork entirely and create suction around it, using a lower-pressure environment to draw it out (this is a real household product, sold with a thin hollow needle and a hand air pump); or, going the opposite direction, we could pressurize the air inside the bottle instead, pushing the cork out with injected gas. A wine steward in a fine restaurant sometimes uses a completely different approach: a fork-like device that shears the sides of the cork off entirely.

The diagram organizes all of these using a structured operand-process-instrument description: the operand is always the cork, but the process varies (pushing, shearing, pulling) and each process has its own instrument options (injected gas or released gas for pushing; fork or tube for shearing; suction or screw for pulling). Emphasize the point made explicitly in the slide’s third bullet: “pulling” alone doesn’t fully specify a concept — you need the instrument too. “Cork pulling with a screw” is what makes it specifically a corkscrew, as opposed to pulling with suction, which is an entirely different concept that happens to share the same process.

Solution-Neutral Function in a Hierarchy

  • Drawing Figure 7.1 is itself a form of structured creativity (Ch. 12) — pulling led us to think about pushing, which we might otherwise have missed
  • Solution-neutral function exists in a hierarchy: translating the cork generalizes to removing the cork, which generalizes to opening the bottle, up to accessing the wine

Tip

The breadth of concepts we generate depends heavily on the functional intent we pose — the more solution-neutral the expression, the broader the set of concepts.

Draw out the meta-lesson from the previous slide before moving forward: the very act of laying out Figure 7.1 systematically — rather than jumping straight to “corkscrew” — is itself a technique, a form of what the book later formalizes (in Chapter 12) as structured creativity. Notice concretely how it worked: starting from “pulling” (the obvious, default process) and deliberately asking “what else could accomplish this operand’s translation?” is precisely what surfaced “pushing” (with injected gas) as an option nobody would have spontaneously proposed. Structuring the space of concepts, even informally, tends to surface options that unstructured brainstorming alone would miss.

Then introduce the second big idea on this slide: solution-neutral function doesn’t exist at just one fixed level of abstraction — it forms a genuine hierarchy. “Translating the cork” (our starting point) generalizes upward to “removing the cork,” which itself generalizes to “opening the bottle,” which generalizes further still to “accessing the wine.” Each step up trades specificity for breadth. The fragment states the resulting design tradeoff plainly, and it’s worth having students internalize this as a rule of thumb they’ll use for the rest of the course: the more solution-neutral (i.e., the higher up this hierarchy) your stated functional intent, the broader the resulting space of concepts you’ll generate — and, implicitly, the more work required to explore that space fully.

Figure 7.2 · A Hierarchy of Broader Concepts

Where to stop is a matter of practicality — with a wine bottle already on the table, concepts above “wine accessing” make no sense. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.2.

Walk through this hierarchy top to bottom, since it’s the concrete payoff of the previous slide’s abstract claim. At the very top sits “accessing the wine” — the most solution-neutral framing shown. That generalizes down (in the diagram’s downward-specializing direction) into “opening” the bottle or, quite differently, “removing” the cork by “destroying” it (drilling a hole, burning it, melting it — note this is a genuinely different branch from “removing” by “translating” the cork intact, which is the branch that eventually leads back to our corkscrew). “Opening” further specializes into “breaching” the cork or “breaching” the bottle itself (say, breaking the glass at the table edge). Follow the “translating” branch all the way down and you land exactly back at “pushing / shearing / pulling” from Figure 7.1 — this diagram literally extends the previous one upward by several more levels of generality.

Use the side-caption’s point to close the discussion of practicality: the architect always has to decide where in this hierarchy to stop and start working. There’s no formula for this — it’s a judgment call the book calls “a matter of practicality.” If a wine bottle is already physically sitting on the table in front of you, it would be absurd to consider concepts sitting above “wine accessing” (like, say, “beverage acquiring,” which might lead you to consider buying a different drink entirely) — the context of the actual problem constrains how far up the hierarchy is worth exploring.

7.2 Identifying the Solution-Neutral Function

Deriving a Solution-Neutral Statement

The procedure (Question 7a):

  • Consider the beneficiary
  • Identify their need
  • Identify the solution-neutral operand that, acted upon, yields the benefit
  • Identify the operand’s attribute
  • Identify other relevant attributes of the operand
  • Define the solution-neutral process that changes the benefit-related attribute
  • Identify relevant attributes of that process

Tip

Two new examples: an air transportation service (beneficiary: the traveler) and a home data network (beneficiary: the surfer).

This is the concrete step-by-step procedure implementing Question 7a, worth walking through as an ordered checklist students can literally follow on their own future projects. Start by considering who the beneficiary is — a specific stakeholder whose need we’re trying to fill, not an abstract “the system.” Identify their need in plain language. Then — this is the crucial move — identify the solution-neutral operand: the thing that, if acted upon, will actually deliver the benefit the beneficiary needs (note this is deliberately not yet a specific operand; it’s still expressed abstractly). Identify that operand’s benefit-related attribute — the specific property of the operand whose change constitutes delivering value. Optionally identify other relevant attributes of the operand that matter but aren’t themselves the value driver. Then define the solution-neutral process that changes that benefit-related attribute — again, stated abstractly, without committing to a specific mechanism. And finally, optionally identify relevant attributes of that process.

The fragment introduces the chapter’s two flagship worked examples, which we’ll carry through the whole remaining lecture in parallel: an air transportation service, where the beneficiary is the traveler, and a home data network, where the beneficiary is the surfer (the person using the network at home). The next slide (Table 7.2) works through this exact seven-step procedure concretely for both.

Walk through both columns of this table carefully, since it’s the direct application of the previous slide’s seven-step procedure — and pause specifically on why the operand choice in each case takes real thought, since it’s not obvious on first glance. For the transportation service: the beneficiary is the traveler, whose need is “visit a client in another city” — a conceptually rich statement, since visiting implies going somewhere, spending time there, and returning. The solution-neutral operand is, perhaps surprisingly to students at first, the traveler themselves — not an abstract “trip” or “distance.” The benefit-related attribute is the traveler’s location: what actually changes, and delivers value, is the traveler’s location changing from origin to destination. Other relevant attributes: the traveler needs to travel alone, with light luggage. The solution-neutral process is “changing,” which we can also call “transporting”; its own relevant attributes are that it must happen safely and on demand. Stitch this together into a single solution-neutral function statement, which is worth having students say aloud: “safely and on demand transporting to a new location a traveler with light luggage.” Notice — and this is the entire point of the exercise — nothing in that sentence says how the transporting happens. We are still entirely within the solution-neutral domain.

For the home network: the beneficiary is the surfer, whose need is “buy a cool book.” The solution-neutral operand is the book itself; its benefit-related attribute is ownership (what changes is who owns the book); other attributes include that the book should be consistent with the buyer’s tastes. The solution-neutral process is buying, with the attribute that it happens online. Combined: “buying online a book that the buyer likes.” Again — no reference yet to how the buying or the browsing happens; that comes later, once we introduce concept. Point out for students that these two solution-neutral functions now become the functional intent driving the rest of both worked examples for the remainder of the chapter.

Intent Vanishes When the System Operates

When a system operates, the intent vanishes. Watching a room full of servers, or a plane in flight, it’s hard to tell why — the architect must record intent, because once built, the system rarely says it back.

In summary: the functional intent for a system should be stated as a solution-neutral function — an operand and a process (with their attributes) linked to value, containing no reference to solution.

This slide makes a genuinely sobering, practical point that’s easy to underestimate until you’ve seen it happen. Once a system is actually operating, its underlying intent tends to become invisible — you cannot look at a room full of running servers and read off, just from observing them, why they exist or what benefit they were built to deliver. You cannot look at an airplane in flight and directly recover the stakeholder need that motivated its architecture. The book adds a memorable observation about how casually intent is normally handled in real organizations: it’s often discussed only informally, in a hallway conversation (“I thought we were building a retro-inspired minivan”), or captured loosely in a piece of concept art, rather than ever being written down rigorously.

This is exactly why the formalism of this chapter matters, even though it can feel a bit pedantic on first encounter: the architect has a specific responsibility to identify and record the system’s main functional intent, precisely because once the system is built, it can be genuinely difficult — sometimes impossible — to reconstruct that intent by simply examining the finished system.

The fragment restates the chapter’s core methodological rule as a clean, quotable summary, worth having students write down verbatim: functional intent should always be stated as a solution-neutral function — an operand and a process, along with their relevant attributes, all linked to the delivery of value, but containing absolutely no reference to how that value gets delivered. That “no reference to solution” clause is the entire discipline of this section in five words.

7.3 Concept

The Notion of Concept

  • The intellectual distance from solution-neutral function to architecture is a large gap to jump — concept bridges it
  • Concept is the transition point from solution-neutral to solution-specific
  • It must allow value-related functions to be executed, enabled by form; it establishes the vocabulary for the solution; it implicitly sets design parameters and the level of technology

Motivate why this section exists at all: having a clean, solution-neutral statement of functional intent (from Section 7.2) is necessary, but it’s still an enormous intellectual leap from that abstract statement all the way to a full, detailed system architecture (the kind of thing built in Chapter 6, and formally in Chapter 8). That gap is too large to jump in one step. Concept is the intellectual construct the book introduces specifically to bridge it — worth telling students explicitly that concept isn’t strictly logically necessary (you could, in principle, try to leap straight from intent to full architecture), but it’s enormously useful practically, because it gives us a much more tractable intermediate stopping point.

Give the three defining properties of concept precisely, since each one earns its place: concept is the specific transition point where thinking shifts from solution-neutral to solution-specific — this is the moment a real solution direction gets chosen, even if many details remain open. Concept must allow the value-related function to actually be executed, and that execution must be enabled by some (still abstracted) form — concept isn’t purely abstract; it has to at least gesture at a workable mechanism. And concept does real practical work beyond just “picking a direction”: it establishes the vocabulary that the rest of the design process will use, and it implicitly sets both design parameters and an implied level of technology, even before any of those things are explicitly specified. We’ll see this vocabulary-setting effect very concretely on the “Concept, Concretely” slide coming up.

Box 7.2 · Definition of Concept

Concept is a product or system vision, idea, notion, or mental image that maps function to form. It embodies a sense of how the system will function and an abstraction of the system form — a simplification of the architecture that allows for high-level reasoning.

Concept is not a system attribute, but a notional mapping between two attributes: form and function.

Give the formal definition and unpack it phrase by phrase, since every word is doing real work. Concept is a product or system vision — deliberately evocative, human, almost artistic language, not a rigorous engineering specification. It is a mapping of function to form — the exact same kind of mapping that full architecture eventually captures, just at a much coarser, more general level of resolution. It embodies both a sense of how the system will function and an abstraction of its form, and precisely because both sides are still abstracted, it functions as a simplification of the full architecture — one that’s tractable enough to reason about at a high level, before committing to the enormous amount of detail that a complete architecture requires.

The fragment makes a subtle but important ontological distinction, worth pausing on: concept is not itself an attribute of the system — it’s not a “thing the system has,” the way mass or color would be. Instead it’s a notional mapping connecting two attributes that the system does actually have: its form and its function. This distinction matters because it clarifies exactly what kind of intellectual object we’re working with — concept lives at the level of how we’re choosing to relate form and function to each other, not as a standalone property sitting alongside them.

Figure 7.3 · Concept and Architecture

Concept and architecture both map function to form — concept does it in a general way, architecture in elaborate detail. If you have a concept, it guides the architecture; if you have an architecture, concept rationalizes it. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.3.

Walk through this figure’s two panels as a direct visual statement of the relationship between concept and full architecture. On the left, concept is drawn as a coarse, general arrow inside a simple form-versus-function grid — a rough, low-resolution mapping. On the right, architecture is drawn as the same fundamental form-versus-function grid, but now filled in with a dense, fine-grained matrix of individual mapping cells — an elaborate, high-resolution version of exactly the same kind of mapping.

Give students the two-way relationship the side-caption states, since it’s genuinely useful in both directions of practice, not just in one. Going forward (synthesis): if you already have a clear concept in mind, it acts as a guide, constraining and directing how you go about building out the full, detailed architecture — this is the direction most of this chapter and the next are teaching. Going backward (analysis, which is what Chapters 4 through 6 practiced): if you’re instead handed a fully-built architecture and asked to understand it, concept rationalizes it — it lets you compress an overwhelming amount of architectural detail back down into a single, comprehensible, high-level story about what the system is fundamentally doing. Both directions matter, and good architects move fluidly between them.

Concept, Concretely

Tip

Pump: solution-neutral function is “moving fluid.” The concept is water (specific operand), pressurizing (specific process), using a centrifugal pump (specific instrument of form).

Tip

Bubblesort is literally the name of a concept: array (specific operand: entries), sequentially exchanging (specific process), using bubblesort (specific instrument).

  • Naming the specific instrument (centrifugal pump, bubblesort) immediately establishes a vocabulary, sets design parameters, and implies a level of technology

Ground the abstract definition in the two running examples students already know inside and out from Chapters 4 through 6, and use this to make the “concept sets vocabulary” claim from a few slides back genuinely concrete rather than just asserted. For the pump: the solution-neutral function, stated with total generality, is simply “moving fluid” — this says nothing about what fluid, or how. The concept specializes this: the operand becomes specifically water, the process becomes specifically pressurizing (one specific way to move a fluid, as opposed to, say, accelerating it or displacing it), and the instrument of form becomes specifically a centrifugal pump. Notice how much information just naming “centrifugal pump” instantly supplies, entirely for free: it immediately establishes a whole technical vocabulary (motor, housing, impeller — terms that would mean nothing without the concept having been chosen), it implicitly sets concrete design parameters (things like impeller speed and expected pressure rise become meaningful, measurable quantities), and it implicitly signals a level of technology (higher-tech than someone manually moving water with buckets, but lower-tech than exotic high-temperature turbomachinery used in jet engines).

The bubblesort example makes the same point even more sharply, because bubblesort is literally, by name, the concept itself — there’s no separating the name from the idea. The solution-neutral function is “sorting the array.” The concept specializes this to a specific operand (the array’s entries), a specific process (sequentially exchanging adjacent out-of-order entries), and a specific instrument (the bubblesort algorithm itself). Just saying “bubblesort” to anyone who’s studied algorithms instantly conjures a complete, specific vocabulary, a precise notion of how it works, and a full set of instrument objects — down to the actual code — needed to define the resulting architecture. Contrast this explicitly with how much less is pinned down before the concept is chosen (just “moving fluid,” or “sorting the array”) — this comparison is the whole lesson of the slide, worth stating as its own takeaway: choosing a concept is where an enormous amount of information about the eventual system gets committed, all at once.

Figure 7.4 · Template for Deriving Concept

Walk through this template’s three-column structure left to right, since it’s the diagram that formalizes everything discussed so far into a repeatable procedure. The leftmost column, labeled “Intent,” shows the solution-neutral operand sitting outside the dashed “Concept” boundary — this is the precursor, what we derived back in Section 7.2, and it is explicitly not itself part of the concept. The middle column, labeled “Function,” is where the specific operand and the specific operating process live — this is where the solution-neutral operand gets specialized into something concrete (note the specialization-triangle relationship connecting solution-neutral operand to specific operand, echoing the OPM notation from Chapter 3). The rightmost column, labeled “Form,” is where the specific instrument of form lives, connected to the specific operating process it enables.

Point out the five specifically highlighted shapes (thick borders in the figure) as the essential ingredients students should be able to name from memory: the solution-neutral operand (the precursor, outside the concept itself), the specific operand, the specific system operating (the specific process), the generic concept form, and the specific system form. Reconnect this template directly to the pump and bubblesort examples from the previous slide: for the pump, “moving fluid” is the solution-neutral function; water is the specific operand; pressurizing (one specific concept of operation among several possible for a pump) is the specific system operating; and a centrifugal pump is the specific system form. For bubblesort: the array is the solution-neutral operand; entries of the array are the specific operand; sequentially exchanging (as opposed to inserting, which is a different sorting concept) is the specific operating; and bubblesort itself (rather than quicksort, a different concept entirely) is the specific form. This template is the operational procedure students should use whenever they need to derive a concept from a solution-neutral statement of intent in their own project work.

Use this table as a comparative capstone, applying the exact same template from the previous slide across all five running examples students have now seen across Chapters 4 through 7, side by side. Walk down each row quickly to reinforce the pattern — fluid/moving specializes to water/pressurizing via a centrifugal pump; array/sorting specializes to array-entries/sequentially-exchanging via bubblesort; cork/translating specializes to cork/pulling via a screw; traveler/transporting specializes to traveler/flying via an airplane; book/buying specializes to internet/accessing via a home DSL connection.

The genuinely important teaching point is in the side-caption, worth dwelling on rather than rushing past: look carefully across these five rows and notice there is no single, consistent relationship between the solution-neutral operand and the specific operand. Sometimes they’re literally identical (cork stays cork; traveler stays traveler). Sometimes the specific operand is completely different from the solution-neutral one (book becomes internet — buying a book online really means, mechanically, accessing the internet). This directly foreshadows the very next slide (Box 7.3), which catalogs exactly this variety of specialization patterns explicitly. The lesson to land here: specializing from solution-neutral to specific concept is not a mechanical, rule-following process — it requires real creativity and domain judgment each time, and there’s no shortcut formula that works uniformly across all cases.

Box 7.3 · Specializing Function to Concept

A few of the patterns the specific operand can take, relative to the solution-neutral operand:

  • Completely different operand and process: entertaining a person by watching a DVD
  • Same process, different operand: choosing a leader by choosing the president
  • Part of the solution-neutral operand: opening the bottle by removing the cork
  • A type of the solution-neutral operand: moving fluid by pressurizing water
  • An attribute of the solution-neutral operand: amplifying a signal by increasing signal voltage

Tip

Similar patterns apply to specializing the process — e.g., transporting a traveler by flying a traveler.

This box directly cashes out the previous slide’s observation — since there’s no single relationship between solution-neutral and specific operand, here’s the actual catalog of patterns that can occur, so students at least have a checklist of possibilities to consider deliberately rather than specializing haphazardly. Walk through each pattern with its example: sometimes operand and process are both completely different from the solution-neutral statement — entertaining a person (solution-neutral) becomes watching a DVD (specific): neither “watching” nor “DVD” appears anywhere in “entertaining a person.” Sometimes the process stays exactly the same while only the operand changes — choosing a leader specializes to choosing the president; “choosing” doesn’t change, only what’s being chosen. Sometimes the specific operand is literally a part of the solution-neutral operand — opening the bottle (solution-neutral) is achieved by removing the cork (specific); the cork is physically part of the bottle system. Sometimes the specific operand is a type or subclass of the solution-neutral operand — moving fluid becomes pressurizing water; water is a type of fluid (this is the class/specialization relationship from Chapter 3, now applied to concept development). And sometimes the specific operand is merely an attribute of the solution-neutral operand — amplifying a signal becomes increasing signal voltage; voltage is an attribute of the signal, not the signal itself.

The fragment closes by noting the exact same variety of patterns recurs when specializing the process instead of the operand, even holding the operand fixed — transporting a traveler (solution-neutral process: transporting) specializes to flying a traveler (specific process: flying), where “traveler” doesn’t change at all, only the verb describing what happens to them. Encourage students, when they eventually do this for their own course projects, to consciously check their chosen concept against this catalog: which pattern does your specialization follow, and have you considered whether a different pattern might reveal concepts you hadn’t thought of?

For the transportation service: the specific operand stays “traveler” (matching the “same as solution-neutral” pattern from Box 7.3), with benefit-related attribute “location” (also unchanged), other attributes “alone with light luggage.” The specific process specializes “transporting” down to “flying,” with the attribute “in less than 2 hours.” Notice the “generic concept form” row — a placeholder the book calls a “dummy form,” here simply “Flyer” — that then specializes further into the actual specific form, “airplane,” with the attribute “commercial.” Put in plain language, as the book does: the resulting concept is “a single traveler with light luggage, flying in less than two hours, using a commercial airplane.”

For the home network: the specific operand jumps from “book” (the solution-neutral operand) to “internet” — an example of the “completely different operand” pattern from Box 7.3, since gaining access to a book online really means, mechanically, gaining access to the internet. The benefit-related attribute becomes “access,” with “high-speed connection” as another relevant attribute. The specific process is “gaining,” or “accessing,” with the attribute “reliably.” The generic concept form is a dummy placeholder, “Accesser,” specializing to the actual specific form, “home DSL connection,” with attribute “inexpensive.” In plain language: “reliably accessing a high-speed internet connection using an inexpensive home DSL network.” It’s worth having students say both plain-language sentences aloud, since — as the next slide points out — this operand-process-instrument construction reads almost exactly like a natural-language sentence, echoing the Chomsky noun-verb-noun parallel first raised back in Chapter 2.

Naming Concepts

  • No simple convention exists — rationally, concepts are named operand + process + instrument, as in light + emitting + diode
  • Naming for just the operand rarely works — a “mower” only makes sense because of the implicit “er” ending suggesting an instrument
  • Sometimes a concept is named for an attribute: “wireless” described two entirely different concepts, a century apart

Tip

Developing concepts is an open-ended creative process — the architect should build a rich set of alternatives before sorting and down-selecting (Ch. 11).

Give students an honest, practical note about naming conventions in real practice, since this is a place where formal rigor and everyday language diverge and it’s worth flagging directly. The book is upfront that there’s no single simple convention — but rationally, if you wanted to be fully systematic, a concept should be named operand + process + instrument. The light-emitting-diode example (LED) is the book’s cleanest case where this actually happens in common usage: light (operand) + emitting (process) + diode (instrument), all three parts present and legible in the name.

Point out the linguistic subtlety in the second bullet, since it’s a genuinely clever observation: naming a concept just by its operand almost never works on its own — you can’t just call something “grass” and expect anyone to understand what device you mean. What actually happens is we implicitly append an instrument-suggesting suffix, most commonly “-er,” and rely on that grammatical trick to imply the missing process and instrument — “mower” only makes sense because English speakers automatically parse the “-er” as “a thing that does the mowing,” even though “mow” (the process) and the actual cutting mechanism (the instrument) are both left completely unstated in the word itself.

The “wireless” example is a good closer, and worth telling as a brief story: the same single word, “wireless,” was used to name two completely different concepts roughly a century apart — around the start of the twentieth century it referred to radio broadcasting technology (literally “communication without wires”), and around the start of the twenty-first century it came to mean local data networking (Wi-Fi). Same attribute-based name, two unrelated underlying concepts, separated by a hundred years. The fragment then pivots toward the chapter’s remaining sections: naming aside, developing concepts is fundamentally an open-ended, creative process, and the architect’s job is to build a genuinely rich set of alternative concepts before doing any sorting or down-selecting — that selection process itself is deferred to Chapter 11, where formal stakeholder-driven criteria are developed.

Figure 7.5 · A Tree of Concept Options

An operand layer, a process layer, and an instrumental form layer. Fewer operand options than process options, and fewer process options than form options — a decision tree. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.5.

Introduce this as the general template for organizing concept alternatives, which the next several slides then apply concretely to each running example. The diagram organizes concept options into three explicit layers: an operand layer at top, a process layer in the middle (with potentially several specific operating options branching off a single specific operand), and an instrumental form layer at the bottom (with potentially several specific form options branching off each specific operating choice). This produces a genuine decision tree, and the shape of that tree follows a predictable, sensible pattern worth pointing out explicitly: there are typically fewer operand options than process options, and fewer process options than form options — the tree gets wider as you go down toward more concrete, specific choices, which makes intuitive sense, since there are usually many different physical or technological ways (form) to implement a given operating principle (process), but far fewer fundamentally different operating principles for a given choice of what’s being acted upon (operand).

Frame this explicitly as connecting back to the Section 7.1 discussion of why alternative concepts matter: recall the wine-cork example, where we deliberately generated pushing, shearing, and pulling as alternatives rather than jumping straight to a corkscrew. This tree structure is simply the general-purpose tool for doing that same kind of structured, systematic alternative-generation for any system — and the next few slides apply it directly to the pump, bubblesort, transportation, and home-network examples in turn.

Figure 7.6 · Pump Concept Options

Seven non-exhaustive concept options for moving fluid — from pressurizing with a centrifugal pump, to blowing air with a fan. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.6.

Apply the general decision-tree template directly to the pump example. Starting from the solution-neutral operand “fluid” and process “moving,” the tree branches by specific operand first — water versus air — and then, for water, by specific operating principle: pressurizing (which can be done by a centrifugal pump or an axial-flow pump), accelerating (a different operating principle, achievable by a different kind of centrifugal pump, or by a jet ejector), or displacing (achieved by a rotary or reciprocating displacement pump — a genuinely different working principle from either of the first two). For air as the operand, the operating principle shown is blowing, achieved by a fan.

Point out this gives seven distinct concept options total for “moving fluid” — explicitly called out as a non-exhaustive list, meaning even more exist beyond what’s shown. Have students notice something instructive: centrifugal pump appears twice in this tree, once under “pressurizing” and once under “accelerating” — the same instrument can implement two genuinely different operating principles, depending on how it’s used and configured; instrument, process, and operand really are three separable choices, not one bundled decision. This is a good moment for students to appreciate just how much richer the concept space is for “moving fluid” than the single default answer (“get a pump”) that most people would jump to without this kind of structured exploration.

Figure 7.7 · Bubblesort Concept Options

Three principles of operation for sorting an array — sequentially exchanging, inserting, displacing — each with specific algorithms. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.7.

Apply the same decision-tree template to sorting. Starting from “array” and “sorting,” with array entries as the specific operand throughout, the tree branches into three distinct operating principles: sequentially exchanging (implemented specifically by bubblesort, or alternatively by cocktail sort), inserting (implemented by insertion sort or shell sort), and displacing (implemented via a partitioning algorithm, most notably quicksort).

This is a genuinely satisfying example for students with any programming background, since it maps directly onto sorting algorithms they may already know by name but perhaps never organized this way — as three distinct underlying operating principles, each with multiple specific algorithmic instruments. It’s worth pointing out explicitly that quicksort — often taught as simply “a fast sorting algorithm” without much conceptual framing — sits under a genuinely different operating principle (displacing, via partitioning) than bubblesort or insertion sort (both of which work by moving individual elements one comparison at a time). This reinforces the general lesson from the pump example: concept options aren’t just “different implementations of the same idea” — some of them represent fundamentally different underlying operating principles, which the architect needs to recognize before comparing them fairly.

Figure 7.8 · Transportation Concept Options

Flying, rolling, and floating — each linked to common instruments. Traveler is the only likely operand; transporting is a long-standing human endeavor. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.8.

Apply the template to the transportation service, now genuinely scaling up in real-world complexity from the pump and sorting examples. Here the specific operand barely branches at all — the traveler is really the only sensible operand — but the specific process branches into three broad, long-standing human strategies for transporting: flying (via aircraft, either an airplane or a helicopter), rolling (via land transport, either a car or a train), and floating (via a boat, specifically a sailboat is given as one instrument example).

Note explicitly for students, as the side-caption highlights, that unlike the pump or sorting examples (which are relatively modern, narrow engineering problems), transporting a traveler is “a long-standing human endeavor” — humans have been solving this problem for millennia, across an enormous range of technologies, and the book deliberately limits the tree to just flying, rolling, and floating for tractability, rather than attempting genuine exhaustiveness. This is a good moment to ask students what other transporting concepts they could add to this tree that aren’t shown (walking, tunneling, space travel) — reinforcing that “non-exhaustive” is a real, working constraint the architect always has to accept.

Figure 7.9 · Home Network Concept Options

A far more limited option space — buying a book online basically requires accessing the Internet, so the only real options are how you access it. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.9.

Apply the template to the home network example, and use it to make a useful contrast with the previous three examples — this tree is genuinely narrower, and it’s worth having students articulate why. The specific operand (“Internet”) and specific process (“accessing”) barely branch at all, since — as established back when we specialized “buying a book” down to “accessing the internet” — there’s really no way around needing internet access to buy a book online in the first place. What actually branches is purely the specific instrument — the access point: home DSL, home cable, mobile broadband, public WiFi, or work cable.

The side-caption’s framing is worth stating explicitly as the lesson: not every solution-neutral function generates an equally rich space of concept alternatives. Sometimes, once you’ve worked through the solution-neutral-to-concept specialization, you discover the option space genuinely collapses down to “the same basic approach, with only the specific instrument varying” — here, essentially every viable concept is “access the internet somehow,” differing only in which access technology is used. This is a legitimate, useful outcome of the exploration process, not a failure of it — recognizing that a problem has a narrow solution space is itself valuable architectural insight, and it tells the architect where creative effort will and won’t pay off.

Broader Concepts and Hierarchy

  • The concept-option analysis looked “downward” toward increasing detail — but we can also look “upward” toward increasingly general intent
  • Why move fluid? Maybe to dry a basement. Why dry a basement? Maybe to improve living conditions
  • There is hierarchy in functional intent, just as there is hierarchy in built systems

The specific function at one level becomes the solution-neutral functional intent at the next level down.

Pivot the discussion’s direction, echoing exactly the “upward vs. downward” move already made once with the wine-bottle hierarchy (Figures 7.1–7.2) — now generalized and applied to all four running examples. Everything from Figures 7.5 through 7.9 explored downward, toward increasing specificity and detail. But we can equally well ask upward questions: why move fluid in the first place? Maybe the actual underlying goal is to dry a basement. And why dry a basement? Maybe the real underlying goal, one level further up, is simply to improve the living conditions of a house. State the general lesson plainly: just as built systems have hierarchy (Chapter 3), functional intent has hierarchy too — there’s no reason to assume the level at which you first stated a solution-neutral function is the “true” or “correct” level; it’s usually just wherever the conversation happened to start.

The fragment states the single most important mechanical insight of this whole discussion, worth writing on the board and having students repeat: the specific function at one level of this hierarchy becomes the solution-neutral functional intent at the next level down. This is a precise, recursive relationship, not just a vague metaphor — it means the entire Section 7.2/7.3 procedure (derive solution-neutral intent, then specialize it into a concept) can be applied repeatedly, chaining downward through as many levels as the architect finds useful, with each level’s output concept becoming the input solution-neutral statement for the level below it. The next two slides (Figures 7.10 and 7.11) show exactly this recursive chaining worked out concretely for both flagship examples.

Figure 7.10 · A Hierarchy Leading to Flying the Traveler

Closing a deal, by learning client preferences, by meeting the client, by traveling, by flying. At every level there are alternatives — a teleconference instead of a visit, a train instead of a flight. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.10.

Walk this hierarchy from top to bottom as the direct, worked demonstration of the recursive relationship just described. At the very top: “closing a deal” — arguably the ultimate underlying reason any of this transportation activity happens at all. That specializes down to “learning client preferences” as one way of pursuing the deal. Learning client preferences specializes to “meeting the client” as one specific way to learn them. Meeting the client specializes to “traveling” as one way of accomplishing the meeting. And traveling — the level we’ve been working at throughout the rest of the chapter — specializes down to “flying” as one specific transporting concept, exactly matching Table 7.4’s earlier result.

Draw out the point emphasized in the side-caption, since it’s the practically useful part: at every single level of this hierarchy, real alternatives exist that a narrower analysis, fixed at just one level, would never surface. Instead of learning client preferences by physically meeting them, you might study their published materials or attend a conference or trade show where they’re speaking. Instead of physically traveling to meet the client at all, you might hold a tele-meeting (a videoconference) instead — genuinely skipping the “traveling” step of the hierarchy entirely. Instead of flying, once travel is chosen, you might drive or take a train. The book’s own conclusion is worth stating directly: there is no single objectively “right” level at which to start examining a system’s goals — but it is genuinely useful for the architect to understand this hierarchy of intent extending upward, because it’s precisely at the higher levels that entirely different categories of solution (a teleconference instead of any physical travel at all) become visible.

Figure 7.11 · A Hierarchy Leading to Home Internet Access

Entertainment → reading books → buying books → buying online → accessing the network. Understanding this hierarchy might surface new options, like selling electronic books. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.11.

Walk this second hierarchy the same way, giving the home-network example equal treatment to the transportation one. Starting from the top: “entertaining” oneself is the broadest underlying intent, which specializes to “reading” as one specific way to be entertained (alongside listening or viewing, shown as sibling alternatives at that same level). Reading specializes to “buying” a book as one way of obtaining something to read (alongside borrowing, a genuine sibling alternative). Buying specializes to “buying online” as one specific channel (alongside buying in a physical store, or buying via a call center). And buying online finally specializes down to “accessing” the network — the level the rest of the chapter has been developed around.

The side-caption’s closing observation is worth spending real time on, because it demonstrates the practical payoff of doing this upward-hierarchy exercise, not just its conceptual tidiness: understanding this hierarchy might genuinely surface new business options that a narrower analysis would never reach — specifically, the book calls out “selling electronic books” as a possibility this broader view reveals. Notice that selling ebooks doesn’t require buying (in the traditional physical sense) or even a home network connection in the same way — it emerges only once you step back up to the “reading” level of intent and re-ask “what other ways could this need be met?” This is a genuinely good closing example for the whole first half of the chapter (Sections 7.1 through 7.3): the discipline of stating things solution-neutrally, and understanding the full hierarchy of intent both up and down, isn’t academic pedantry — it’s a concrete tool for finding business and design opportunities that would otherwise stay invisible.

Section 7.3 Summary

  • Concept is a system vision that maps function to form; it sets the solution vocabulary and design parameters, and rationalizes the architecture
  • Concept is derived by specializing the solution-neutral operand and process to a specific operand, process, and abstraction of form
  • There is no naming convention, and no single relationship between solution-neutral and specific — concept generation requires creativity
  • Concepts sit in a hierarchy: understanding it one or two levels up is very useful for the architect

Use this as a checkpoint before the chapter moves into its next major topic (integrated concepts). By this point students should be comfortable stating the formal definition of concept and explaining, in their own words, why it’s useful as an intermediate step between solution-neutral function and full architecture; walking through the Figure 7.4 template (solution-neutral operand → specific operand/process → specific form) on a system of their own choosing; recognizing that specialization from solution-neutral to specific has no single fixed pattern (Box 7.3’s catalog) and therefore genuinely requires creative judgment, not mechanical rule-following; and understanding that concepts — like the systems they describe — form a hierarchy, where looking one or two levels up from wherever you started can surface categorically different solution options (as dramatically demonstrated in Figures 7.10 and 7.11). This closes out Section 7.3’s development of “what a concept is and how to derive one.” Section 7.4, up next, tackles a different question: what happens when a single concept’s process turns out to be rich enough that it needs to be unpacked into several internal sub-processes?

7.4 Integrated Concepts

Rich Concepts and Concept Fragments

  • A concept’s process is often rich in meaning, and can be “unpacked” into several internal functions — visiting a client implies going there, spending time, and returning
  • An integrated concept is made of smaller concept fragments — one per internal process — combined recursively using the same concept-development procedure

Tip

Transporting has at least three important internal processes: lifting (overcoming gravity), propelling (overcoming drag), and guiding. Without all three, transporting doesn’t work.

Introduce Section 7.4’s central new idea by recalling a phrase used offhandedly several slides ago, back when “visiting a client” was first introduced — that need was described as “conceptually rich,” implying going somewhere, spending time there, and returning. That richness is exactly what this section formalizes: often, a concept’s process turns out to be dense enough in meaning that it can be unpacked, almost immediately, into several distinct internal functions that must all happen for the concept to actually work.

Define the key term precisely: an integrated concept is one built up out of smaller concept fragments, one for each of these unpacked internal processes — and each fragment is itself developed using exactly the same operand-process-instrument procedure we’ve been using throughout the chapter, just applied recursively, one level down. This is a direct callback to the recursion idea from Chapter 3: the same conceptual machinery (specialize operand and process, choose an instrument) gets reapplied at a finer grain, internal to the top-level concept.

The fragment gives the chapter’s primary worked example for the rest of this section: “transporting,” it turns out, has at least three genuinely necessary internal processes — lifting (overcoming gravity), propelling (overcoming drag/resistance), and guiding (controlling direction). Emphasize the word “necessary” here: without accomplishing all three, transporting simply doesn’t work as a concept — you can’t have a vehicle that overcomes gravity and drag but has no way to steer, for instance. This sets up the morphological-matrix analysis on the very next slide.

The Morphological Matrix

  • For a car, lifting, propelling, and guiding are all done by wheels — a car is wheels-wheels-wheels
  • A train differs only in guiding — wheels-wheels-ground
  • Combining one instrument per internal process across many choices reveals a surprising number of integrated concepts — this structured layout is a morphological matrix (covered in full in Ch. 14)

Make the “wheels-wheels-wheels” shorthand concrete and a little playful, since it’s a genuinely memorable way to compress a vehicle’s whole architecture down to three words. For a car, all three necessary internal processes from the previous slide — lifting, propelling, and guiding — happen to be accomplished by exactly the same instrument, the wheels: the wheels lift the car off the ground (supporting its weight against gravity), the wheels propel it forward (via friction with the road, driven by the engine), and the wheels guide it (via steering). Hence: wheels-wheels-wheels.

A train, the book points out, differs from a car in exactly one of these three internal processes: lifting and propelling are still accomplished by wheels, but guiding is instead accomplished by the rail/ground itself (the rails physically constrain the train’s path, rather than any active steering mechanism) — hence wheels-wheels-ground. This is a striking illustration: cars and trains, which feel like completely different categories of vehicle to most people, differ conceptually in exactly one internal-process instrument choice out of three.

Introduce the formal name for the structured layout that makes this kind of comparison possible: a morphological matrix — one row per internal process, one column per specific instrument choice for that process, letting you combine one selection per row across many possible instrument choices to enumerate a surprising number of distinct integrated concepts systematically, rather than by ad hoc guessing. Flag for students that morphological matrices get covered in much fuller mathematical and computational detail in Chapter 14 (part of the course’s later, more quantitative Part 4) — here we’re just seeing the basic idea and its payoff.

Table 7.6 · Morphological Matrix for Transporting

Each column is an integrated concept. Aircraft and gliders differ only in propulsion; airships and submarines are conceptually identical, differing only in operating medium. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Table 7.6.

Walk through this table’s structure and then draw out two of its most striking observed patterns, since they’re excellent illustrations of what a morphological matrix is actually good for. The three rows are exactly our three necessary internal processes — lifting, propelling, guiding — and each row lists several possible specific-instrument choices (wheels, propeller, wings, closed hull, open hull for lifting; wheels, propeller, jet, gravity for propelling; wheels, propeller, rudder, “ground” for guiding). Eleven columns across the top name eleven different integrated vehicle concepts (car, train, jet aircraft, propeller aircraft, helicopter, airship, glider, boat, submarine, jet boat, hydrofoil), each one defined by exactly which instrument, in each row, gets marked with an X for that column.

Point students to two comparisons explicitly called out in the side-caption, since they’re genuinely illuminating. First: aircraft (propeller-driven) and gliders are lifted the same way (wings) and guided the same way (rudder) — they differ in exactly one row, propelling: powered aircraft use a propeller, while a glider uses gravity itself (converting altitude into forward motion, with no active propulsion at all). Conceptually, a glider is simply “an aircraft with the propulsion instrument removed.” Second: airships and submarines turn out to be conceptually identical across all three internal processes in this matrix, differing only in which medium they operate in (air versus water) — a comparison most people would never think to draw, since one flies and one dives, but the underlying morphology is the same. This is exactly the kind of unexpected structural insight a systematic morphological matrix can surface that ad hoc, intuition-only brainstorming typically misses.

Integrating the Home Data Network

  • The home network has five key internal processes: connecting the local network to the ISP; modulating the ISP carrier signal; managing data on the local network; connecting user devices; interacting with the user
  • Combining one instrument per process (with some flexibility — a residential gateway and a separate WAP can both manage the network) yields an integrated concept

Apply the exact same integrated-concept methodology to the second running example, giving it equal depth to the transportation case. The home network’s rich process (“accessing,” from our earlier concept work) unpacks into five distinct internal processes — worth having students briefly explain in their own words what each one physically means: connecting the local network to the internet service provider (the “last mile” connection); modulating the ISP’s carrier signal (translating between the raw signal format on the wire and something local devices can use — a modem’s job); managing data on the local network (routing traffic among local devices, and to/from the internet — a gateway or router’s job); connecting user devices to that local network (Wi-Fi, Ethernet, and so on); and interacting with the user (the actual device the surfer touches — laptop, phone, and so on).

Point out the caveat in the second bullet, since it’s a useful nuance beyond the strict car/train example: unlike lifting-propelling-guiding, where the book’s examples always picked exactly one instrument per process, real integrated concepts sometimes have a bit more flexibility — a single physical device might combine what look like separate rows (a residential gateway performing both signal modulation and local-network management), or a function might genuinely be split across multiple devices at once (a residential gateway and a separate wireless access point, both simultaneously contributing to “managing local network data”). This is worth flagging honestly: morphological matrices are a powerful organizing tool, but real integrated concepts don’t always map onto them with perfectly clean, one-instrument-per-row simplicity — the architect sometimes needs to use judgment about how strictly to apply the framework.

Figure 7.12 · An Integrated Concept for the Home Network

A DSL modem plus a box combining a residential gateway and Ethernet switch, with both WiFi and Ethernet connections to user devices. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.12.

Walk through this figure as one concrete, fully-specified integrated concept resulting from the previous slide’s five internal processes — trace the signal path physically, left to right, since that’s the clearest way to make the diagram land. The internet arrives from the internet service provider’s side (through a broadband remote access server and DSLAM at the telecom exchange center) over a phone line to a DSL modem in the home. From there, an integrated box — combining a residential gateway and an Ethernet switch in one physical device — handles local network management. From that integrated box, wired Ethernet connections reach a desktop PC and a printer, while a separate wireless access point provides WiFi connectivity (2.4 GHz radio) to a laptop and a cell phone.

Point out this is precisely “Integrated Concept 1” from the upcoming Table 7.8 comparison — one specific, complete assignment of an instrument to each of the five internal processes (DSL for connecting to the ISP; a dedicated DSL modem for modulating the carrier; the combined residential-gateway-plus-switch-plus-WAP for managing local data; WiFi and Ethernet together for connecting user devices; and laptop, cell phone, desktop, and printer for interacting with the user). This is worth using as a template for how students might diagram their own future integrated-concept designs — notice how much more concrete and buildable this figure feels compared to the earlier, purely functional Figure 3.6-style OPM diagrams, precisely because every box here is now a specific instrument choice, not a solution-neutral placeholder.

Section 7.4 Summary

  • Rich concepts often unfold into a set of internal processes that must all be executed — choosing a specific operand, process, and instrument for each defines a concept fragment
  • The architect explores and recombines concept fragments to build desirable integrated concepts
  • The morphological matrix organizes this combinatorics — one column per integrated concept, one row per fragment choice

Use this as a checkpoint before the chapter’s final section. By now students should be able to explain, given any sufficiently rich top-level concept, how to identify its necessary internal processes (as we did with transporting → lifting/propelling/guiding, and accessing → the five home-network processes); define what a concept fragment is (a specific operand-process-instrument choice for one internal process, developed using the exact same procedure as any top-level concept); explain how fragments recombine into an integrated concept (one instrument choice per internal process, combined together into a coherent whole, as in “wheels-wheels-wheels” for a car); and use a morphological matrix to organize and compare multiple integrated-concept alternatives systematically, rather than relying purely on intuition. With concept and integrated concept now both fully defined, one piece of the conceptual-development puzzle remains, which the chapter’s final section addresses: concept tells us what the system’s form and function are — but says relatively little yet about how the system actually behaves, moment to moment, once operating. That’s Section 7.5’s subject: concept of operations.

7.5 Concepts of Operations and Services

Concept of Operations (“ConOps”)

  • Function is a somewhat static view of what a system can do; operation is the sequence of things leading to delivery of the primary function — what the system actually does (Ch. 6)
  • The concept of operations sketches out how the system will operate: who operates it, when, and coordinated with what else
  • The relationship between concept and architecture is the same as between concept of operations and a detailed operations sequence

Introduce the chapter’s final major topic by first recalling a distinction from Chapter 6, since concept of operations builds directly on it: function is a somewhat static description of what a system can do (its capability), while operation — as defined back in Chapter 6 — is the actual sequence of events leading to delivery of that primary function: what the system does, unfolding in time. Function tells you the system can pressurize water; operation tells you the actual step-by-step sequence by which pressurizing happens on a given day, with a given operator, under given conditions.

Define concept of operations (commonly abbreviated “ConOps,” a term worth giving students explicitly since it’s standard industry vocabulary they’ll encounter constantly in real engineering practice) as a sketch, at a conceptual level, of exactly how the system will operate: who operates it, at what times, and coordinated with what else going on simultaneously. The third bullet draws an explicit, elegant parallel worth spending a moment on: the relationship between concept of operations and a fully detailed operations sequence is exactly analogous — the same kind of relationship — as between concept (from earlier in this chapter) and full detailed architecture (Chapter 6, and formally, Chapter 8). In both cases, we have a coarse, high-level sketch that guides (and is later rationalized by) a much more elaborate, fully worked-out detailed version.

Service vs. Good

If the enterprise transfers the instrument, it is a good (an aircraft manufacturer sells airplanes). If it transfers the function, it is a service (an airline sells transportation).

From the aircraft’s own conops, the aircraft is the operand — it’s loaded, flown, maintained. From the perspective of the service, the aircraft is the instrument — it transports the traveler.

Introduce a genuinely useful business distinction that concept of operations helps clarify precisely. The dividing line between selling a good and selling a service, stated this precisely, comes down to exactly what gets transferred from the enterprise to the customer: if the enterprise transfers the physical instrument itself, that’s a good — an aircraft manufacturer (like Boeing, our Chapter 1 example) sells the physical airplane itself as a good. If the enterprise instead transfers the function — the benefit, without transferring the underlying instrument — that’s a service: an airline doesn’t sell you an airplane, it sells you the function of being transported, using an airplane it retains ownership of.

The fragment introduces a genuinely subtle and important perspective shift, worth taking slowly, since it directly sets up the next slide’s diagram. From the aircraft’s own concept of operations — thinking purely about how the aircraft itself operates — the aircraft is the operand: it’s the thing being acted on (loaded with passengers and cargo, flown, maintained). But from the perspective of the service being sold to the traveler, that exact same aircraft flips roles entirely and becomes the instrument: it’s the tool used to transport the traveler, who is now the operand whose location is being changed. Same physical object, two entirely different roles (operand versus instrument), depending purely on which system’s concept of operations you’re examining — the aircraft’s own, or the transportation service built on top of it. This dual perspective is worked out visually, side by side, on the next slide.

Notice how much more operational detail this contains than the simple statement of function (“moving fluid”-style) we worked with earlier in the chapter — passengers and baggage appear here only as things being loaded onto and unloaded from the aircraft, essentially treated as cargo from the aircraft’s own operational point of view.

The right side is the concept of the service, seen instead from the traveler’s side: the traveler plans the trip and purchases a ticket; arrives and checks in (and checks baggage); is loaded onto the aircraft (explicitly marked “is” the same loading event, connecting the two diagrams); is transported (again explicitly marked “is” the same core transporting process from the left side); is unloaded on arrival; and finally checks out of the airport. Point out the crucial modeling insight the book draws from this: the details of taxi, takeoff, climb, and landing — hugely important to the aircraft’s own concept of operations — are relatively unimportant to the traveler’s experience of the service; the traveler mostly just cares that transporting happened, safely and reasonably promptly. Same underlying process, two genuinely different levels of relevant detail, depending on whose concept of operations you’re viewing it through.

A Service Is a System

  • A service can be architected the same way as a system — it is simply more process-focused than a product
  • The concept of operations contains information vital to understanding the system architecture: it’s much easier to predict how a system delivers value once you know how it operates

Tip

Details of taxi, takeoff, and the like are relatively unimportant to the traveler — but very important to the aircraft’s own concept of operations. Same system, different conops, depending on perspective.

State the section’s central conclusion plainly, since it resolves a question that might have been implicitly nagging students since “air transportation service” was first introduced back at the start of the chapter: is a service even the same kind of thing as the physical systems (pumps, sorting algorithms, home networks) this course has been analyzing all along? The answer is yes, unambiguously — a service can be architected using exactly the same tools and methods as any other system; the only real difference is one of emphasis: a service is simply more heavily process-focused than a typical physical product, but everything about entities, form, function, and emergence from Chapters 2 and 3 still applies fully.

The second bullet makes a genuinely important methodological point about why concept of operations matters enough to earn its own section of this chapter: it contains information that is vital to actually understanding the system’s architecture, information that pure function statements alone don’t capture. Knowing how a system operates — the actual sequence of events, who’s involved, what’s coordinated with what — makes it dramatically easier to predict how the system will actually deliver value in practice, compared to just knowing its static functional capability.

The fragment restates, one final time, the perspective-dependence lesson from the previous two slides — worth repeating because it’s genuinely easy to forget in practice: exactly the same underlying system (the aircraft) has details that are hugely important in one concept of operations (its own — taxi, takeoff, climb, landing) and nearly irrelevant in a different, equally valid concept of operations built on top of it (the traveler’s service experience). Neither view is more “correct” than the other; they’re both legitimate, and which one matters depends entirely on whose operational perspective you’re taking.

Section 7.5 Summary

  • The concept of operations defines, at a conceptual level, how the system actually operates when it delivers value
  • Concepts of operations can be defined both for the system itself, and for any service built upon it

A brief checkpoint before the full chapter summary. Students should now be comfortable with concept of operations as the operational counterpart to concept itself — concept answers “what is this system, roughly, in terms of form and function,” while concept of operations answers “how does it actually run, in sequence, when it’s delivering that value.” And they should understand the service/good distinction (transferring the instrument versus transferring the function), along with the perspective-dependent operand/instrument role-swap that occurs when a service is built on top of an underlying system, as vividly illustrated by the aircraft/transportation-service example. With concept, integrated concept, and concept of operations all now covered, the chapter is ready to step back and summarize the whole synthetic process it has developed.

Chapter 7 Summary

  • This chapter transitioned from simple to more complex systems, and from purely analytical to the beginning of a synthetic approach to architecture
  • The synthetic process begins with the stakeholder’s need, expressed as a solution-neutral function — then specializes the operand, process, and instrument to define a concept
  • For almost any solution-neutral function there are many possible concepts; the architect creatively develops, sorts, and selects among them
  • Rich concepts unfold into concept fragments, recombined into an integrated concept — and a concept of operations describes how it all actually runs

Tip

Reference: Crawley, E., Cameron, B., & Selva, D. (2016). System Architecture: Strategy and Product Development for Complex Systems. Pearson. Chapter 7.

Use this final summary to retrace the chapter’s full arc for students, in order, since it genuinely built one coherent procedure step by step rather than being a loose collection of topics. We started by naming the pivot from reverse engineering (Chapters 4–6, analyzing systems that already exist) to forward engineering (this chapter, starting from nothing and working toward a design) — and introduced two more realistically complex running examples, the transportation service and the home network, to replace the pump and bubblesort going forward.

The synthetic process itself has a clear sequence, worth having students recite: start from the stakeholder’s actual need; express that need as a solution-neutral function (an operand and process, with attributes, containing zero reference to solution — Section 7.2); specialize that solution-neutral operand, process, and now also instrument, into a concept (Section 7.3) — and recognize that for almost any solution-neutral function, many different concepts can satisfy it, which is exactly why the architect’s job here is fundamentally creative, not mechanical, requiring deliberate generation and comparison of alternatives (Figures 7.5 through 7.9) rather than settling for the first idea that comes to mind. When a concept’s process turns out to be rich enough to unpack into multiple necessary internal processes, each one becomes its own concept fragment, and the fragments recombine into a full integrated concept (Section 7.4) — with the morphological matrix as the tool for organizing and comparing that combinatorics. And finally, a concept of operations (Section 7.5) fills in the missing operational dimension: not just what the system is, but how it actually runs, in sequence, when delivering value — a distinction that also clarifies the difference between selling a good and selling a service.
