Box 7.1 · Principle of Solution-Neutral Function

“We cannot solve our problems with the same thinking we used when we created them.” — Albert Einstein

  • Poor system specifications frequently contain clues about an intended solution, function, or form — and those clues narrow the architect’s options before exploration even begins
  • Use solution-neutral functions where possible, and use the hierarchy of solution-neutral statements to scope how broad an exploration should be
  • First named principle — connect the Einstein quote to something concrete: specs that smuggle in a solution disguised as a requirement
  • Happens constantly, often invisibly: “add a corkscrew to the kitchen drawer inventory” already silently decided the solution before anyone asked the actual need (getting the cork out, or more broadly, accessing the wine)
  • → Such a clue narrows exploration before it starts, foreclosing whole classes of alternatives (gas pressure, suction — next couple slides)
  • Prescription (have students repeat it back): state functions solution-neutrally — what, never how — and use the hierarchy of solution-neutral statements to scope how broad an exploration should be
  • → Sets up the rest of the chapter as a rigorous methodology for exactly this

Figure 7.1 · Concepts for Removing a Wine Bottle Cork

  • A corkscrew opens a wine bottle. But can we name other concepts for the same job?
  • We describe concepts as an operand-process-instrument set: cork (operand) pushing (process) injected gas (instrument)
  • Pulling alone is insufficient to describe a concept — “cork pulling with a screw” is a corkscrew

Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.1.

  • Chapter’s running worked example — simple, everyone has physical intuition, why the book leads with it
  • Everyone defaults to “corkscrew” — push further: how do you describe a corkscrew generally? Uses a screw to pull. Is pulling the only way?
  • Get creative: glue a tab and pull (no screw); create suction around the cork (real household product — hollow needle + hand pump); pressurize air inside the bottle, pushing the cork out; wine steward’s fork-like device that shears the cork’s sides off
  • Diagram = structured operand-process-instrument: operand always cork; process varies (pushing/shearing/pulling); each process has its own instruments (gas for pushing; fork/tube for shearing; suction/screw for pulling)
  • → “Pulling” alone doesn’t specify a concept — need the instrument too. “Cork pulling with a screw” = corkscrew; pulling with suction is an entirely different concept sharing the same process

Solution-Neutral Function in a Hierarchy

  • Drawing Figure 7.1 is itself a form of structured creativity (Ch. 12) — pulling led us to think about pushing, which we might otherwise have missed
  • Solution-neutral function exists in a hierarchy: translating the cork generalizes to removing the cork, which generalizes to opening the bottle, up to accessing the wine

Tip

The breadth of concepts we generate depends heavily on the functional intent we pose — the more solution-neutral the expression, the broader the set of concepts.

  • Meta-lesson from the previous slide: laying out Figure 7.1 systematically (rather than jumping to “corkscrew”) is itself a technique — formalized later (Ch. 12) as structured creativity
  • Starting from “pulling” and asking “what else could do this?” is precisely what surfaced “pushing” (gas) — nobody proposes that spontaneously
  • → Structuring the concept space, even informally, surfaces options unstructured brainstorming misses
  • Second big idea: solution-neutral function forms a genuine hierarchy, not one fixed abstraction level — “translating the cork” → “removing the cork” → “opening the bottle” → “accessing the wine”; each step up trades specificity for breadth
  • → Rule of thumb for the rest of the course: the more solution-neutral (higher up the hierarchy) the stated intent, the broader the resulting concept space — and the more work to explore it fully

Figure 7.2 · A Hierarchy of Broader Concepts

Where to stop is a matter of practicality — with a wine bottle already on the table, concepts above “wine accessing” make no sense. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.2.

  • Walk top to bottom — concrete payoff of the previous slide’s abstract claim
  • Top: “accessing the wine” (most solution-neutral) → specializes down to “opening” the bottle, or “removing” the cork by “destroying” it (drilling, burning, melting — a different branch from “translating” the cork intact, which leads back to the corkscrew)
  • “Opening” further specializes into “breaching” the cork or “breaching” the bottle itself
  • Follow “translating” all the way down → lands exactly at “pushing/shearing/pulling” from Figure 7.1 — this diagram extends the previous one upward
  • → Practicality: the architect always decides where in the hierarchy to stop — no formula, a judgment call. Wine bottle already on the table? Absurd to consider concepts above “wine accessing” (e.g. “beverage acquiring”) — the actual problem context constrains how far up is worth exploring

7.2 Identifying the Solution-Neutral Function

Deriving a Solution-Neutral Statement

The procedure (Question 7a):

  • Consider the beneficiary
  • Identify their need
  • Identify the solution-neutral operand that, acted upon, yields the benefit
  • Identify the operand’s attribute
  • Identify other relevant attributes of the operand
  • Define the solution-neutral process that changes the benefit-related attribute
  • Identify relevant attributes of that process

Tip

Two new examples: an air transportation service (beneficiary: the traveler) and a home data network (beneficiary: the surfer).

  • Concrete step-by-step procedure for Question 7a — an ordered checklist students can follow on future projects:
    • Consider who the beneficiary is (specific stakeholder, not abstract “the system”)
    • Identify their need in plain language
    • Crucial move: identify the solution-neutral operand — the thing that, acted upon, delivers the benefit (still abstract, not specific yet)
    • Identify the operand’s benefit-related attribute — the property whose change delivers value
    • Optionally, other relevant attributes of the operand
    • Define the solution-neutral process that changes that attribute (still abstract)
    • Optionally, attributes of that process
  • → Two flagship worked examples for the rest of the lecture: air transportation service (beneficiary: traveler), home data network (beneficiary: surfer). Table 7.2 next works this exact procedure for both

  • Transportation: beneficiary = traveler, need = “visit a client in another city” (conceptually rich: going, spending time, returning).   • Home network: beneficiary = surfer, need = “buy a cool book.” Operand = the book; attribute = ownership; other attribute = consistent with tastes. Process = buying, attribute = online
  • → Combined: “buying online a book that the buyer likes” — again no how yet
  • These two statements become the functional intent driving both worked examples for the rest of the chapter

Intent Vanishes When the System Operates

When a system operates, the intent vanishes. Watching a room full of servers, or a plane in flight, it’s hard to tell why — the architect must record intent, because once built, the system rarely says it back.

In summary: the functional intent for a system should be stated as a solution-neutral function — an operand and a process (with their attributes) linked to value, containing no reference to solution.

  • Sobering, practical point — easy to underestimate until seen: once a system operates, its intent becomes invisible
  • Can’t look at a room of running servers and read off why they exist. Can’t look at a plane in flight and recover the stakeholder need
  • Book’s observation: intent is normally handled casually in real orgs — hallway conversations (“I thought we were building a retro-inspired minivan”), loose concept art, rarely written down rigorously
  • → This is why this chapter’s formalism matters (even if pedantic at first): the architect must record intent, because reconstructing it later from the finished system can be genuinely difficult or impossible
  • Core rule, worth writing down verbatim: functional intent = solution-neutral function — operand + process + attributes, linked to value, no reference to solution

7.3 Concept

The Notion of Concept

  • The intellectual distance from solution-neutral function to architecture is a large gap to jump — concept bridges it
  • Concept is the transition point from solution-neutral to solution-specific
  • It must allow value-related functions to be executed, enabled by form; it establishes the vocabulary for the solution; it implicitly sets design parameters and the level of technology
  • Motivate why this section exists: a clean solution-neutral statement (Section 7.2) is necessary, but it’s still an enormous leap to a full detailed architecture (Ch. 6/8) — too large to jump in one step
  • Concept bridges that gap — not strictly necessary logically, but enormously useful practically as a tractable intermediate stopping point
  • Three defining properties:
    • Transition point where thinking shifts from solution-neutral to solution-specific
    • Must allow the value-related function to be executed, enabled by some (still abstracted) form
    • Establishes vocabulary for the rest of the design process; implicitly sets design parameters and level of technology
  • → We’ll see the vocabulary-setting effect concretely on “Concept, Concretely” coming up

Box 7.2 · Definition of Concept

Concept is a product or system vision, idea, notion, or mental image that maps function to form. It embodies a sense of how the system will function and an abstraction of the system form — a simplification of the architecture that allows for high-level reasoning.

Concept is not a system attribute, but a notional mapping between two attributes: form and function.

  • Unpack the formal definition phrase by phrase — every word doing real work
  • Concept is a vision — deliberately evocative, human language, not a rigorous engineering spec
  • It’s a mapping of function to form — same kind of mapping full architecture eventually captures, just coarser resolution
  • Embodies a sense of function and an abstraction of form → a simplification of the full architecture, tractable enough to reason about at a high level
  • → Ontological distinction (pause on this): concept is not an attribute of the system (not a “thing it has,” like mass or color) — it’s a notional mapping connecting the form attribute and the function attribute. Lives at the level of how we’re choosing to relate them, not as a standalone property

Figure 7.3 · Concept and Architecture

Concept and architecture both map function to form — concept does it in a general way, architecture in elaborate detail. If you have a concept, it guides the architecture; if you have an architecture, concept rationalizes it. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.3.

  • Two panels, direct visual statement: left = concept, a coarse general arrow in a simple form-vs-function grid; right = architecture, same grid but filled with a dense fine-grained matrix — elaborate, high-resolution version of the same mapping
  • Two-way relationship, both useful in practice:
    • Forward (synthesis): concept as a guide, constraining how you build out the full architecture — most of this chapter and the next
    • Backward (analysis, Ch. 4-6’s practice): concept rationalizes an existing architecture — compresses overwhelming detail into one comprehensible high-level story
  • → Both directions matter; good architects move fluidly between them

Concept, Concretely

Tip

Pump: solution-neutral function is “moving fluid.” The concept is water (specific operand), pressurizing (specific process), using a centrifugal pump (specific instrument of form).

Tip

Bubblesort is literally the name of a concept: array (specific operand: entries), sequentially exchanging (specific process), using bubblesort (specific instrument).

  • Naming the specific instrument (centrifugal pump, bubblesort) immediately establishes a vocabulary, sets design parameters, and implies a level of technology
  • Ground the abstract definition in the two examples students know cold from Ch. 4-6 — makes “concept sets vocabulary” concrete, not just asserted
  • Pump: solution-neutral function = “moving fluid” (says nothing about what fluid or how). Concept specializes: operand → water, process → pressurizing, instrument → centrifugal pump
  • → Naming “centrifugal pump” supplies, for free: a technical vocabulary (motor, housing, impeller), concrete design parameters (impeller speed, pressure rise), and an implied level of technology (above buckets, below jet-engine turbomachinery)
  • Bubblesort makes the point even sharper — the name is literally the concept. Solution-neutral function = “sorting the array” → specific operand (array entries), process (sequentially exchanging), instrument (the bubblesort algorithm itself)
  • → Just saying “bubblesort” conjures a complete vocabulary, mechanism, and instrument set (down to the code). Contrast with how little is pinned down before the concept is chosen — choosing a concept commits an enormous amount of information all at once

  • → Foreshadows Box 7.3’s catalog of specialization patterns. Lesson: specializing to concept is not mechanical — requires real creativity and judgment, no shortcut formula

Box 7.3 · Specializing Function to Concept

A few of the patterns the specific operand can take, relative to the solution-neutral operand:

  • Completely different operand and process: entertaining a person by watching a DVD
  • Same process, different operand: choosing a leader by choosing the president
  • Part of the solution-neutral operand: opening the bottle by removing the cork
  • A type of the solution-neutral operand: moving fluid by pressurizing water
  • An attribute of the solution-neutral operand: amplifying a signal by increasing signal voltage

Tip

Similar patterns apply to specializing the process — e.g., transporting a traveler by flying a traveler.

  • Cashes out the previous slide’s observation — catalog of the patterns that can occur, so students have a checklist rather than specializing haphazardly
  • Completely different: entertaining a person → watching a DVD (neither word appears in the original)
  • Same process, different operand: choosing a leader → choosing the president
  • Part of the solution-neutral operand: opening the bottle → removing the cork (cork is part of the bottle)
  • A type of the solution-neutral operand: moving fluid → pressurizing water (water is a type of fluid — the class/specialization relationship from Ch. 3, now applied to concepts)
  • An attribute of the solution-neutral operand: amplifying a signal → increasing signal voltage
  • → Same variety of patterns recurs when specializing the process instead, holding operand fixed: transporting a traveler → flying a traveler (“traveler” unchanged, only the verb changes)
  • Encourage students, in their own project work, to check their concept against this catalog: which pattern does it follow, and would a different pattern reveal concepts they hadn’t considered?

Naming Concepts

  • No simple convention exists — rationally, concepts are named operand + process + instrument, as in light + emitting + diode
  • Naming for just the operand rarely works — a “mower” only makes sense because of the implicit “er” ending suggesting an instrument
  • Sometimes a concept is named for an attribute: “wireless” described two entirely different concepts, a century apart

Tip

Developing concepts is an open-ended creative process — the architect should build a rich set of alternatives before sorting and down-selecting (Ch. 11).

  • Honest, practical note: no single simple naming convention — but rationally, systematic naming = operand + process + instrument
  • LED = the book’s cleanest real example: light (operand) + emitting (process) + diode (instrument), all three legible in the name
  • Linguistic subtlety: naming by operand alone rarely works (“grass” means nothing as a device name) — English implicitly appends “-er” to imply the missing process/instrument. “Mower” works because “-er” is parsed as “a thing that does the mowing,” even though “mow” and the actual mechanism stay unstated
  • “Wireless” story: same word named two completely different concepts a century apart — early 1900s = radio broadcasting (literally no wires), early 2000s = local data networking (Wi-Fi)
  • → Naming aside, developing concepts is open-ended and creative — build a rich set of alternatives before sorting/down-selecting (deferred to Ch. 11’s formal stakeholder-driven criteria)

Figure 7.8 · Transportation Concept Options

Flying, rolling, and floating — each linked to common instruments. Traveler is the only likely operand; transporting is a long-standing human endeavor. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.8.

  • Template applied to the transportation service — scaling up real-world complexity from pump/sorting
  • Specific operand barely branches (traveler is really the only sensible one) — process branches into three long-standing strategies: flying (airplane, helicopter), rolling (car, train), floating (boat, e.g. sailboat)
  • → Unlike pump/sorting (modern, narrow engineering problems), transporting is “a long-standing human endeavor” — millennia of technologies; the book limits the tree to these three for tractability, not exhaustiveness
  • Ask students what’s missing (walking, tunneling, space travel) — reinforces that “non-exhaustive” is a real constraint the architect must accept

Figure 7.9 · Home Network Concept Options

A far more limited option space — buying a book online basically requires accessing the Internet, so the only real options are how you access it. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.9.

  • Template applied to the home network — useful contrast: this tree is genuinely narrower, ask students to articulate why
  • Operand (“Internet”) and process (“accessing”) barely branch — no way around needing internet access to buy a book online. Only the instrument branches: home DSL, home cable, mobile broadband, public WiFi, work cable
  • → Not every solution-neutral function generates an equally rich alternative space — sometimes it collapses to “same basic approach, instrument varies.” A legitimate, useful outcome, not a failure — recognizing a narrow solution space is itself valuable architectural insight, telling the architect where creative effort will and won’t pay off

Broader Concepts and Hierarchy

  • The concept-option analysis looked “downward” toward increasing detail — but we can also look “upward” toward increasingly general intent
  • Why move fluid? Maybe to dry a basement. Why dry a basement? Maybe to improve living conditions
  • There is hierarchy in functional intent, just as there is hierarchy in built systems

The specific function at one level becomes the solution-neutral functional intent at the next level down.

  • Pivot — echoes the wine-bottle “upward vs. downward” move (Figures 7.1-7.2), now generalized to all four examples
  • Figures 7.5-7.9 explored downward. Upward questions: why move fluid? Maybe to dry a basement. Why dry a basement? Maybe to improve living conditions
  • → Just as built systems have hierarchy (Ch. 3), functional intent has hierarchy too — no reason the level you first stated is the “true” one, it’s just wherever the conversation started
  • Single most important insight, write on the board: the specific function at one level becomes the solution-neutral intent at the next level down — a precise, recursive relationship
  • → Means the Section 7.2/7.3 procedure chains downward through as many levels as useful, each level’s output concept becoming the next level’s input. Next two slides (7.10, 7.11) show this chaining worked out for both examples

Figure 7.10 · A Hierarchy Leading to Flying the Traveler

Closing a deal, by learning client preferences, by meeting the client, by traveling, by flying. At every level there are alternatives — a teleconference instead of a visit, a train instead of a flight. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.10.

  • Worked demonstration of the recursive relationship, top to bottom: “closing a deal” (ultimate reason) → “learning client preferences” → “meeting the client” → “traveling” → “flying” (matches Table 7.4)
  • Practically useful part (side-caption): at every level, real alternatives exist that a narrower fixed-level analysis would never surface
  • Instead of meeting to learn preferences: study published materials, attend a conference. Instead of traveling at all: a tele-meeting, skipping “traveling” entirely. Instead of flying: drive or train
  • → No single objectively “right” level to start at — but understanding intent’s hierarchy going up reveals entirely different solution categories (teleconference instead of any physical travel)

Entertainment → reading books → buying books → buying online → accessing the network. Understanding this hierarchy might surface new options, like selling electronic books. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.11.

  • → Selling ebooks doesn’t require buying (traditional sense) or the same home network connection — emerges only by stepping back up to “reading” and re-asking “what other ways could this need be met?”
  • Good closer for the whole first half of the chapter: stating things solution-neutrally and understanding intent’s full hierarchy isn’t pedantry — it’s a concrete tool for finding otherwise-invisible business/design opportunities

Section 7.3 Summary

  • Concept is a system vision that maps function to form; it sets the solution vocabulary and design parameters, and rationalizes the architecture
  • Concept is derived by specializing the solution-neutral operand and process to a specific operand, process, and abstraction of form
  • There is no naming convention, and no single relationship between solution-neutral and specific — concept generation requires creativity
  • Concepts sit in a hierarchy: understanding it one or two levels up is very useful for the architect
  • Checkpoint before Section 7.4 — students should be comfortable with:
    • The formal definition of concept and why it’s a useful intermediate step
    • Walking the Figure 7.4 template on a system of their choosing
    • No single fixed specialization pattern (Box 7.3) — requires creative judgment, not mechanical rules
    • Concepts form a hierarchy — looking one or two levels up can surface categorically different options (Figures 7.10, 7.11)
  • → Closes Section 7.3’s “what a concept is and how to derive one.” Next: what happens when a concept’s process is rich enough to unpack into several internal sub-processes?

7.4 Integrated Concepts

Rich Concepts and Concept Fragments

  • A concept’s process is often rich in meaning, and can be “unpacked” into several internal functions — visiting a client implies going there, spending time, and returning
  • An integrated concept is made of smaller concept fragments — one per internal process — combined recursively using the same concept-development procedure

Tip

Transporting has at least three important internal processes: lifting (overcoming gravity), propelling (overcoming drag), and guiding. Without all three, transporting doesn’t work.

  • Section 7.4’s central new idea — recall “visiting a client” was called “conceptually rich” (going, spending time, returning). That richness is what this section formalizes
  • A concept’s process can be unpacked into several distinct internal functions that must all happen for it to work
  • Define precisely: an integrated concept is built from smaller concept fragments, one per unpacked internal process — each developed with the same operand-process-instrument procedure, applied recursively (callback to Ch. 3’s recursion)
  • Primary worked example: “transporting” has at least three necessary internal processes — lifting (overcoming gravity), propelling (overcoming drag), guiding
  • → Emphasize “necessary”: without all three, transporting doesn’t work as a concept (can’t have a vehicle that overcomes gravity/drag with no way to steer). Sets up the morphological-matrix analysis next

The Morphological Matrix

  • For a car, lifting, propelling, and guiding are all done by wheels — a car is wheels-wheels-wheels
  • A train differs only in guiding — wheels-wheels-ground
  • Combining one instrument per internal process across many choices reveals a surprising number of integrated concepts — this structured layout is a morphological matrix (covered in full in Ch. 14)
  • “Wheels-wheels-wheels” — memorable shorthand for compressing a vehicle’s whole architecture into three words
  • Car: lifting, propelling, guiding all done by the wheels — lift (support weight), propel (friction + engine), guide (steering)
  • Train differs in exactly one process: lifting/propelling still wheels, but guiding = rail/ground itself (rails constrain the path, no active steering) → wheels-wheels-ground
  • → Striking: cars and trains feel like completely different vehicle categories but differ conceptually in exactly one instrument choice out of three
  • Morphological matrix: one row per internal process, one column per instrument choice per row — combine systematically to enumerate integrated concepts, not by ad hoc guessing
  • (Flag: full treatment comes in Ch. 14 — here, just the basic idea and payoff)

Table 7.6 · Morphological Matrix for Transporting

Each column is an integrated concept. Aircraft and gliders differ only in propulsion; airships and submarines are conceptually identical, differing only in operating medium. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Table 7.6.

  • Table structure: three rows (lifting, propelling, guiding), each with several instrument options (wheels, propeller, wings, hull for lifting; wheels, propeller, jet, gravity for propelling; wheels, propeller, rudder, “ground” for guiding). Eleven columns = eleven integrated vehicle concepts (car, train, jet aircraft, propeller aircraft, helicopter, airship, glider, boat, submarine, jet boat, hydrofoil), each an X-pattern across the rows
  • Two illuminating comparisons (side-caption):
    • Aircraft and gliders: same lifting (wings), same guiding (rudder), differ in exactly one row — propelling (propeller vs. gravity). A glider = “an aircraft with the propulsion instrument removed”
    • Airships and submarines: conceptually identical across all three processes, differing only in operating medium (air vs. water) — most people would never draw this comparison, but the underlying morphology matches
  • → Exactly the kind of unexpected structural insight a systematic morphological matrix surfaces that ad hoc brainstorming misses

Integrating the Home Data Network

  • The home network has five key internal processes: connecting the local network to the ISP; modulating the ISP carrier signal; managing data on the local network; connecting user devices; interacting with the user
  • Combining one instrument per process (with some flexibility — a residential gateway and a separate WAP can both manage the network) yields an integrated concept
  • Same methodology, second example, equal depth: “accessing” unpacks into five internal processes — have students explain each:
    • Connecting the local network to the ISP (the “last mile”)
    • Modulating the ISP’s carrier signal (a modem’s job)
    • Managing data on the local network (a gateway/router’s job)
    • Connecting user devices (WiFi, Ethernet)
    • Interacting with the user (laptop, phone, etc.)
  • → Useful nuance beyond car/train: real integrated concepts have more flexibility than exactly one instrument per process — a single device might combine rows (gateway doing modulation and management), or a function might split across devices (gateway and a separate WAP both managing data)
  • Flag honestly: morphological matrices are powerful but don’t always map with perfectly clean one-instrument-per-row simplicity — the architect uses judgment on how strictly to apply the framework

Figure 7.12 · An Integrated Concept for the Home Network

A DSL modem plus a box combining a residential gateway and Ethernet switch, with both WiFi and Ethernet connections to user devices. Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.12.

  • One concrete, fully-specified integrated concept — trace the signal path left to right
  • ISP side (broadband remote access server, DSLAM at the exchange) → phone line → DSL modem in the home → integrated box (residential gateway + Ethernet switch) → wired Ethernet to desktop PC and printer; separate WAP gives WiFi to laptop and cell phone
  • → This is “Integrated Concept 1” from the upcoming Table 7.8: DSL (connect to ISP), dedicated DSL modem (modulate), gateway+switch+WAP (manage data), WiFi+Ethernet (connect devices), laptop/phone/desktop/printer (user interaction)
  • Good template for students’ own diagrams — much more concrete/buildable than earlier purely functional OPM diagrams, because every box is a specific instrument, not a solution-neutral placeholder

Section 7.4 Summary

  • Rich concepts often unfold into a set of internal processes that must all be executed — choosing a specific operand, process, and instrument for each defines a concept fragment
  • The architect explores and recombines concept fragments to build desirable integrated concepts
  • The morphological matrix organizes this combinatorics — one column per integrated concept, one row per fragment choice
  • Checkpoint before the final section — students should be able to:
    • Identify a rich concept’s necessary internal processes (transporting → lifting/propelling/guiding; accessing → five home-network processes)
    • Define a concept fragment (specific operand-process-instrument choice for one internal process)
    • Explain how fragments recombine into an integrated concept (“wheels-wheels-wheels”)
    • Use a morphological matrix to compare alternatives systematically
  • → Concept tells us what form and function are — but not yet how the system behaves moment to moment once operating. That’s Section 7.5: concept of operations

7.5 Concepts of Operations and Services

Concept of Operations (“ConOps”)

  • Function is a somewhat static view of what a system can do; operation is the sequence of things leading to delivery of the primary function — what the system actually does (Ch. 6)
  • The concept of operations sketches out how the system will operate: who operates it, when, and coordinated with what else
  • The relationship between concept and architecture is the same as between concept of operations and a detailed operations sequence
  • Final major topic — recall Ch. 6’s distinction: function = static “what it can do,” operation = actual sequence of events delivering that function, unfolding in time
  • Function: the system can pressurize water. Operation: the actual step-by-step sequence, given operator, given conditions
  • ConOps (standard industry term, give it explicitly) = a sketch of how the system will operate: who operates it, when, coordinated with what else
  • → Elegant parallel: concept-of-operations : detailed operations sequence :: concept : full detailed architecture (Ch. 6/8) — coarse high-level sketch guiding (and later rationalized by) an elaborate detailed version

Service vs. Good

If the enterprise transfers the instrument, it is a good (an aircraft manufacturer sells airplanes). If it transfers the function, it is a service (an airline sells transportation).

From the aircraft’s own conops, the aircraft is the operand — it’s loaded, flown, maintained. From the perspective of the service, the aircraft is the instrument — it transports the traveler.

  • Useful business distinction ConOps helps clarify precisely: what gets transferred to the customer?
  • Transfer the instrument → good (Boeing sells the physical airplane). Transfer the function → service (an airline sells transportation, keeping the airplane)
  • Subtle, important perspective shift (take it slowly): from the aircraft’s own ConOps, the aircraft is the operand (loaded, flown, maintained). From the service’s perspective, that same aircraft becomes the instrument (transports the traveler, who’s now the operand)
  • → Same physical object, two entirely different roles depending on whose ConOps you’re examining — worked out visually side by side next slide

Figure 7.13 · Two ConOps, Side by Side

Left: the concept of operations of the aircraft itself (scheduling, loading, taxi/takeoff, climb/cruise, approach/landing, unloading, maintaining). Right: the concept of the service of air transportation, seen from the traveler’s side (purchasing, check-in, loading, transporting, unloading, check-out). Source: Crawley, Cameron & Selva (2016), Fig. 7.13.

  • Concrete payoff of the role-swap — walk both sides
  • Left (aircraft’s own ConOps): scheduling, flight planning, loading, taxi/takeoff → climb/cruise → approach/landing (all inside “transporting”), unloading, maintaining. Passengers/baggage appear only as things loaded/unloaded — essentially cargo from the aircraft’s own point of view
  • Right (the service, traveler’s side): plan trip + purchase ticket → check in (baggage) → is loaded (same event as left) → is transported (same process as left) → unloaded on arrival → check out
  • → Crucial modeling insight: taxi/takeoff/climb/landing detail matters hugely to the aircraft’s own ConOps, but is nearly irrelevant to the traveler’s service experience — same underlying process, two very different levels of relevant detail depending on whose ConOps you’re viewing

A Service Is a System

  • A service can be architected the same way as a system — it is simply more process-focused than a product
  • The concept of operations contains information vital to understanding the system architecture: it’s much easier to predict how a system delivers value once you know how it operates

Tip

Details of taxi, takeoff, and the like are relatively unimportant to the traveler — but very important to the aircraft’s own concept of operations. Same system, different conops, depending on perspective.

  • Section’s central conclusion: is a service the same kind of thing as a physical system (pump, algorithm, network)? Yes — a service can be architected with exactly the same tools; the only difference is emphasis, more process-focused than a product, but entities/form/function/emergence (Ch. 2-3) still apply fully
  • Why ConOps earns its own section: it contains information vital to understanding architecture that pure function statements don’t capture — knowing how a system operates makes it much easier to predict how it delivers value
  • → Restate the perspective-dependence lesson: same aircraft, hugely important details in its own ConOps, nearly irrelevant in the service’s ConOps built on top of it — neither view is more “correct,” it depends whose operational perspective you’re taking

Section 7.5 Summary

  • The concept of operations defines, at a conceptual level, how the system actually operates when it delivers value
  • Concepts of operations can be defined both for the system itself, and for any service built upon it
  • Brief checkpoint before the full chapter summary
  • ConOps = operational counterpart to concept — concept answers “what is this system, roughly,” ConOps answers “how does it run, in sequence”
  • Students should understand: the good/service distinction (transfer instrument vs. transfer function), and the perspective-dependent operand/instrument role-swap (aircraft example)

Chapter 7 Summary

  • This chapter transitioned from simple to more complex systems, and from purely analytical to the beginning of a synthetic approach to architecture
  • The synthetic process begins with the stakeholder’s need, expressed as a solution-neutral function — then specializes the operand, process, and instrument to define a concept
  • For almost any solution-neutral function there are many possible concepts; the architect creatively develops, sorts, and selects among them
  • Rich concepts unfold into concept fragments, recombined into an integrated concept — and a concept of operations describes how it all actually runs
