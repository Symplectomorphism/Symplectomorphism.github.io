Estimation

The model is continuous and deterministic; the data are daily, noisy and full of gaps. This page makes the model a discrete stochastic state-space system, then estimates it in two phases: phase 1 identifies the noise from weight history alone, and phase 2 runs a joint unscented Kalman filter over the state and nine parameters. It ends with what the data can identify, checks on synthetic data, and a forecast backtest. Särkkä (2013) covers the filtering theory in depth.

Discretization

Food logs report daily totals, so each day’s inputs are held constant over the day, which makes the one-day step an exact zero-order-hold discretization:

\[ x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k) = x_k + \int_0^1 g\bigl(x(s), u_k\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s , \]

with \(g\) the vector field of the assembled model. The integral uses fixed-step RK4 with four substeps per day; the fastest mode, the 1.5-day glycogen lag, is six substeps long. A test requires the RK4 map (step) to agree with an adaptive Tsit5 reference (simulate_diffrax; Tsitouras, 2011; Kidger, 2021) to \(10^{-5}\) (measured: \(2.5 \times 10^{-7}\)), so that a discretization error cannot pass for a physiological finding.

Timing: the morning state and the day’s food

Each row of the data file holds the weigh-in on the morning of day \(k\) and the food eaten during day \(k\), after it. The state \(x_k\) is the morning state, so day \(k\)’s food moves morning \(k\) to morning \(k+1\):

\[ x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k), \qquad y_k = H x_k + v_k . \]

Monday’s reading reflects food through Sunday; Monday’s dinner first shows on Tuesday’s scale. The filter shifts the inputs by a day (previous_day_inputs) so that step \(k\) propagates with day \(k-1\)’s food and then applies morning \(k\)’s reading, and step 0 is an update only (predict_first=False). Where a plan starts when today’s food is partly logged is a calendar question for the controller.

Process noise

Process noise stands for unlogged snacks, hydration swings, log error and changes in spontaneous activity. Standard deviations are per day, so a week without weigh-ins widens the band by \(\sqrt{7}\), not 7:

\[ Q_k = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_T^2, \sigma_F^2, \sigma_G^2, \sigma_N^2, \sigma_A^2) + \bigl[(1 - m_k)\,\sigma_I^2 + m_k\, s_k^2\bigr]\, b b^{\top}, \qquad b = \frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}}\,(1, p, 0, 0, 0)^{\top}, \]

where \(m_k \in \{0, 1\}\) indicates a food log, \(\sigma_I = 600\) kcal/day is the spread of plausible intake on an unlogged day, and \(s_k\) is a logged day’s own uncertainty: zero for a day logged as eaten, a quarter of its energy for a day marked as an estimate (wdm log --estimate), which then informs the energy balance less than a weighed day. The code’s diagonal defaults are \(\sigma_T = 0.08\), \(\sigma_F = 0.04\), \(\sigma_G = 0.15\), \(\sigma_N = 0.10\) kg and \(\sigma_A = 3.0\) kcal/day.

The inflation is rank one because an unknown intake moves tissue and fat together, in the ratio \(p\) and with the physical sensitivity \(1/\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}\); independent noise on \(T\) and \(F\) would let the filter learn about body composition from a day it knows nothing about.

Missing data and episodes

The loader reindexes each episode onto a complete daily calendar, so a missing day is a missing observation: with \(o_k \in \{0, 1\}\) indicating a weigh-in, the update is skipped when \(o_k = 0\) and the predicted belief carries forward. For data missing at random this is exact (Durbin and Koopman, 2012): gaps cost precision but add no bias, so never fill in a missed weigh-in (an invented observation makes the filter overconfident) and never delete an inconvenient one (it corrupts the noise estimate); mistyped values are left to the gate. Separate diet attempts (episodes), possibly years apart, share the subject’s metabolism but not their state, so parameters are shared and states episode-specific (pooling).

The filter

For a linear model \(x_{k+1} = F x_k + w_k\), \(y_k = H x_k + v_k\) with \(w \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q)\), \(v \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R)\), the Kalman filter (Kalman, 1960) predicts and updates in closed form:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat x_{k \mid k-1} &= F \hat x_{k-1 \mid k-1}, & P_{k \mid k-1} &= F P_{k-1 \mid k-1} F^{\top} + Q, \\ e_k &= y_k - H \hat x_{k \mid k-1}, & S_k &= H P_{k \mid k-1} H^{\top} + R, \\ K_k &= P_{k \mid k-1} H^{\top} S_k^{-1}, & \hat x_{k \mid k} &= \hat x_{k \mid k-1} + K_k e_k . \end{aligned} \]

The innovation \(e_k\) is the surprise in today’s reading and \(S_k\) its predicted variance. The covariance update uses the Joseph form, \(P_{k \mid k} = (\mathbb{I} - K_k H) P_{k \mid k-1} (\mathbb{I} - K_k H)^{\top} + K_k R K_k^{\top}\), a sum of positive semi-definite terms that round-off cannot drive negative. The same quantities give the log-likelihood that all parameter estimation maximizes:

\[ \log p(y_{1:n} \mid \theta) = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k:\,o_k = 1} \left[ \log 2\pi + \log S_k + \frac{e_k^2}{S_k} \right]. \]

Unscented time update. The dynamics are nonlinear (partitioning saturates, and parameters in the state make them bilinear), and linearization (the extended filter, kept only as a test reference) is first-order accurate. The unscented transform (Julier and Uhlmann, 2004; Wan and van der Merwe, 2000) instead pushes \(2n + 1\) deterministic points through the exact step map. For a belief \((\hat x, P)\) of dimension \(n\) with Cholesky factor \(L\) and \(\lambda = \alpha^2(n + \kappa) - n\),

\[ \mathcal{X}^{(0)} = \hat x, \quad \mathcal{X}^{(\pm i)} = \hat x \pm \sqrt{n + \lambda}\,L_{:,i}, \qquad W_m^{(0)} = \frac{\lambda}{n + \lambda}, \quad W_c^{(0)} = W_m^{(0)} + 1 - \alpha^2 + \beta, \quad W_m^{(\pm i)} = W_c^{(\pm i)} = \frac{1}{2(n + \lambda)} . \]

The points reproduce the mean and covariance exactly, and the predicted belief is the weighted mean and covariance of their images \(\mathcal{Y}^{(i)} = f(\mathcal{X}^{(i)}, u)\), plus \(Q_k\). The package uses \(\alpha = 0.3\), \(\beta = 2\), \(\kappa = 0\); the classic \(\alpha = 10^{-3}\) makes \(W_c^{(0)}\) large and negative in 14 or 16 dimensions and can break positive definiteness (van der Merwe, 2004).

TipThe scale update is exact

The scale reading is linear in the state and does not involve the parameters, so the linear update above applies exactly. The filter’s only approximations are the time update and, on days with a tape reading, the waist update.

Smoothing and numerics. A Rauch-Tung-Striebel pass (Rauch et al., 1965) runs backwards to give \(p(x_k \mid y_{1:n})\), with the sigma-point cross-covariance the time update already stores in place of \(P_{k \mid k} F^{\top}\). Figures show the smoothed history; the controller uses the filtered state at the last step, the only estimate that exists when deciding what to eat. The code runs in double precision, symmetrizes every covariance update and adds \(10^{-9}\) to the diagonal before each Cholesky factorization; tests check the linear filter and smoother against a dense batch posterior to \(10^{-8}\) and \(10^{-7}\).

Phase 1: noise from the weight record

Weight without a food log cannot identify metabolism, but it can say how noisy a reading is and how fast the trend wanders. Their ratio sets how hard to smooth: wrong one way and the estimate chases every salty dinner, wrong the other and it takes weeks to notice a real change. Phase 1 fits the local linear trend model (Harvey, 1989),

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \ell_{k+1} \\ b_{k+1} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \ell_k \\ b_k \end{pmatrix} + w_k, \qquad y_k = \ell_k + v_k, \]

with level \(\ell\), daily slope \(b\), \(Q = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\ell^2, \sigma_b^2)\) and \(R = \sigma_v^2\), by maximum likelihood over \((\sigma_\ell, \sigma_b, \sigma_v)\). Every episode with at least two weigh-ins contributes, logged or not; episodes share the noise scales, each with its own diffuse start. The search is L-BFGS-B (Byrd et al., 1995) in log coordinates with JAX gradients, and it refuses to run on fewer than 10 weigh-ins. Phase 2 takes \(\sigma_v\) from this fit.

Standard errors come from the observed information, reported as multiplicative factors, except that the code reports “unidentified” when the optimum sits on a bound, the Hessian’s condition number exceeds \(10^{10}\), or a variance comes out non-positive. The first case is common: maximum likelihood often drives \(\sigma_\ell \to 0\), a fit in which the trend moves through changes of slope. For the same reason the fit reports the steady-state gain on the level (the fraction of each surprise the filter acts on, from iterating the covariance recursion to a fixed point) rather than \(\sigma_\ell/\sigma_v\). Its reciprocal is the effective averaging window in days. The trend \(\ell\) with its slope \(b\) is, on its own, the package’s most useful output.

Phase 2: joint state and parameter estimation

Phase 2 appends the parameters to the state and filters everything together:

\[ z_k = \bigl(\underbrace{T, F, G_c, G_n, A}_{x_k},\; \underbrace{\eta}_{\in \mathbb{R}^9},\; \underbrace{\eta_w}_{\in \mathbb{R}^2}\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^{16}. \]

The waist block \(\eta_w\) exists only when some episode has a waist reading (\(\mathbb{R}^{14}\) otherwise), because the sigma-point spread depends on the dimension and idle entries would perturb every other estimate. \(\eta\) and \(\eta_w\) follow a slow random walk, \(\eta_w\) never enters the dynamics, and each weigh-in updates all of \(z_k\) through the cross-covariances the time update builds.

Reparameterization

Time constants and gains are positive and \(\beta_A \in [0, 1)\), but a Gaussian sigma point can land anywhere, and a negative time constant makes the dynamics explode. The filter therefore works in unconstrained coordinates and maps to natural units only inside the dynamics:

Kind Map Used for
log \(\theta = c\,e^{s\eta}\) \(c_0\), \(c_1\), the time constants and gains; the waist slope \(b_w\) (\(s = 0.2\))
logit \(\theta = \sigma(\operatorname{logit} c + s\eta)\) \(\beta_A\)
affine \(\theta = c + s\eta\) \(\kappa_P\); the waist offset \(a_w\) (\(s = 7.2\) cm for men)

Centers and scales put the prior at \(\eta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbb{I})\) exactly (parameter table, waist priors).

Slow drift. A random walk of \(\sigma_\eta = 0.02\) per day, in prior-sigma units, keeps the filter from locking in early. Without it the covariance only shrinks and the filter goes deaf to later evidence, including evidence that its early estimate (from the least reliable food logs) was wrong. The fiction is partly true, since maintenance does drift with season, activity and age. By design, each posterior then settles where information gain and drift balance; on synthetic data the 90% width of \(c_0\) plateaus at about 0.9 times the prior width from day 30 to day 180.

Which episodes are filtered

Phase 2 filters episodes with a food log, plus the latest episode, logged or not, because planning starts from its state. Past weight-only diets feed phase 1 only. On an unlogged day, intake is modeled as unknown around expenditure, so a past diet that was in fact losing weight looks like a long run of same-signed surprises, which the filter can explain only by bending parameters; on the report’s record it pulled the glycogen time constant far from its prior. A test checks that adding a weight-only past episode leaves the metabolic posterior unchanged.

Pooling across episodes

Each episode starts from its first weigh-in: \(T_0\) is that reading, \(F_0\) the configured body-fat fraction times it or else the Deurenberg et al. (1991) prediction from BMI, age and sex (standard error about 4 percentage points), and \(G_c = G_n = A = 0\), with initial standard deviations of 4 kg on fat and 5 kcal/day on adaptation.

The parameter posterior carries forward as the next episode’s prior (sequential Bayes), with its covariance relaxed toward the prior across the gap \(\Delta t\) (days):

\[ P_\eta \leftarrow (1 - w) P_\eta + w P_\eta^{\mathrm{prior}}, \qquad w = 1 - e^{-\Delta t / \tau_{\mathrm{forget}}}, \qquad \tau_{\mathrm{forget}} = 730\ \text{days}. \]

The mean is left alone: elapsed time makes you less certain about your metabolism, not wrong about it. The waist block is carried and relaxed the same way. Each filtered episode is then smoothed, and the final parameter summaries come from 20,000 Monte Carlo draws of the unconstrained posterior mapped to natural units, since the exponential and logistic maps make the posteriors skewed. The reported “shift” is the relative move of each posterior median from its prior center; near zero means the data said nothing (Reading the output).

Noise model: Gaussian with a 4-sigma gate

A weight mistyped as 231 lb instead of 213 lb would otherwise count as a real 18 lb gain, and the filter would revise your metabolism to match. The gate inflates the observation variance by the square of any exceedance beyond \(n_\sigma = 4\) predicted standard deviations:

\[ R_k^{\mathrm{eff}} = R \cdot \max\!\left(1, \frac{\lvert e_k \rvert}{n_\sigma \sqrt{S_k}}\right)^{2}. \]

It downweights rather than discards, so a sustained shift still moves the estimate once its first readings have pulled the prediction along, and an honest Gaussian reading is almost never touched (probability about \(6 \times 10^{-5}\)). It guards the waist update too.

Student-\(t\), as an option. Treating the noise as a Gaussian with a random precision \(\omega \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\nu/2, \text{rate } \nu/2)\) gives, for a standardized innovation \(z_k = e_k/\sqrt{S_k}\), the one-step approximation

\[ R_k^{\mathrm{eff}} = R\,\frac{\nu + z_k^2}{\nu + 1}, \]

followed by an ordinary update, with a Student-\(t\) likelihood term. At \(\nu = 4\) (Lange et al., 1989) a ten-sigma reading keeps about a twentieth of its weight (\(5/104\)). It replaces the gate and is available through wdm backtest --student-t 4 (Commands).

Why the Gaussian was kept. Refitting phase 1 on the real record with heavier tails (\(\nu = \infty, 30, 10, 6, 4, 3\)), the report finds:

  • the likelihood falls monotonically as the tails get heavier;
  • the Gaussian’s residuals look Gaussian, with kurtosis near 3 and no standardized residual beyond 3;
  • heavier tails move variance from the readings into the trend and raise the steady-state gain, so the filter chases readings, the opposite of smoothing.

The backtest log score agrees, on too few forecasts to decide anything, and the comparison should be rerun as the record grows. Under Student-\(t\), \(\sigma_v\) is a scale (the sd is \(\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_v\) at \(\nu = 4\)), so the same \(\nu\) must be used in both phases.

The waist update

On a day with a tape reading, the exact scale update comes first and the tape second. The tape prediction \(a_w + b_w F\) multiplies two uncertain entries, so its update is unscented: fresh sigma points from the post-scale belief \((\hat z^{+}, P^{+})\) give predicted readings \(\mathcal{Z}^{(i)}\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat y^w_k &= \textstyle\sum_i W_m^{(i)} \mathcal{Z}^{(i)}, & S^w_k &= \max\Bigl(0, \textstyle\sum_i W_c^{(i)} (\mathcal{Z}^{(i)} - \hat y^w_k)^2\Bigr) + \sigma_w^2, \\ K^w_k &= \textstyle\sum_i W_c^{(i)} (\mathcal{X}^{(i)} - \hat z^{+})(\mathcal{Z}^{(i)} - \hat y^w_k) / S^w_k, & \hat z_{k \mid k} &= \hat z^{+} + K^w_k (y^w_k - \hat y^w_k), \\ P_{k \mid k} &= P^{+} - S^w_k K^w_k K^{w\top}_k . \end{aligned} \]

The floor at zero guards against a negative central weight. The innovation adds a likelihood term under the same gate. Without tape readings the filter is unchanged.

What the tape identifies. Between two readings, \(\Delta y^w \approx b_w\, p\, \Delta W\), so the data constrain only the product \(b_w p\): a 20% error in the fat share can hide behind a 20% error in the slope. What the tape adds is a second, independent anchor for the fat level, \(F = (y^w - a_w)/b_w\) with \(a_w\) known to 7.2 cm, alongside the Deurenberg starting value.

Identifiability

Read this before quoting any parameter. Synthetic figures are from the run below.

Status Quantity Why
Well identified Maintenance \(c_0 + c_1 W\) at a given weight Over the narrow mass range of one dataset, raising \(c_0\) and lowering \(c_1\) barely changes the fit, so each coefficient is wide while their combination is well determined.
Well identified \(k_G\), \(\tau_G\) Carbohydrate varies daily and the water swings are large next to the noise. On synthetic data \(k_G\) moved 33% off its prior.
Weak \(\beta_A\), \(\tau_A\) Separable from a mis-estimated \(c_0\) only through the lag; constant intake gives almost no information. A diet break or refeed week helps.
Weak Fat mass \(F\) The scale sees it only through \(\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}\), which a 15 kg loss changes by under 10%. Always shown with a credible band.
Not identified \(\kappa_P\) Second-order effect through \(\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}\). On synthetic data it moved less than 1%, with a 90% interval spanning zero.
Not identified Thermic-effect coefficients Held fixed: their effect looks like a proportional logging bias, which \(c_0\) already absorbs.
NoteQuote maintenance, not its coefficients

Quoting \(c_0\) or \(c_1\) alone overstates the uncertainty in what you can eat; treating them as independent understates it. maintenance_interval_kcal carries the full covariance through the combination, and a test asserts it is more than 20% narrower than the naive independent interval.

Validation on synthetic data

wdm demo generates two weight-only past episodes of 40 days and a current episode of 120 days with macronutrients (89% weigh-in and 88% logging coverage), from true parameters deliberately offset from the priors (if the truth sat at the prior center, an estimator that ignored the data would pass). All nine true values lie inside their 90% posterior intervals; for example, \(k_G\) (truth 0.0042) has median 0.00398, and \(\kappa_P\) (truth 0.06) has median 0.0497 with an interval from -0.0325 to 0.135. Maintenance at 100 kg is estimated at 2923 kcal/day [2713, 3161] against a true 2843 (2.8% error), and the smoothed trend’s error against the hidden truth is 205 g RMS, against 488 g of single-weigh-in noise. This validates the inference machinery and says nothing about whether the model resembles a person: the data come from the dynamics the estimator assumes.

Backtest: forecasting readings not yet seen

wdm backtest replays each phase-2 episode day by day. For every origin day \(j\) and horizon \(h\) (default 1 and 7 days) whose target morning has a weigh-in, it takes the filtered belief on morning \(j\), applies \(h\) unscented time updates driven by the food logged on days \(j\) to \(j + h - 1\), and scores the predictive distribution \(\mathcal{N}(H\hat z, H P H^{\top} + R)\) against the reading. Using the logged food separates model error from adherence.

Scores: bias and RMSE (lb); skill, the fractional RMSE improvement over persistence (tomorrow equals the last reading), which a model must beat at a week to earn its complexity; the standardized-error sd and 50% and 90% coverage, which a calibrated forecast puts near 1, 50% and 90%; and the mean log predictive density (higher is better), the fair score for comparing noise models.

On its own synthetic data the model’s intervals are honest to slightly wide, and it beats persistence by 21% at a day and 40% at a week. A test asserts the one-day calibration, the one-week skill, and that the one-day forecast equals the filter’s own prediction. On real data the first mornings of a diet drop more than the water compartments explain, most likely gut contents, which no state represents.

Caveats. Phase-1 noise is fitted on the whole record, so coverage is slightly optimistic. Forecasts from neighboring origins overlap (at \(h = 7\), consecutive forecasts share six days of inputs), so the effective sample is several times smaller than \(n\).
