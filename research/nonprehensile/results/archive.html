The runs before the friction band

WarningThe runs below predate the current configuration

Every run below was measured with the simulator’s disk–box friction at 0.99 and the gate and tracker at 0.35 (the gate at the simulator’s 0.99 before the evening of Nov 9), not the declared band μdisk ∈ [0.3, 0.5] with the gate and tracker at 0.3; with release edits on except in the quasi-static lift (the current search has releases off: their rules were hand rules, and a principled release is a proposal); with Λqs before its current default options; and without the workspace and dynamic-event rules of the paper’s search, unless a section says so. Most lifts below let the box drop onto the platform; the current target is the indirect lift, held at every instant. These results are the record of those configurations, not the current planner’s.

The runs are the night of November 9–10, when the search did the whole task end to end; November 9, on elliptic friction contacts; and, in the last section, a summary of the November 8–9 runs on MuJoCo’s pyramidal contacts, before the redesign.

Each clip below replays calls that the planner actually made in its teacher runs (branch phase1/planar). No motion here was staged for the video. The search chose the edits (the contact changes), and each edit was executed by the local solver the run used: the quasi-static realizer Λ_qs or a learned skill Λ_RL (see The local solver). Each call was re-run in MJX from the logged start state, recorded, and drawn. The re-run was checked against the log: the same success, the same end formation, and the end pose within a few millimetres. One clip that did not match is labelled DIVERGED below.

Night of November 9–10: end to end

WarningThese runs used a temporary ±20 cm push range

The end-to-end, lower-friction and quasi-static runs in this section ran with the transport’s push range widened from the stock ±5 cm to ±20 cm, a temporary setting; it is not a direction, and the parametric certificate (below) is to replace it. A single push could then move the box up to 200 mm. This was not stated here before (it was set outside the recorded run options). The teacher runs, the learned-ranker comparison and the November 9 results below use the stock range. Without the wider range the end-to-end task fails: see the push range decides the task.

Overnight the search did the whole task: lift the box onto the platform and push it to a set place there. The task (lift_to) asks for the box’s centre at x = 0.25 ± 0.01 m on the platform, at rest. On 16 instances the redesign solves 13 (Wilson 95%: 57–93%), and 15 with the last push aimed at the window’s centre (described below; the default since November 10), both with the ±20 cm push range. Every solve takes the same route:

push to the riser → make s → pivot → release onto {t} → disk to the box’s side face → push → push to the window.

lift_to, instance 0: SOLVED in 180 calls, 7 on the path, every call a success; the box ends at x = 0.243 m. The replay reproduces all 7 calls to 0.0 mm.

lift_to, instance 2: SOLVED in 196 calls. One call on the path (a release the realizer did not finish) still pushed the box 108 mm along the platform; the search kept that outcome and finished with a push to x = 0.240 m.

What made the platform pushes work

All evening, every push along the platform was refused before the disk moved: “the target is not in the disk’s run along the contacted feature”. The cause was the disk’s placement, not the mechanics. The relaxed gate put the disk 0.8 mm past the end of the stretch of face the controller recognises. The fix (decided by Aykut): for a push, the disk goes to the most robust of 5 placements inside that stretch, as it already did for contact changes. In the replays, two pushes that had moved the box 0 mm then moved it 48.6 mm and 47.2 mm.

Where the misses stop

The solved instances end at x = 0.240–0.245 m, the near edge of the window. A push toward the goal aims at the nearest of the goal poses, which are spread across the window, so it aims at the edge, x = 0.24, and the realizer stops a few millimetres short of its aim. Once the box is within 10 mm of a goal pose no further push counts as progress, so one instance stalled at x = 0.2374, just outside. Aiming the last push at the window’s centre (the default since Nov 10) solves that instance (ends at x = 0.247) and keeps the others solved. Paired on all 16 instances, the flag solves 15 against 13, loses none, and never needs more calls (median 148 against 180). The one instance it misses fails for another reason (its long pushes came too late in the budget).

The push range decides the task

At the stock range (±5 cm per push) the same task fails: 0 of 3 instances on November 9 and 0 of 7 on the morning of November 10 (the second run with the push feasibility check on), 0 of 10 in all. Each 200 mm slide along the platform becomes four or five 50 mm pushes, each needing its own approach, and the search runs out of its 256 calls with the box about 5 cm short of the window. The ±20 cm range works because it checks a 200 mm push as one call. Doing that properly is the job of the next build, a parametric push certificate: certify that the disk can push the box along the whole path, as a function of the position along it, instead of on a fixed 5 cm grid. These runs are the evidence for why it comes next.

A quasi-static lift, and a caveat

With releases switched off, the search must keep the box held at every instant (these runs also used the ±20 cm push range). On the evening’s code it solved lift instance 2 that way in 52 calls, 5 on the path: the pivot, a held tilt-up from 3° to 73°, and a push over the step’s corner that leaves the box upright on the platform. The replay reproduces all 5 calls to 0.0 mm.

Rerun overnight with the robust disk placement, instance 1 ran out of budget (184 calls), and instance 0 was solved through the pivot and two held tilt-up pushes (5° → 63°), with the box finally resting on the step’s corner. But its last call, a transfer that the realizer did not complete, ended with the box toppling onto the platform and being dragged about 0.8 m by the disk, which was flung off. That end is at rest and on the platform, so the search accepted it. It was a solve by the rules then, not a quasi-static one. Decided Nov 10: such outcomes no longer count. An end with the disk outside its workspace is invalid in every run, and quasi-static runs reject ends reached through a dynamic event (a topple). Under these rules this instance 0 “solve” is rejected; the video stays as the example of what the rule removes.

Paired on the same starts (exact McNemar): 0.35 against 0.1, 15 instances are solved only at 0.35 and 1 only at 0.1 (p ≈ 0.0005); 0.35 against 0.2, 4 against 1 (p ≈ 0.38). At μ = 0.1 the failures are not budget: the search runs out of moves, most of them at the pivot, where the release onto the platform needs more friction than there is.

Where to put the disk on a face

The 5-placement rule is a stopgap. For a disk on a box face, the moment couples to the disk’s position s only through s·F_n, and with τ = s·F_n (exact, since F_n ≥ 0) the set of positions where the box is held is an interval that two linear programs find. The margin is quasi-concave in s, so the best placement is found exactly. This rule is in the code (off by default; the certified placement on Certifying a whole box is its interval-certified version). Two findings came out of building it:

  • When the box needs the disk to stand, the realizer keeps the disk where it already is on the box, so no placement rule, sampled or exact, is carried out there (errors up to 34 mm).
  • The same lifting applied to pushing (support friction at its limit, no tipping, the disk sticking) gives the interval of positions from which a push can move the box. On 63 logged pushes it predicts failure with precision 0.88 and recall 0.79, and it flags pushes the holding test offers but that cannot move the box at all (a disk on the top face trying to drag it 182 mm). Since November 10 this check is on by default (Aykut’s decision); the paired run planned for it did not run.

The literature (from sampling and scoring, as in Posa’s group, to exact contact regions for grasps, to convex relaxations) is collected in the parametric certification review.

November 9: elliptic contacts, the redesign, and CMGMP

Four things happened on November 9. The simulator’s friction model turned out to be wrong for a box at rest, and was changed. The CMGMP baseline was re-run on the new contacts. The lift’s pivot was fixed. And the redesigned search (Search) ran on 40 lifting and 40 lowering instances: it lifts 37 of 40 and lowers 40 of 40, and, paired with CMGMP on the same starts, lifts more within 30 minutes while CMGMP is faster at short budgets (results below).

The simulator, not the gate, was wrong

MuJoCo’s default pyramidal friction cones let a box creep off a rest that the statics say is held. Elliptic cones (impedance ratio 10), at the same step and contact parameters, remove the creep. On the box leaning on the step corner with the disk pulled away:

pyramidal (old default) elliptic, impratio 10 (adopted)
creep, °/s 0.40 0.008
rests held for 10 s 0 of 84 84 of 84
simulator agrees with the gate (poses away from the tipping boundary) 87 of 453 452 of 453

So the gate’s statics were right; the simulator let held boxes slide. Every scene now uses elliptic cones and full float32 matrix products. The redesigned search and CMGMP are run on them; the earlier pipeline is superseded by the redesign and is not re-run (Aykut’s decision, Nov 9). The results further down this page, below the November 9 section, used the pyramidal cones.

How CMGMP lifts

Every CMGMP lift we rendered takes the same route, read by our formation recognizer:

{g} → {m_x, g} → {m_x, s, g} → {m_x, g_v, s_v} → {m_x, s_v} → {m_x, t}

The disk pushes the box against the riser and tips it into our pivot formation {m_x, g_v, s_v}. The floor corner lets go and the box hangs on the step corner, {m_x, s_v}. The disk keeps pushing and rolls the box a quarter turn onto its +x face on the platform, {m_x, t}, with the disk still on the face it pushed. Our planned route shares the pivot, then moves the disk round the box’s corner onto its top instead.

The redesigned search: lift 37 of 40, lower 40 of 40

The redesigned search ran tonight on 40 random lifting and 40 random lowering instances, on elliptic contacts, with everything as decided: the gate at the controller’s friction, the most robust disk placement (5 candidates), box transport and release edits, a search ordered by the value estimate V₀ (η = 1 + h/g), no learned ranker (every admissible edit equally likely), and no retries. A call budget caps the search: the number of times the local solver Λ may be run.

Task (40 instances) budget solved 95% CI Λ calls per solve, median (range) wall time per solve, median
lower 64 40 91–100% 10 (6–13) 396 s
lift 192 37 80–97% 88 (52–148) 572 s
lift 64 6 7–29% 52 (52–64) 390 s

On every solve the per-run check of the call bound (the paper’s Theorem C) holds. The three unsolved lifts at budget 192 ran out of things to try (the search tree was exhausted), not out of budget.

How the search lifts. Every one of the 37 solutions has the same core:

{m_x, g, s} ⇒ {m_x, g_v, s_v}   pivot: the box tips onto its floor corner against the step corner
{m_x, s_v, g_v} → {t}            release: the box tips onto its +x face on the platform
{t} → {m_z, t}                   make: the disk goes to the box's −z face
{m_z, t} → box on its +z face    release: the box rolls once more and rests fully on the platform

Before the core, most solutions first push the box flush against the riser. The search found a way that lets the box go twice; it did not find the held route, the indirect lift, which is now the target (with releases off, these solutions are no longer available to the search). CMGMP’s lifts also roll the box onto its +x face; ours adds a second roll. Transport was tried 27 times and is in no solution.

Redesign, lift instance 2: SOLVED (52 calls in the search, 4 on the solution; 19 s). Pivot, release onto the platform, make on −z, release. The replay reproduces all 4 logged calls exactly (0.0 mm).

Redesign, lift instance 16: SOLVED (88 calls, 7 on the solution; 31 s). The disk first pushes the box to the riser (make, release, make), then the same four-edit core.

Why budget 64 lifts only 6. The run at 64 calls makes exactly the same calls as the run at 192, just cut off: 33 of its 34 unsolved instances had already reached {t}, one make and one release from the finish. Without a learned ranker every edit is equally likely, so a node’s priority falls by a factor of about 7 per level, and the value estimate shifts it by at most about 2.5. The search therefore tries every shallower branch before the platform node, four edits deep. That is a ranking problem, which is what the learned ranker is for.

How the search lowers. Every solution is one release off the platform, half of them after making the disk’s contact on the box’s +x face. No transports. The release still throws the box (known issue, below).

A first learned ranker

A value network V₁ and a ranker π were trained on tonight’s lowering run and the 64-call lifting run (the solutions relabeled in hindsight). On 11 held-out instances V₁ predicts the remaining Λ calls with a mean error of 0.19 calls (the graph’s hop count: 0.63; V₀: 0.77), and π puts its top choice on the solution’s edit 97% of the time (uniform: 24%).

Used in the search (95% learned, 5% uniform, so no edit is ever ruled out), on 3 lifting instances from a seed the training never saw, at budget 64: the learned ranker solves 3 of 3 (in 44, 53 and 43 calls), the uniform ranker none. Three instances are a small sample; it shows the mechanism, not a rate. V₁ itself does not yet order the search. (Update, Nov 10: on 40 unseen instances, paired with uniform, 40 against 3; see above.)

Learned ranker, unseen lift instance (seed 1, instance 2): SOLVED in 43 calls (26 s). The uniform ranker does not solve it within 64. The replay reproduces the log exactly.

The redesign’s first lowering solutions, with a known issue

In the redesign’s smoke test (three instances, a check of the code, before the full run above) the search solved both lowering instances in 13 calls each. Each solution is two calls: make the disk’s contact on the box’s +x face, then a release: the disk pushes the box off the platform and lets it fall to the floor. WarningKnown issue: the release throws the box

The release’s push does not set the box down; it throws it. In instance 14 the box leaves at 1.46 m/s and lands 0.54 m past the predicted landing; in instance 3, 0.19 m past. Both still end at rest inside the goal, so both count. Releases also end in the same pose whatever formation they were asked for. This was a property of the release’s scripted push. Releases are now off, and the release path as built is superseded by the principled-release proposal (R1–R3).

Tonight’s work: the lift’s pivot

On elliptic contacts the lift stalled at its first hard step, the pivot {m_x, g, s} ⇒ {m_x, g_v, s_v} (box flat against the riser tipping onto its floor corner). The cause was a friction mismatch. The gate assumed the simulator’s disk–box friction, 0.99, and placed the disk 45 mm below the middle of the box’s face. The controller only uses 0.35, and from there it could not tip the box: 0.01° over 680 samples. On pyramidal cones the same pivot had passed only because the disk slipped up the face.

Two fixes, both decided by Aykut tonight:

  1. The gate uses the controller’s friction, 0.35, at the disk. The hard break that follows the pivot stays legal. (Superseded on Nov 11 by the friction band: the gate and the tracker use 0.3.)
  2. The disk goes to the most robust placement. The gate is re-checked exactly at 5 placements along the face, and the one with the largest margin wins. This is an interim rule: in the long run a learned pivot skill chooses its own placement (a first one is trained; compared below).

With both, lift instance 2’s pivot passes into exactly {m_x, g_v, s_v}, with the disk 44 mm above the face’s middle, and the search went on to a release onto the platform. A side finding: the exact gate passes at every disk height on that face, but the controller succeeds only from about 10 mm below the middle upward, so low on the face the gate is no test of what the controller can do. Checking 5 placements costs about 5.6× the gate at a node where the disk touches the box.

The full run above uses this fix: every lift solution passes the pivot. On 353 held-out pivot states the quasi-static realizer with the robust placement passes 348, a learned pivot skill 337, and the two together all 353.

Since then: Aykut’s quasi-static route test has run (releases off; see the night of November 9–10). V₁ and the ranker were retrained on the full lift goal on Nov 10 (V₁’s error on lift-goal pairs 1.03 → 0.33 calls; the ranker’s top-1 on lift steps 0.93 → 0.98); V₁ enters the order behind a flag. The paired rerun of these networks was dropped: they were trained on solves that are mostly releases and falls, and are to be retrained on held solves.

Earlier runs on pyramidal contacts (superseded)

Before the redesign and before elliptic contacts (Nov 8–9: simulator disk friction 0.99, the gate at the simulator’s friction, MuJoCo’s pyramidal cones, under which held boxes creep), the teacher search solved the sub-goals almost always (119 of 120), lifted only with two learned skills (Λqs tips the box onto the step corner, a learned break skill flips it onto its side against the corner, a learned roll skill sets it down: 5 of 10, 7 of 10 with a tie rule for the success test, since shelved), and lowered none (0 of 40: the only edit off the platform, a knife-edge transfer onto the step corner, failed in 52 of 52 calls). Its lift goal accepted a box straddling the step edge. These runs are not continued.
