The local solver

Realizing an edit: one success test for every realizer, the mode-scheduled quasi-static realizer, re-planning and failure diagnostics, and the MPPI baseline.

NoteResult (steps 3b–3c): the quasi-static realizer follows the chain; one class of breaks remains

The mode-scheduled realizer Λqs realizes 23 of 24 chain edits from canonical and perturbed starts (GPU), including the pivot that no MPPI cost realized; MPPI-E realizes 15. Receding-horizon re-planning does not raise success (22–23 of 24) but halves the tracking deviation at a 0.2 s interval. Every remaining failure is a break(gv) whose quasi-static hold needs more disk friction than the tracker uses: the gate and the plan count on μ = 0.99 at the disk, the tracker on 0.35. Choices for Aykut: Issues.

What the solver must do

The local solver Λ gets an edit σ → σ′ that passed the hold gate, the current pose χ₀, and the transition pose χtr the gate found. It returns a simulated record, and the edit is accepted only if the record passes one success test, shared by every realizer:

  • persistence: every kept contact is on at every sample; for a transfer c → d, one of c and d is on at every sample;
  • departure: a contact dropped outright ends above εon;
  • dwell: the recognized formation equals σ′ for the last samples, with no tie;
  • overlap: no gap below −εpen;
  • the manipulator’s time limit, support while the disk lets go, and progress toward the goal for transport.

The mode-scheduled realizer Λqs (step 3b)

The edit names the contacts before and after, and the gate’s sweep gives χtr, so Λqs plans on known contacts and tracks the plan in MuJoCo:

  • plan: knots on the contact constraints (Gauss–Newton projection of an interpolation); phase I from χ₀ to χtr keeping σ; phase II by edit kind: a relaxing edit ends at the nearest pose recognized exactly as σ′ with σ′ holding; a constraining edit ends at χtr projected onto σ′ and pressed 2 mm past the closure; transport moves up to 0.05 toward the goal;
  • feed-forward: a quasi-static force at each knot (nonnegative least squares over the gate’s friction-cone rays);
  • tracking: while the disk touches, a PD force along the box’s free directions, mapped through the contact and kept inside the disk’s friction cone (μ = 0.35, below the simulator’s 0.99, so the contact sticks) and the ±1 N motor box; otherwise PD on the planned disk positions.

A learned skill for the hard breaks

The hard break(gv) starts fail for every planner so far because the box’s corners slide: the maneuver needs timing or momentum that a quasi-static plan can’t provide. So a controller for this one maneuver is being learned, in the same MJX simulator the rest of the system uses:

  • Two algorithms, two variants: proximal policy optimization (PPO) and soft actor-critic (SAC), from Brax, each trained as a residual correction on top of Λqs’s commands and from scratch.
  • Training: randomized starts (tilt 30–45°, disk height, friction, mass) with a curriculum from easy to hard tilts; the reward is reaching the target formation under the strict success test, with penalties for slipping, falling and deep penetration. On Juno’s H200 GPUs, several seeds each.
  • Validation: the six hard starts and 50+ held-out ones, against Λqs and MPPI (both 0 of 6 so far), with a penetration limit and a re-check with stiffer contacts, so a policy can’t win by exploiting the simulator.
  • Feasibility: if some start stays unsolved by every run, a brute-force search over open-loop force sequences within the motors’ ±1 N tells whether it is hard to learn or impossible.

A learned skill would carry a measured success rate, not a quasi-static certificate; the call bound still holds (it counts Λ calls).

Result: SAC trained from scratch solves the whole family. Under the strict success test (settle clause off; a re-evaluation with it on is in progress), on the same starts for every method:

Method Hard starts Held-out starts (95% CI) Penetration violations
SAC, from scratch (3 seeds) 18 / 18 150 / 150 (98–100%) 0
SAC, residual on Λqs 6 / 18 50 / 150 (26–41%) 75

SAC gives the same result with stiffer contacts, so it isn’t exploiting soft contacts; every hard start was solved, so the feasibility search wasn’t needed. Learning from scratch beats the residual because Λqs’s commands are a poor starting point here (they fail 79 of 80 grid starts). PPO learned nothing under the same tuning budget. Two caveats: SAC is unstable (two of three seeds collapse after peaking, so the best checkpoint is kept), and the policy tips the box to about 72° in 0.25 s and then holds the new contact state in a small limit cycle (about ±2° at 4 Hz), which the strict test accepts because its settle clause is off; the settle-on numbers will decide that clause’s default. The skill exists as ΛRL (cmg/rl/skill.py): the search will call it for the hard break whenever the start is within its training range, and Λqs otherwise (being wired in). It carries an empirical success rate with a confidence interval, outside the gate’s quasi-static certificate; the call bound treats it as a solver that succeeds at random.

Overnight Nov 8–9: from the break to a solved lift.

  • Settle clause (decision pending). With the clause on at 10⁻³ the policy fails; SAC retrained with a settling term passes 4/18 and 32/150 (10/18 and 93/150 with stiffer contacts). But Λqs’s own chain edits also pass only 2 of 7 at that tolerance (6 of 7 with it off), so turning it on would redefine success for the whole system.

  • PPO with more budget: a further 16 configurations at 100M steps all fail; the box slides back or tips onto the platform.

  • The skill’s range, from the search. The teacher search reaches the break at about 10° tilt with the disk about 10 mm above the box’s centre line, far from the training range, so the skill was never used. SAC retrained over tilt 5–45° and disk heights −40 to +20 mm solves all 84 break states the search actually reached in every seed (Λqs: 0/84). That is now the deployed skill. Widening to 70° didn’t help.

  • The next step, the roll onto the platform. After the break the box stands at about 83° on the step corner, and Λqs’s roll onto the platform fails (0/16: the box falls back to about 86° before the success window). A second learned skill for that roll (off by default):

Re-planning and failure diagnostics (step 3c)

Re-planning (RHk). Every k steps (20, 40, 80: every 0.2, 0.4, 0.8 s) the plan is rebuilt from the current simulated state and its first segment tracked. The phase comes from the recognized formation: still σ, re-plan phase I toward the same χtr; already σ′, a path to the previous end pose; in between, or past χtr on the schedule but not yet σ′, the previous plan continues. Each phase keeps its scheduled end. The record stops early if a contact that must persist is lost. The first version gave every new plan the time that was left and restarted the change at each re-plan, and was worse than open loop (issue); the table is after the fix.

Ablations. F1: a release must lift the dropped contact at least 4 mm (phase II goes farther past χtr). F2: the feed-forward ramps in from the start’s equilibrium force instead of from zero. RH40r re-sweeps χtr at each re-plan.

Diagnostics for every run (time series kept for failed ones): planned vs executed box and disk, the deviation; recognized vs planned formation and the first unexpected loss or gain; the edit’s gaps; MuJoCo’s contact forces (replayed), friction-cone ratios and slip; motor commands against the limits; the box’s kinetic energy; the re-plan log; a one-line diagnosis and a failure mode.

Why the remaining runs fail

All are break(gv): the hard set and one or two perturbed chain starts.

  • Slump during ramp-in (disk 40 mm low, every quasi-static variant). The riser contact sv is lost within 9–21 steps; from 30° the box falls flat in 0.2 s. The motors are not saturated then and the disk barely slides. At that start the plan asks the disk for (0.33, 0.09) N, a tangential/normal ratio of 0.99 at the disk–box contact, which the simulator’s μ = 0.99 allows and the gate counts on. The tracker keeps the disk’s force inside μ = 0.35 and applies (0.16, −0.03) N, the same with and without F2 (both ramps are projected onto the same point of the cone, so F2 changes nothing here).
  • Corner not released (disk 20 mm low, and the failing chain starts). The box tilts, but the riser contact runs at its friction limit (ratio 1.00, the environment’s μ = 0.2) and the box chatters between riser and floor, so the floor corner never opens past εon, or opens and falls back in the dwell when the disk’s force drops. The lift the plan gives to disk friction lands on the riser contact, which cannot carry it.
  • Late change (RH40, RH80): σ′ is reached in the last 0.2 s, fewer than the 20 dwell samples.

The check. With the plan’s forces computed with the disk’s friction capped at 0.35, the quasi-static residual of every failing break rises from about 0.015 to 0.22–0.27: no disk force inside the tracker’s cone holds the box through these breaks. Raising the tracker’s μ to 0.6 removes the slump at 30° and 40° but the corner still stays down (5 of 12 probe cases instead of 4); at 0.9 the disk slides on the soft contact (2 of 12). So the gate passes edits that this realizer cannot do, and the fix starts with giving the gate and the plan the friction the realizer can use.

F1 and F2. No consistent effect: on the GPU they cost a transfer(sv → t) start (the full equilibrium force at the first step overshoots the soft contact), on the CPU they gain a break(gv) start with re-planning. F1 cannot help the remaining failures: the corner does not follow the planned lift at all.

Re-planning before the fix realized 1–2 hard starts per variant (all with the disk 20 mm low) while failing the chain’s constraining edits: its compressed, faster phase II lifted the corner where the quasi-static schedule does not. That points at quasi-dynamic motion for these breaks, after the friction question.

What the gate passes and Λqs cannot realize is now one class: breaks that hold the box on a corner with more disk friction than the tracker uses.

The friction match (step 3d)

Decided Nov 8 (compendium, Decision 15): give the gate and the plan the disk–box friction the tracker uses (0.35) instead of the simulator’s 0.99, so the gate rejects the breaks Λqs cannot realize. Built as a scene parameter, manipulator_friction, and measured. It does not do what it was meant to, and it is left unset pending Aykut’s choice.

A defect found first: the gate’s sweep depended on the backend. The sweep lays a 5 × 5 grid along the free directions of the more-constrained formation. Those were the SVD’s null vectors, and when two directions are free (the pivot’s tilt and the disk along −xB) any rotation of them is an equally valid answer. The CPU and the GPU returned different rotations, so they probed different poses. At μ = 0.99 the feasible region hid this; at 0.35 it decided the verdict (the chain’s break(gv): refused on the CPU, margin 0.29 on the GPU). Fixed: a basis that depends on the subspace only. The backends now agree to 10⁻³, and checkpoint 1 at the simulator’s friction is unchanged.

With the decided grid, 0.35 refuses the chain’s own break, which the realizer does: a false refusal. With a 9-point grid it passes every start, including the six the realizer fails.

What the realizer does with friction-matched plans (GPU, open loop, 9-point sweep): the chain’s break(gv) 3 of 3 (step 3c: 2 of 3); the hard starts 0 of 6, with the same failure modes as before (slump during ramp-in with the disk 40 mm low, corner not released with it 20 mm low). The disk’s contact now stays inside its cone (ratio at most 0.37). The box’s floor and riser corners reach ratio 1.0 and slide, while the tracker’s correction adds up to twice the planned disk force. So the hard breaks fail dynamically, not for lack of a quasi-static transition, and no friction setting in the gate will reject them. Checkpoint 1 barely moves (chain 32.4% → 33.8% rejected with the sweep; demo 39.5% either way).

Choices (compendium, Step 3d): keep the friction cap unset; refine the sweep (9 points, or adaptive around the best pose) before the teacher search; for the hard breaks, a tracker that also keeps the environment contacts inside their cones, then a quasi-dynamic method.

Decided (Nov 8, Decision 16): the cap stays unset; the hard breaks wait for an environment-aware tracker, then a quasi-dynamic method; the sweep’s resolution was settled by measurement (next section).

The sweep’s resolution, measured (step 3d follow-up)

Every candidate edit that is an edge and passes Near, on the chain’s starts, the six hard break(gv) starts and the 205 demo nodes (1,797 candidates), was swept with several grids and compared with a dense reference (65 × 65 poses for two free directions, up to 4,913 poses), at the simulator’s friction and at the stress case μ = 0.35. The cube grid is the old one (5 or 9 values on all three directions; unused directions repeat poses). The dim grid puts about N poses on the directions that are actually free (8 × 8 for two directions at N = 64). Refinement adds a 3 × 3 × 3 grid at half spacing around the recognized pose of largest margin, twice.

grid poses CPU ms per candidate false refusals, μsim false refusals, μ = 0.35
cube 5 (until now) 125 13.7 0 1
cube 9 729 96.8 0 0
dim 125 125 13.8 0 2
dim 64 + 2 levels (now the default) 118 12.1 0 0
cube 5 + 2 levels 179 18.8 0 0
reference ≤ 4,913 767 (1,112 pass) (1,112 pass)

No grid ever passes an edit the reference refuses. At the default friction the resolution changes no verdict; at 0.35 the 5-point grid refuses the chain’s realizable break(gv) from 45°, and the adaptive grid does not. It costs what the old grid cost, a seventh of 9 points.

What the gate costs in a search. Per expanded node: one Λ call (0.16 s on CPU, open loop) and one gate evaluation (0.75 s on CPU; 0.33 s at χ₀, 0.42 s for the sweep). So the gate is about 82% of a node’s time; 9 points would make it 95% and a node 4× slower. The gate is 13× faster on CPU than on a GPU (the LPs’ iterations batch poorly). Two cheaper levers remain: sizing the sweep’s slots to a node’s candidates (32 slots for 8.6 candidates on average; 8 slots: 0.43 s per node), and evaluating the gate once per key (Decision 18): the demo nodes fall into about 8 keys, about 26 nodes each, which would bring the gate’s share to about 16% if a per-key gate cost about one node’s.

The compiled cost (step 3, MPPI)

The first Λ is MPPI, a sampling-based model-predictive controller running the MuJoCo simulation. The paper’s compiled cost is dimensionless: band, leave and overlap terms on the gaps, a control term, and a terminal barrier on the destination formation. It extends to Xiao and Ji’s edits through the point-contact view:

  • a transfer keeps the contained contact and moves the released end: for g → gv the other bottom corner lifts until the tilt passes 2θe; for the disk going round a box corner, the contact point slides past the vertex; a constraining transfer (sv → t) lays the edge flat;
  • a compound edge adds up its parts;
  • options: a margin term at the end, a dwell term, the two-phase subgoal (reach χtr, then change), and scaling the contact terms by εon instead of the box size.

The cost experiment (step 3, MPPI)

Setup. MPPI with 256 samples, 5 updates, controls held 3 steps and a 30-step look-ahead; executed records of 60 + 20 steps (0.8 s). Each edit of the chain is tried from its canonical start and from 2 perturbed starts, plus break(gv) from 30°, which needs the sweep. The variants:

  • A: the current MPPI cost (hand-set weights; a signed linear arrival term);
  • B: the paper’s compiled cost;
  • C: B plus the terminal margin and dwell terms;
  • D: C plus the transition subgoal;
  • E: D with the contact terms divided by εon instead of the box size, a smooth terminal cost, and a barrier of 100.

After a success, the next chain edit is attempted from where the record ended.

What the table says

  • The current cost overlaps. A reaches σ′ in 24 of 25 records but succeeds in 3: its linear arrival term pushes contacts up to 14 mm deep.
  • The paper’s cost is badly scaled for millimetre contacts. With the box size as the unit, a 1 mm gap costs (1/125)² ≈ 6·10⁻⁵, so the barrier decides almost everything. C and D are worse than B, probably because their margin term outweighs those tiny contact terms (the run does not isolate the cause). E, the same terms over εon, matches B’s success rate with half the overlap and the best follow-on rate.
  • The subgoal changed nothing on the chain: from every canonical start the gate passes at χ₀, so χtr = χ₀. Where it matters (break(gv) from 30°), D also fails.
  • What no variant does: the pivot (B tilts the box to about 30° and reaches σ′ but fails another clause; E never tilts it); break(gv) from 45° (B’s box topples onto the platform; E’s stays on its corner at 62–65° and never lifts); break(gv) from 30°. These are the edits where the disk must hold the box on a corner through an unstable motion, and a 0.3 s look-ahead with 1 N motors does not find that motion.
  • Transport succeeds with B (3 of 3) but not with E (0 of 3, still moving about 20 mm in the dwell window).

Checkpoint 2 with MPPI: does Λ realize the edits that pass the gate? Not reliably: 12 of 25 (48%) at best, and never the pivot or break(gv). (With Λqs: step 3c.) (Checkpoint 1, whether the gate prunes, is on the hold gate.)
