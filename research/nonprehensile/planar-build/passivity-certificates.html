Background: dissipation (passivity) certificates

A storage function, a supply and a dissipation inequality: what they prove, how a sliding block’s rest follows from its kinetic energy, why that beat per-class deceleration bounds, and the general avenue.

Before this page: interval arithmetic, zonotopes and certified funnels and composition. The terminology: the block is the manipulated object; a box is only an interval box (a product of intervals).

1. The idea in one inequality

Intuition. A system that cannot create energy, and loses some whenever it moves, must eventually stop. Friction is the classic loss: while anything slides, Coulomb friction turns kinetic energy into heat. If the forces that can add energy are weaker than the friction that removes it, the energy can only go down, and with it the motion.

The general statement [1], [2]. Take a state \(x\) in a set \(\mathcal X\), an input \(u\) and a disturbance \(w\). Choose

  • a storage function \(S(x)\ge 0\) (an “energy”);
  • an output \(y\) and a supply rate \(s(u,y,w)\) (the power the outside world can feed in);
  • a dissipation \(d(x)\ge 0\) (the power that is surely lost).

They form a dissipation certificate on \(\mathcal X\) if, along every motion,

\[ \dot S \;\le\; s(u,y,w) - d(x). \]

For contact, “every motion” means every admissible stick/slip contact class at every state of \(\mathcal X\), and the inequality is checked as a lower bound on \(s-d-\dot S\) with the same sound enclosures as every other certificate on this site: interval or zonotope arithmetic with outward rounding, where a refusal is always possible and a false pass is not.

What it gives. Each follows in one line from the inequality:

Use Condition Conclusion
Termination \(s\le0\), \(d\ge c\sqrt S\) off the target target reached by \(T\le 2\sqrt{S_0}/c\)
Reach set \(d\ge c\,\lvert\dot r\rvert\) for a distance \(r\) \(\Delta r\le S_0/c\)
Disturbance the measured residual’s power inside \(s\) a margin \(c\) that shrinks, no extra argument
Composition the exit’s level set \(\{S\le S_0\}\) inside the next entry one containment check at the junction

2. A worked toy: a block pushed against friction

A block of mass \(m=0.1\) kg slides on a floor with friction \(\mu=0.2\) at \(v_0=50\) mm/s, while something pushes it forward with \(F=0.1\) N.

  • Storage: \(E=\tfrac12 mv^2\), with \(E_0=\tfrac12(0.1)(0.05)^2=1.25\times10^{-4}\) J.
  • Supply: the push’s power, \(Fv\).
  • Dissipation: friction’s power, \(\mu m g\,v = 0.196\,v\) W.
  • So \(\dot E = Fv-\mu mgv = -c\,v\) with \(c=\mu mg-F=0.0962\) N \(>0\).

The push is not passive (it feeds energy in), but friction removes more. Integrating, \(c\,\Delta x\le E_0\):

\[ \Delta x \;\le\; \frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{1.25\times10^{-4}}{0.0962} = 1.30\ \text{mm}. \]

And since \(v=\sqrt{2E/m}\), \(\tfrac{d}{dt}\sqrt E=\dot E/(2\sqrt E)\le -c/\sqrt{2m}\), so the block stops within \(T\le\sqrt{2mE_0}/c=52\) ms.

Check it by hand. The deceleration is \(c/m=0.962\) m/s², so the exact stopping distance is \(v_0^2/(2\cdot0.962)=1.30\) mm and the exact time \(0.05/0.962=52\) ms. In one dimension the bounds are exact.

3. The real case: the rest after the impact

After the impact on the platform, the block slides forward at \(v_B\) (up to 48.4 mm/s on the hand-off) and the disk slips up the block’s face at \(v_s\) (up to 73 mm/s), while the hold force presses the block forward. Does it come to rest, and where?

  • Storage: \(E=\tfrac12(m_B+m_M)v_B^2+\tfrac12 m_M v_s^2\).
  • Supply: the hold force’s power and the measured simulator residual’s.
  • Dissipation: friction at the block’s two platform supports, \(\mu_t N_t v_B\), and at the disk, \(\mu_d N_m v_s\), with \(\mu_d\) anywhere in \([0.3,0.5]\).

The balance is \(\dot E\le -c_Bv_B-c_sv_s\), where \(c_B\) and \(c_s\) are the friction forces’ lower bounds less the hold force and the residual. Then \(\Delta x_B\le E_0/c_B\) and \(\Delta k\le E_0/c_s\), with \(k\) the disk’s place on the face.

With numbers. With the whole hand-off’s largest speeds, \[ E_0=\tfrac12(0.12)(0.0484)^2+\tfrac12(0.02)(0.073)^2=1.94\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}. \] The certified margins on the test units are at least \(c_B=0.0821\) N and \(c_s=0.0297\) N, which give \(\Delta x_B\le 2.36\) mm and \(\Delta k\le 6.53\) mm, and rest within \(\sqrt{2(0.12)E_0}/0.0297=0.23\) s. Per unit, with each unit’s own \(E_0\), the certificate’s bounds are at most 2.26 mm and 5.93 mm.

What makes it closed-form: position invariance. The bounds need the friction forces over the whole region the block can reach, and that region is what the bounds produce, a fixed point in general. On the flat platform it is not: the two supports keep the block from rotating, and the translational balance has no lever arm in it, so the total normal force at the supports and the disk’s forces depend only on the tilt, the friction coefficients and the hold force, never on where the block is. They are enclosed once, at a point, and hold everywhere.

The result on a test sample. Of 400 hand-off units, 300 had been refused by the per-class deceleration bounds; the energy certificate certifies all 400. The rest set it gives does not lie in the entry of the transport the composed chain uses, so that link of the chain stays measured; the transport’s wide entry is the one it can lie in.

4. Why it beat per-class deceleration bounds

The per-class route asks, in every contact class, that each slip decelerate at every state of a region: the hand-off’s positions grown by the stopping distance. It encloses the class’s accelerations over that grown position box with an interval Jacobian, and the box is wide enough that the enclosure loses the margin: the deceleration bound crosses zero although every actual motion decelerates. Splitting the box recovers some units, at a cost.

The energy route asks a different question. It never bounds a deceleration pointwise over the grown box; it adds up the power over the whole motion. And because the forces it needs are position-invariant, the grown box does not enter at all. The same physics, a different inequality, and no loss to the box.

5. Where this stands

The facts are classical: dry friction stops an oscillator in finite time [3], and mechanical systems with unilateral contact and friction have a full stability theory [4]. The closest certificate is Posa, Tobenkin and Tedrake’s [5], [6]: sums-of-squares Lyapunov functions for rigid bodies with impacts and friction, with energy as the first example. What differs here is the certificate’s form: an input and a measured disturbance as supply, a friction band, outward-rounded enclosures, and composition at the junctions of a plan.

6. The general avenue

Kinetic energy is monotone here because nothing but the hold’s small push feeds it. Where it is not (a pivot driven by the disk, an accelerating transport), the storage function and the output must be found together:

  1. Search, then verify soundly. Propose \(S\) and \(y\) per edge, from a parametric family or a learned proposer, and verify the inequality with the same enclosures; a bad proposal can fail, never pass. Joint storage–supply search with a sound verifier exists for smooth systems [7], [8] and in sums-of-squares form [9]; mode-dependent storage for hybrid systems goes back to [10].
  2. The step-integrated inequality. The simulator steps, and an impact is a jump, so the inequality to verify is \(S(x_{k+1})-S(x_k)\le\int (s-d)\,dt\) over one step, on the reachable set. Hybrid zonotopes represent an edge’s contact classes exactly [11], and a ReLU storage function has an exact hybrid-zonotope graph [12].
  3. An ablation. The joint search must be shown to matter: certify the same edges with a fixed supply and with the searched one.
  4. Composition by level sets along the contact-formation graph, in the tradition of compositional dissipativity [13].
