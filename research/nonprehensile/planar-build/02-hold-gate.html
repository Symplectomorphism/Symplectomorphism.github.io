2. Valid, transfers and a certified hold gate

Critical-path step 2: which edits the planner may propose, and a support test that proves its answer either way.

Code. Branch phase1/planar of Symplectomorphism/c-gnn, commit d810e32. New: marking/edits.py, physics/planar_hold.py, tests/test_planar_step2.py (17 tests); changed: Cand, the solver’s success check and the search’s acceptance, for planar scenes only. Angelo’s 3D path is untouched.

ImportantUpdated for Decision 9 (Nov 7, 2026): Xiao and Ji’s construction

Commit 9261ece replaces three parts of this step with Xiao and Ji’s construction (Decision 9):

  • Exclusions within a pair are derived: one contact per convex element pair, none alongside a contact it contains.
  • Transfers are Xiao–Ji containment only: 56 pairs, down from 92. The disk now goes round a box corner in two transfers.
  • Pass-through becomes the general compound rule: all changes relaxing or all constraining, kept only if every proper subset is invalid.

The gate tests statics of the less-constrained end of each edit. The landed box is {t}, so landing is the transfer sv → t. Superseded text below is marked.

What step 2 changes

Step 1 made the contact labels right. Step 2 makes the planner’s menu of edits right. Cand proposes an edit only when it passes the paper’s checks:

  • The edit is an edge of the marking graph: both the current formation and the new one are Valid. No two excluded contacts ({g, t}, {gv, s}, {g, sv}, {g, mz}), at most one contact per convex element pair, and no contact alongside one it contains (per body pair before Decision 9).
  • Near: a contact it adds is already within the gap gate.
  • H: the contacts that persist through the edit hold the box at the current pose.

The menu now has five kinds of edit:

Edit Changes Example
make(c), break(c) one contact make(s): push the box against the riser
transport nothing (moves the pose) slide the box along the floor
transfer(c → d) (new) one contact for one it contains, or the reverse (Xiao–Ji cases 2–3) g → gv: the box starts to tip onto its corner; sv → t: it lands
compound (new; “pass-through” before Decision 9) several single changes that cannot be separated the pivot: g → gv and s → sv at once

Transport is now offered only when the current formation is supported, as the paper defines it; before, it was always legal.

At the start ({g}, box flat on the floor), Cand no longer offers a second floor contact, t, sv or mz (all excluded with g), or the transfer g → gv. That last one is refused by H: during the swap only the box’s corner would still touch the floor, and one corner cannot hold a flat box.

Pass-through, made precise

WarningSuperseded by Decision 9 (Nov 7, 2026)

The two-transfer rule below is replaced by Xiao and Ji’s neighbor relation. An edge applies a set of single changes (make, break, transfer), all relaxing or all constraining. It is kept only if the result is valid and every nonempty proper subset of the changes gives an invalid formation; compound edges are capped at three changes.

On the step this gives the same compound edges, the pivot and its reverse (128 instances), and searching up to four changes finds no more. A make followed by a break in one pair is still not an edit, because it passes through a valid formation. The rest of this section is kept as the record of choice 1.

Decision 7 adopted pass-through edges for the pivot: from {mx, g, s} the box can only reach {mx, gv, sv} by changing two contacts at once, because both one-at-a-time intermediates, {gv, s} and {g, sv}, are excluded. Taken literally (“some sequence of steps through invalid markings”), the definition would also let any contact replace any other contact of its pair in one edit: make the new one first, which is invalid because the pair then has two, then break the old one. That would make transfer adjacency pointless.

Implemented: two single steps in different body pairs whose intermediates are invalid in both orders. Both must then be transfers with crossed exclusions. On the step scene the only pass-throughs are the pivot and its reverse. A brute-force search over all 12,393 markings with at most one contact per pair finds exactly the same 128 pass-through edges.

NoteSettled (Nov 7, 2026): the two-transfer rule

Let w → x be a transfer in one body pair and y → z a transfer in another, with x excluded alongside y and w excluded alongside z (while w with y, and x with z, are allowed). Then pass(w → x, y → z) replaces w and y by x and z in one edit. It is an edge when both ends are valid markings. It is the only edit that changes more than one body pair:

  • A make followed by a break in the same pair is not an edit. The in-between state has two contacts in one pair, which is not a marking, and allowing it would let any contact replace any other, skipping transfer adjacency.
  • A move that needs three or more inseparable steps is not an edge. A scene that needs one gets it as a new generator by its own decision.
  • A transfer paired with a make or a break never qualifies: two single edits whose in-between states are both invalid are always two such transfers.

The literal “any sequence through invalid markings” rule, restricted to one contact per pair, finds the same edges on this scene but needs a search instead of a closed form, so it was declined.

H: persistent contacts, decided exactly

NoteSince Decision 9: statics of the less-constrained end

Every edge now links a formation to one it contains, so H tests the less-constrained end: the new formation for a relaxing edit, the current one for a constraining edit. This is the transition rule of multi-contact planning. Hauser et al. (2008): the robot “can support itself only with forces applied at the contact in [σ]”, since “the applied force is zero at the instant of time when the contact is made or broken”. Hauser, Bretl and Latombe (2005) and Escande et al. (2013) state the same rule.

The test suite checks that the less-constrained end equals the persisting contacts below, for make, break, both transfer directions and the pivot. The old contact’s normal (choice 2) now follows from containment: the feature that defines the normal is the one that stays.

H asks whether the contacts that persist through the edit hold the box (Decision 7). For a make or a break that is the paper’s rule (the contacts kept). For a transfer, only the part of the old contact set that the new one still covers persists: for g → gv, the box’s corner.

NoteSettled (Nov 7, 2026): the old contact’s normal

The forces on those persisting points use the old contact’s normal (and friction, which is the same for every contact of a body pair). H is checked at the current pose, where the old marking is realized and the new one is not, so the old contact’s geometry is what carries the load. On the demo trajectory (1,155 transfer edits), the two normals differ by at most 1.77° where the new contact is about to be realized (within the on-set angle 1.83°). They differ by up to 179° only where the new contact is far from realized, and there its normal is not a real force. Using the new normal changes none of the 1,155 decisions.

Background tutorial: Deciding support exactly derives this test step by step, with a worked example, proofs of the witness, certificate and margin, and exercises. The hold test as a linear program poses the same question as LPs that also serve the 3D build. Since Nov 7 that backbone is the default gate (below); the exact test described next is its cross-check.

The test. In the plane, every contact force is a nonnegative mix of the two edges of its friction cone. So “the contacts hold the box” means that the wrench cancelling gravity (x force, z force, moment) lies in a cone in 3D generated by at most 16 vectors. That is decided exactly, with proof either way:

  • Supported: a nonnegative combination of at most three generators that reproduces the wrench, by Carathéodory’s theorem. This is the witness.
  • Not supported: a plane through the origin with every generator on one side and the gravity wrench on the other, by Farkas’s lemma. This is the certificate. Every face of the cone is spanned by two generators, so the candidate planes are few.
  • Margin: the signed distance from the gravity wrench to the cone’s boundary, with forces in units of mg and moments in mg·a/2. Inside the cone it is the largest disturbance the contacts absorb in every direction; outside, minus the distance to the cone. A flat box on the floor: 0.196, which is μ/√(1+μ²) for μ = 0.2. With the disk in the formation the cone often fills all of 3D, because the disk’s force is unbounded. A finite margin there needs the manipulator’s force limits. Both points are settled since Nov 7: see the gate.

The same function takes a lower friction coefficient and mass and center-of-mass vertices, and returns the worst-case margin (the robust margin of Decision 6).

The pivot needs the disk low

The chain’s canonical pose for σ3 puts the disk at the middle of −xB. There, H refuses break(gv): pushing at mid-height, the disk cannot keep the box on the step corner without the floor. It must push low (toward the floor), and, since its motors give at most 1 N per axis, the box must already be tipped about 40° or more: at 30° holding the box on the corner takes more force than the motors give, wherever the disk pushes. Between about 65° and 70° any disk height works; past about 72° the box falls onto the platform. A search gets there by transport, tilting the box further and moving the disk down the face within the formation. A float64 LP with the same motor limits confirms every answer, so this is a fact about the physics (and the motors), not about the test. The same holds for the landing at σ4, now transfer(sv → t).

With that, Cand offers every step of the chain from a pose in its formation: make(mx), make(s), the pivot (compound), break(gv), transfer(sv → t), break(mx), make(mz), then transport. Before Decision 9 the landing was make(t) and the last edit break(sv); the landed box is now {t}. Theorem relocate holds: with σ = {mx, s}, break(mx) is never offered.

Which hold solver

The plan was to build H on MPAX, Angelo’s LP solver, then benchmark and switch if a solver won clearly. One did. The benchmark covers 1,000 random cases and 416 cases within 2 mm of the support boundary. Every answer is checked against a float64 LP (SciPy’s HiGHS) on an independently coded formulation.

Backend Random (1,000) Near the boundary (416) ms per 256, CPU / GPU
Old 6-row MPAX LP 998 220 (194 false rejections) 3,879 / 313
3-row MPAX LP 999 222 (192 false rejections) 63 / 110
Exact, certified 1,000 393 on CPU, 389 on GPU (21–26 false rejections, 1–2 false acceptances) 4.3 / 2.0

Every error of the exact test lies within 0.33 mm (of disk height) of the boundary on CPU, and within 0.67 mm on GPU, where float32 geometry decides. Everywhere 0.7 mm or more away it is right, on both. One GPU pitfall was caught on the way: JAX runs float32 matrix products on the GPU in reduced precision (TF32), which broke the test’s check of supporting planes; it now forms every dot product elementwise. The step-1 contact layer is not affected: on 5,721 poses its gaps agree between CPU and GPU to 2.7 µm, and its on-sets exactly. Both LPs reject supported formations across the whole 2 mm band: their “not supported” often meant “ran out of iterations”. The exact test became the hold gate for planar scenes; the MPAX version stays in the code for comparison. Since Nov 7 the gate is the LP backbone with actuator limits, which the exact test cannot take (the gate).

The gate since Nov 7: LP, motor limits, ℓ₁ margin

NoteSettled (Nov 7, 2026): step-2 choices 3 and 4

What the margin is. At the current pose, with the contacts that persist through the edit: how large a disturbance (a push or twist, in units of mg and mg·a/2) the contacts can absorb before they can no longer hold the box. Negative: how far from holding. H uses its sign; Decision 6 uses its value (to rank formations, as the controller’s terminal sets, and checkpoint 5 tests whether it predicts the solver’s success).

Shape: set inclusion. The margin is the largest r such that the gravity-cancelling wrench plus r·D is still supported, for a disturbance polytope D (D is absorbed iff r ≥ 1). By default D is the ℓ₁ unit ball (pushes and twists along the axes), which makes the margin the signed ℓ₁ distance to the boundary: 0.2 = μ on the flat box. A scene can pass its own D. The exact test’s Euclidean margin is always within a factor √3 of it (√6 in 3D) and stays as a cross-check.

The disk’s force: the simulator’s motor limits. The disk is held by two slide motors, ±1 N each (step.xml). Held still, the motors must supply its force on the box plus its own weight (0.20 N), so its force on the box is bounded: two rows per motor in the LP, read from the MuJoCo model. The box weighs 0.98 N, so the disk alone cannot hold it up, and margins with the disk are finite (no more cap). OpenArm’s limits replace these when it arrives.

The gate: the LP backbone. With force limits the set of supportable wrenches is a polytope, not a cone, and the exact test (which needs a cone) cannot decide it. So PlanarHold and Cand now default to method="lp" with the motor limits; method="exact" stays as the cone-only cross-check and MPAX only for comparison.

Checked against a float64 LP (SciPy’s HiGHS) that applies the same motor limits, coded independently (the limits hard-coded from step.xml, not read from the model). Near the boundary means 16 sweeps of disk height on the pivot, bisected to the boundary, with 13 cases within 2 mm each.

Gate (float32) Random (1,000) Near the boundary ms per 256, CPU / GPU
LP + motor limits (default) 1,000 199 / 202 of 208 (CPU / GPU): no false rejection, no “uncertain” 14.7 / 3.4
LP, no limits (vs HiGHS without limits) 1,000 386 / 383 of 416 8.5 / 2.5
Exact test, no limits (vs HiGHS without limits) 1,000 390 / 389 of 416 5.0 / 1.6

Every miss of the default gate (9 on CPU, 6 on GPU) is the bisected boundary pose itself, within 10−10 m of disk height: the gate’s margin there is 0 (at most 2·10−7), and HiGHS in float64 says “just not”. At every offset of 0.33 mm or more, the two agree. With the limits, no margin reaches the cap (0 of 568 supported cases); without them, about 190 of 636 did.

εpen and ties

Step 1 left a known issue: with εpen = 10 mm, two contacts of one pair can both count as touching under deep penetration. Step 2 was to set εpen from the penetration measured in the solver’s rollouts. The measurement says not yet:

  • In 24 full MPPI rollouts of the chain’s edits, the pushing edits penetrate up to 9.9 mm (95th percentiles 4–8 mm); the passive breaks stay under 1.6 mm.
  • The cause is MPPI’s cost, whose arrival term rewards penetration (the shove on walkthrough page 7). A tighter εpen now would fail every pushing rollout.
  • So εpen moves to step 3, after the cost.

Done now: a pose with a tie can neither count as a solver success nor become a search node. A stress test in the suite tracks ties on 4,000 jittered demo poses: 357 when step 2 was built, 475 since Decision 9. 474 of the 475 have a contact deeper than εon, and in 136 of them sv loses to t, which contains it. The test’s budget is 500.

One more finding for step 3: all 24 rollouts fail the solver’s success check, the passive breaks included, and not because of ties.

Checks

  • The marking graph, exhaustively (11,959 valid markings; since Decision 9, 37,203 of the 59,049 markings with at most one contact per convex element pair):
    • every edge lands on a valid formation;
    • every edge has its reverse;
    • the degree bound holds;
    • the graph is connected, and its distance is between a quarter of the Hamming distance and the Hamming distance.
    These are the claims of the restated Lemma markgraph-struct in the paper.
  • Supported against the float64 LP: 250 random cases and the pivot scan, all certified, all equal.
  • The cone test against HiGHS on 400 random cones in 3D, plus the degenerate cases (wedge, ray, empty, all of 3D).
  • The full suite, 48 tests, passes on Python 3.14 (CPU and GPU) and 3.12. Since Decision 9: 68 tests on Python 3.14, CPU and GPU.
