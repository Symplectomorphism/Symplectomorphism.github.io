Background: deciding support exactly

How the planar hold test answers whether contacts can hold the box, proves its answer either way, and measures by how much.

What this explains. Step 2’s hold gate H asks, before every edit, whether the contacts that persist through it can hold the box against gravity (step 2). This page derives the test that answers that question without force limits; since Nov 7 the gate itself is the LP form, which also takes the disk’s motor limits, and this test is its cross-check. It turns the question into a membership problem for a cone in three dimensions, then decides it by enumeration: a witness if the answer is yes, a certificate if it is no, and a margin either way. You need Coulomb friction and a little linear algebra. The code is cone_test in cmg/src/cmg/physics/planar_hold.py (branch phase1/planar, commit 1986c73).

1. The question

A rigid box rests in the x–z plane on some contacts. Each contact pushes on the box with a force that Coulomb friction confines to a cone. The box is supported if some choice of admissible contact forces cancels gravity exactly: the net force and the net moment. That is statics only: no dynamics and no time.

2. From forces to one cone

Friction cones have two edges. At a contact with inward normal \(n\), tangent \(t\) and friction coefficient \(\mu\), the admissible forces are those with \(|f\cdot t| \le \mu\, f\cdot n\). In the plane that set is spanned by its two edge rays: \[ f = \lambda_+\,(n+\mu t) + \lambda_-\,(n-\mu t), \qquad \lambda_\pm \ge 0 . \]

A segment contact acts through its endpoints. A contact set can be a segment \([p_0,p_1]\) (an edge lying on the floor) with the same \(n\) and \(\mu\) along it. A force \(f\) applied at \(p = s\,p_0 + (1-s)\,p_1\) has the same force and moment as \(s f\) at \(p_0\) plus \((1-s) f\) at \(p_1\), because the moment \((p-c)\times f\) is linear in \(p\). So two points per contact suffice. A point contact is the case \(p_0 = p_1\).

Wrenches. Write each ray \(r\) at each endpoint \(p\) as a wrench: force and moment about the center of mass \(c\), made dimensionless, \[ w = \Big(r_x,\; r_z,\; \tfrac{(p-c)\times r}{\ell}\Big) \in \R^3, \qquad (p-c)\times r = (p-c)_z\, r_x - (p-c)_x\, r_z , \] with forces in units of the weight \(mg\) and moments in units of \(mg\,\ell\), where \(\ell = a/2\) is the box’s half-size. Gravity applies the wrench \((0,-1,0)\) about the center of mass, so the contacts must supply \[ \text{need} = (0,\, 1,\, 0). \]

The question becomes geometry. Let \(w_1,\dots,w_k\) be all the ray wrenches: at most 4 body pairs × 2 endpoints × 2 rays = 16. Then \[ \boxed{\ \text{supported} \iff \text{need} \in C = \Big\{\textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i w_i : \lambda_i \ge 0\Big\}\ } \] where \(C\) is the convex cone the generators span. Everything below is about deciding this membership, with proof, for a cone in \(\R^3\) with at most 16 generators.

Running example. A square box flat on the floor, \(\mu = 0.2\), touching at its two bottom corners A (left) and B (right). In units of \(\ell\), the corners sit at \((\mp1,-1)\) from the center of mass. The four generators are:

Ray Force \((f_x, f_z)\) Moment / ℓ Wrench \(w\)
A₊ (+0.2, 1) +0.8 (0.2, 1, 0.8)
A₋ (−0.2, 1) +1.2 (−0.2, 1, 1.2)
B₊ (+0.2, 1) −1.2 (0.2, 1, −1.2)
B₋ (−0.2, 1) −0.8 (−0.2, 1, −0.8)

3. Seeing the cone: the slice fz = 1

A cone in \(\R^3\) is hard to draw, but when every generator pushes upward (\(f_z > 0\)) it is fixed by where its rays cross the plane \(f_z = 1\). Each generator becomes a point \((f_x/f_z,\ \tau/f_z)\), the cone becomes the convex polygon of those points, and need becomes the origin. Supported then means the origin lies in the polygon.

The slice is only a picture. A disk pushing sideways can have \(f_z \le 0\), and the margin below is measured in \(\R^3\), not in the slice. The test itself always works in \(\R^3\).

4. “Yes”, with a witness: Carathéodory

Carathéodory’s theorem (conic form). If \(x \in \operatorname{cone}\{w_1,\dots,w_k\} \subset \R^d\), then \(x\) is a nonnegative combination of at most \(d\) linearly independent generators.

Why. Suppose \(x = \sum_i \lambda_i w_i\) with \(\lambda > 0\) on generators that are linearly dependent, so \(\sum_i \alpha_i w_i = 0\) for some \(\alpha \ne 0\), which we may take to have a positive entry. Replace \(\lambda\) by \(\lambda - s\alpha\) and increase \(s\) until the first coefficient hits zero. The sum still equals \(x\), the coefficients stay nonnegative, and one generator has dropped out. Repeat until the generators are independent; there are at most \(d\) of them.

Algorithm. Here \(d = 3\), so the test solves every subset of 1, 2 and 3 generators, \(16 + 120 + 560 = 696\) tiny systems. Single generators use a projection, pairs a 2×2 system, triples Cramer’s rule. A subset whose coefficients are \(\ge 0\) and that reproduces need is a witness: actual contact forces that hold the box.

Example. The flat box needs only two generators: \(\tfrac12 w_{A_+} + \tfrac12 w_{B_-} = (0, 1, 0)\). Half the weight goes through each corner, both forces leaning slightly inward (panel a). In the slice, the origin is the midpoint of A₊ and B₋ (panel c).

5. “No”, with a certificate: Farkas

Farkas’s lemma (cone form). \(x \notin \operatorname{cone}\{w_i\}\) if and only if some \(y\) has \(y\cdot w_i \ge 0\) for every \(i\) and \(y \cdot x < 0\).

Geometrically, \(y\) is the normal of a plane through the origin with every generator on one side and need strictly on the other. Such a \(y\) is a certificate: anyone can check the \(k+1\) inequalities.

Which \(y\) to try. A full-dimensional polyhedral cone is the intersection of the half-spaces of its facets, and each facet is a plane containing at least two independent generators. If need is outside the cone, it violates one of those half-spaces. So the normals \(\pm\, w_i \times w_j\) of the planes through two generators contain a certificate. Cones that are flat (a wedge, like one corner’s) or a single ray need a few more candidates:

  • \(\pm w_i\);
  • the part of need perpendicular to \(w_i\), negated;
  • normals of planes containing a single generator;
  • \(-\text{need}\).

The test tries all of them, 385 for 16 generators, and keeps those that have every generator on their side.

Example. On corner A alone the generators are \(w_{A_\pm}\), a flat cone. The plane through both has normal \(y = (1,-1,1)/\sqrt3\). It gives \(y\cdot w_{A_\pm} = 0\) and \(y\cdot \text{need} = -0.577 < 0\): certified unsupported (panel d). Physically, every force at A has a moment about the center of mass, unless it points straight at the center of mass, which is outside A’s friction cone. Gravity has no moment to cancel it.

6. How much: the margin is a signed distance

A yes/no answer says nothing about robustness. The test also returns \[ m \;=\; \min_{y \text{ supporting},\ \|y\| = 1}\; y\cdot \text{need}, \] the minimum over every candidate \(y\) that has all generators on its side.

Claim. \(m\) is the signed Euclidean distance from need to the boundary of \(C\). Inside \(C\) it is the distance to the boundary; outside it is minus the distance to \(C\). It is \(0\) when need lies on a flat cone.

Inside. \(C\) is the intersection of its facet half-spaces \(\{x : y_f\cdot x \ge 0\}\). A ball of radius \(\rho\) around need fits inside \(C\) exactly when it fits inside every half-space, that is, when \(\rho \le y_f\cdot\text{need}\) for every facet. So the distance to the boundary is \(\min_f y_f\cdot\text{need}\). Any other supporting plane bounds a half-space that contains \(C\), so it can only give a larger value. The minimum over all candidates is therefore the facet minimum.

Outside. The nearest point of \(C\) to need lies inside a two-dimensional face (a facet, or the whole of a flat cone), on a generator’s ray, or at the apex. The unit vector from need toward it is then that face’s normal, the negated perpendicular part of need with respect to that generator, or \(-\text{need}\). All three are candidates, and each supports \(C\). By duality, \(\operatorname{dist}(x, C) = \max\{-y\cdot x : y \text{ supporting},\ \|y\|\le1\}\). So the minimum of \(y\cdot\text{need}\) is minus that distance.

Checked. On random cones, the margin agrees with minus the distance computed by non-negative least squares (to 3·10⁻⁶) outside the cone. Inside, it agrees with the shortest exit along hundreds of directions, each found by a float64 LP (to 4·10⁻⁷). This is a test in the suite (test_cone_margin_is_signed_distance).

Reading it. \(m\) is the largest disturbance wrench (force in \(mg\), moment in \(mg\ell\)) that the contacts absorb in every direction. The flat box has four facets:

Facet Through Normal y y · need Meaning
Sliding (friction toward +x) A₊, B₊ (−0.981, 0.196, 0) 0.196 friction gives out
Sliding (friction toward −x) A₋, B₋ (0.981, 0.196, 0) 0.196 friction gives out
Tipping about A A₊, A₋ (−1, 1, −1)/√3 0.577 B lifts off
Tipping about B B₊, B₋ (1, 1, 1)/√3 0.577 A lifts off

So \(m = \mu/\sqrt{1+\mu^2} = 0.196\), and sliding binds first. Two points help when reading this number:

  • A purely sideways push of up to \(\mu\,mg = 0.2\,mg\) is absorbed (the test agrees: 0.19 holds, 0.21 does not). The worst direction also lifts the box a little, which unloads friction, hence 0.196.
  • The tipping value does not depend on \(\mu\). The plane through B’s two rays contains every wrench a force at B can produce, so it depends only on where B is. With \(\mu\) above \(1/\sqrt2\), tipping binds instead: \(m = \min(\mu/\sqrt{1+\mu^2},\ 1/\sqrt3)\).

For corner A alone, \(m = -0.636\): need lies 0.636 from the cone. The certificate of §5 had \(y\cdot\text{need} = -0.577\). Any certificate proves “no”, but only the nearest one measures the gap.

Two caveats.

  • The distance mixes forces and moments, so it depends on the choice of \(\ell\). Changing \(\ell\) rescales the moment axis and changes \(m\), not the yes/no answer.
  • With the disk in the formation, the disk’s force has no upper limit, so \(C\) often fills all of \(\R^3\). There is then no boundary to be near, and the test reports a cap (\(10^3\)). A finite margin there needs the manipulator’s force limits (Decision 6, open).

7. Robustness comes cheaply

  • Friction. A smaller \(\mu\) narrows every friction cone, so it shrinks \(C\). For an interval of \(\mu\), the worst case is its lower end.
  • Mass and center of mass enter only through need. Need is linear in \((m,\ m c)\), and the set of \((m,\ m c)\) over a box of masses and centers is a polytope whose corners come from the box’s corners. Membership in a convex cone holds over a polytope iff it holds at the corners. The margin is a minimum of linear functions, so its worst case is also at a corner. The robust margin is the minimum over those few needs.

8. In the code

PlanarHold builds the problem and cone_test solves it:

  1. Which contacts. For an edit from σ to σ′, a body pair contributes only if it touches in both. Its contact set is the overlap of the two, with the old contact’s normal and \(\mu\). For a make or a break, that is the kept contact itself (step 2).
  2. Generators from the overlap’s endpoints (§2), padded to 16 with inactive slots.
  3. Need for each mass and center-of-mass corner (§7).
  4. cone_test for each need: witness (§4), certificates (§5), margin (§6).
  5. The gate. At most one contact per pair, a witness for every need, and margin \(\ge\) margin_min (default 0).
import jax.numpy as jnp
from cmg.physics.planar_hold import cone_test

mu = 0.2
corners = jnp.array([[s * mu, 1.0, -s * mu - ax] for ax in (-1.0, 1.0) for s in (1, -1)])
need = jnp.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0])
cone_test(corners, jnp.ones(4, bool), need)       # feasible, margin 0.196
cone_test(corners[:2], jnp.ones(2, bool), need)   # certified_infeasible, margin -0.636

Every size is fixed (696 subsets and 385 candidate planes for 16 generators), so the test is branch-free and runs under jit and vmap: 4.3 ms per 256 cases on CPU and 2.0 ms on GPU.

Numerics. The arithmetic is float32 with a relative tolerance of 10⁻⁵. Within tolerance of the boundary, neither a witness nor a certificate may pass. The gate then answers “not supported” and flags the case as uncertain. In the benchmark every error lay within 0.7 mm (of disk height) of the support boundary (step 2). Every dot product is formed elementwise: on the GPU, JAX runs float32 matrix products in reduced precision (TF32), which broke the supporting-plane checks.

9. Why not an LP solver, and why only in the plane

The same question is a linear program: find \(\lambda \ge 0\) with \(\sum_i \lambda_i w_i = \text{need}\). An iterative first-order solver such as MPAX approaches the answer without a proof of infeasibility. When it stops early, “not optimal” must be read as “not supported”. Near the boundary it did exactly that for about half of 416 supported cases, while the exact test was 14 to 900 times faster (benchmark).

The enumeration is a planar luxury. A spatial wrench has 6 components, so Carathéodory needs subsets of up to 6 generators. With 4 contacts × 4 points × 8 rays of a pyramidal friction cone (128 generators), that is \(\binom{128}{6} \approx 5.4\times10^{9}\) subsets. In 3D the test needs a float64 LP with an infeasibility certificate instead: see the hold test as a linear program.

Check yourself

Tip1. What is the flat box’s margin with μ = 0.5, and what binds?

\(0.5/\sqrt{1.25} = 0.447 < 1/\sqrt3 = 0.577\): sliding still binds. cone_test returns 0.4472.

Tip2. Can a larger μ let corner A alone hold the flat box?

No. Every wrench a force at A can produce lies in one plane, \(\tau/\ell = f_z - f_x\) (arm \((-1,-1)\)), whatever \(\mu\) is. Need \(= (0,1,0)\) gives \(1 \ne 0\), so it is off that plane. In general, a single point contact holds a body only if the vertical through the center of mass passes through that point and the vertical direction lies in its friction cone.

Tip3. Why does the test try planes through two generators, not three?

A plane through the origin in \(\R^3\) is fixed by two independent directions. Every facet of a 3D cone is such a plane, and it contains at least two generators. Three generators on one plane add nothing new.
